# Weather & Sky Phenomena of Anka

A note on the basics, before the entries: Anka is flat, doesn’t rotate, has no axial tilt, and orbits nothing — so there are no seasons, no moon, and no stars. “Day” and “night” as other worlds understand them don’t really apply either; locals just say “day,” with the Scar’s slow rotation overhead dividing it into a bright stretch and a dark one. Barring local conditions, the climate everywhere is more or less temperate as a baseline — there’s no underlying cycle to push it warmer or colder from one stretch of spins to the next, so “temperate” is simply the default a traveler can expect to return to once any local weather passes.

What passes for “wind” on Anka mostly comes from two sources: the Scar’s rotation itself drives a steady high-altitude breeze, and the transition between bright and dark produces a gentler ground-level breeze about an hour after it happens. Beyond that, anything stronger is usually down to local causes — geography, a nearby Titan remnant, or something with wings big enough to make its own weather.

Entries marked *(new)* round out the existing notes to about twenty; all dice and roll instructions from the source material have been reworded into plain description of what travelers can expect.

-----

## Ash Veil *(new)*

In regions near old battlefields or active veins of black glass, a fine grey ash sometimes drifts down — flecks of blackglass, worn to dust by wind and time, lifting free of the cracks and settling over everything in a pale film. Harmless in small amounts, but anyone caught in a heavy fall without something to cover their mouth and nose risks real lung trouble, the same as breathing in any fine mineral dust for too long. Travelers who know the signs carry a cloth for exactly this reason.

## Baking Sun

On certain stretches, during the bright phase, the light seems to take a personal interest in whoever’s walking through it. There’s no shade, no breeze, and within an hour clothes are soaked through with sweat. Pushing on through it for hours steadily wears a person down — by the end of a long stretch under a baking sun, even fit travelers are dragging their feet — and anyone who doesn’t drink at least a little extra water every hour starts to feel it badly.

## Bloodmist *(new)*

A red-tinted fog that occasionally rolls out from an active geyser field, carrying faint traces of Titan blood in the steam itself. Breathing it for any length of time doesn’t do any lasting harm, but it leaves people on edge — short-tempered, quick to take offense, prone to picking fights over nothing. Towns near a working geyser know a bloodmist day when they see one, and the smarter taverns simply close early until it passes.

## Crackling Calm *(new)*

A dead, total stillness — no breeze, no birdsong, just a faint smell of ozone slowly building in the air, sometimes strong enough to make hair stand on end. It’s the surest warning a lightning storm is coming, often the only warning there is, since the storm itself can arrive without a single visible cloud. Anyone who’s spent time in storm country learns to treat the calm itself as the signal to find cover, rather than waiting for the first crack of lightning to convince them.

## Driftfrost *(new)*

For a world with no winter, certain low-lying spots — the underside of Causeway arches, swamp hollows, the bottoms of old craters — sometimes wake up coated in frost that has no business being there. It never spreads beyond the hollow itself, and it’s usually gone again within an hour of the next bright phase. Greybeards blame it on cold air leaking through some thin spot between Anka and Akna. Whatever the cause, anyone who beds down in a driftfrost hollow overnight wakes up stiff, chilled to the bone, and unhappy about it.

## Emberfall *(new)*

Near lava country, glowing cinders sometimes drift down out of the sky like a hot, lazy snowfall — strange and almost pretty, as long as nobody lets one sit too long on cloth or bare skin. A light emberfall is mostly a curiosity; a heavy one is a genuine fire risk, especially for thatch roofs and dry goods left uncovered. In towns that get it regularly, brushing the embers off a companion’s shoulders before they scorch through is considered basic good manners.

## Fogglow *(new)*

On overcast nights, the Spire’s permanent glow diffuses through low cloud or fog and casts a faint directional brightness across the sky — useful for travelers trying to keep their bearings toward spireward when the Spire itself isn’t visible. Near large expanses of glass, though, that same glow can bounce and scatter in confusing ways, throwing false impressions of direction or distance that have led more than one overconfident traveler astray on a foggy night.

## Groundshake *(new)*

The old stories say that when the buried, hungry Titan stirs in its sleep — still trying, after everything, to rejoin its severed halves — the ground itself answers. A groundshake usually announces itself with a low groan felt through the soles of the feet before any visible shaking starts, and most are over within moments: a brief, frightening shudder that rattles shelves and spills water, rarely more. The exceptions are dangerous, though — a poorly built structure can come down in even a modest shake, and near unstable terrain (the Giant’s Graveyard is a notorious example) a groundshake can send a great deal of loose rock moving all at once.

## Hollow Wind *(new)*

Wind moving through cracks and canyons of black glass produces a faint, breathy sound that — if you listen long enough — starts to sound almost like whispered words, in no language anyone recognizes. It’s entirely harmless, just air moving through glass passages of different shapes and sizes, but it unsettles people all the same. Some greybeards swear they can read omens in the patterns of a hollow wind; just as many say that’s nonsense, and the wind doesn’t mean anything at all.

## Lightning Storm

An ozone smell builds, and then the lightning starts — arcing thunderously between points with no visible cloud cover to explain it, sometimes seeming to leap directly out of the ground itself. Anyone carrying a noticeable amount of metal, or caught on high ground, draws far more of it, and far worse strikes, than someone tucked low and metal-free. Armor offers no protection at all against a direct hit — only solid cover does, and getting to it quickly enough matters. Fortunately, even the worst of these storms tends to blow itself out within several minutes.

## Needle Storm

Found in tundra, ice caps, ice forests, mountains, and similar cold terrain — anywhere ice and heat meet closely enough that the rising steam freezes instantly into tiny shards of ice, which a strong wind then turns into a storm of “ice needles.” Anyone who tries to push on through one takes a steady battering of small, stinging cuts, and the poor visibility makes it alarmingly easy for even an experienced guide to lead the group the wrong way entirely, losing all the ground they’d covered.

Making camp instead is safer, but not free — between the time it takes and the conditions, a party that stops to shelter loses just as much ground as one that gets lost pushing through. The difference is that a properly sheltered camp keeps everyone safe and reasonably comfortable, while a hastily thrown-together one leaves everybody cold, miserable, and worse off come the next stretch.

## Pressure Hush *(new)*

A few minutes before the Scar’s light flips from bright to dark (or back again), the world seems to hold its breath. Animals fall silent, insects stop, and the air itself takes on a strange, pressing quality that’s hard to describe but impossible to miss once you’ve felt it. Experienced travelers treat the hush as their cue — time to find shelter, finish a task, or simply brace for the change that’s about to arrive with very little further warning.

## Scar’s Breath *(new)*

A gentle, predictable breeze that rises roughly an hour after every light-to-dark or dark-to-light transition, as the air settles into its new state. On its own it’s barely worth mentioning — sailors of skybarges and overland travelers alike use it as a rough clock and compass, since it tends to blow in a consistent direction relative to the Scar’s rotation. The only real hazard is incidental: a strong Scar’s Breath moving through a stand of Bellow Reed can produce an unsettling amount of noise.

## Stony Weather

Rocks fall out of the sky — there’s no gentler way to put it, and the old stories blame it on the glutton Titan’s scattered teeth. A real stony usually announces itself first: a pebble here, a pebble there, in the moments before the real storm arrives. This has made “faking” a stony with a handful of thrown rocks a time-honored prank among Anka’s youth — one that eventually, reliably, gets every prankster caught in a real one and properly frightened.

The storm itself rarely lasts more than a few moments, but those moments can be brutal — anyone caught in the open without cover can be seriously hurt, or worse, by the sheer weight of falling rock. Crops and grazing animals left exposed suffer the most, though even simple wooden roofing is sturdy enough to turn the rocks aside, which is more protection than most armor manages.

## Swelter *(new)*

Distinct from the dry misery of a baking sun, a sweltering day is a swamp or bog phenomenon — thick, wet heat with no breeze to relieve it. Anyone in heavy armor needs extra water just to keep up, or risks becoming dangerously thirsty, and the swamp itself seems to take on an even worse smell than usual. Long-time swamp dwellers have a saying that a sweltering day is the swamp’s way of telling you something else is about to go wrong — which is, admittedly, easy to believe when you’re standing in one.

## Tarnishfall *(new)*

A light, unremarkable-looking rain that falls in regions near old battlefields or heavy glass veins — except that it carries enough dissolved magefire dust to visibly tarnish exposed metal within a few hours, turning bright copper or silver black or sickly green well ahead of its time. It’s the same slow process that blackens every coin in circulation, just concentrated and sped up. Traders and smiths working in these regions keep their stock covered, and any merchant caught trying to sell freshly tarnished goods right after a tarnishfall has a hard time convincing buyers the goods weren’t already old.

## Thick Fog

A dense fog that can cut visibility down to just a few paces — bad news for spotting trouble ahead, but just as bad for anything trying to spot you. Thick fog tends to favor whoever’s doing the ambushing, which is exactly why it makes everyone else nervous.

## Thunderstorm

A torrential downpour with rolling, near-constant thunder layered on top of it — loud enough that shouted warnings can go completely unheard until whatever’s being warned about is already on top of you. Everything that makes a torrential rain miserable applies here too, with the added problem of not being able to hear it coming.

## Torrential Rain

A heavy, sustained downpour that swells streams, saturates the ground, and turns easy travel into a slog. Wading through flooded ground in these conditions can take four times as long as it normally would — and that’s assuming the ground underfoot hasn’t shifted into something more dangerous in the meantime.

## Whiteout Glare *(new)*

The flip side of the Glass Sea’s frigid, exposed nights: during the bright phase, sunlight reflecting off the black glass becomes genuinely blinding without eye protection — bright enough to make the whole expanse feel like midday even at the edges of the Scar’s reach. The glare doesn’t just hurt to look at, either; it can wash out real hazards — cracks, uneven footing, even other travelers — until they’re nearly invisible in plain sight.
