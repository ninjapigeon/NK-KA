# One

The last customer left when the Scar had wheeled to a hand's-width from full dark, which gave Klar and me just long enough to close before the light went and we'd be finishing by firelight.

"You don't have to stay," I told him — which was true, and also the thing you say when you'd rather not lock up alone.

"I'm not in a hurry." He kept stacking bowls, and didn't make anything of it, which was a kindness. Klar was five thousand spins old — barely grown, by the reckoning we keep, where the Scar turns over you once a day and even a long life runs out somewhere short of twenty thousand. He cooked like a man three lifetimes into it. On the nights I closed alone he always found a reason to still be there, and never said what it was, and I never made him. I wiped the tables. He dealt with the kitchen. 

Through the shutters came the militia's boots on the packed earth, the patrol heading out, a touch of wax scent in their wake from their yet-unlit torches.

They'd been in an hour before, as they were every night: the whole watch at the bar for one measure each, which Tondi charged them half for and never explained. Courage, they called it, without embarrassment, because there was none to feel. Two of them drank fast and made faces. One held his under his nose a while first, like a man reading a letter whose contents he already knew. Roan took water. He always took water. He stood there with his cup while the rest of them warmed up, and laughed in the right places, and went out into the dark with nothing in him but water, the same as every other night.

The Scar was almost across. Thirty minutes, maybe, before I'd want a locked door between me and the dark. The banked fire and the warm bricks smelled, as they did every night, exactly like the start of a morning, and I had never found a way to talk my nose out of it.

The greybeards call them Titanspawn. The militia call them problems. The rest of us don't call them anything — you don't name the thing that took the Pell family, you just get your ladder up before the Scar finishes crossing and you don't listen too hard to the sounds that come, some nights, off the far side of the wall. After long enough the not-thinking is a skill of its own. I'd had the practice.

Ours was not a remarkable town, and everyone who passed through said as much, kindly enough, and enough of them passed through to make it a fair verdict. The trail linked two larger places, so we got travellers, and travellers meant trade, and trade was the whole reason there was an inn for me to close. Tondi had taken me on as a girl — old enough to carry a stack of bowls without wearing them — back when my father had stopped being someone you could count on to be anywhere in particular. He was alive, last anyone knew. Just elsewhere. I was good at not thinking about that.

"That's me." Klar hung his apron on its hook and squared it so it wouldn't crease, and at the door he stopped. "Bar it after me."

"Go home, Klar."

His feet crossed the yard, the tall gate clattered, and the last living sound went with him, and left me the fire and the dark and the patrol thinning out around the mill.

The table under the window was Kern's. He was the Kro who'd been coming to the inn longer than I'd been closing it — a fixture, like the memorial pillar — in for a single cup most afternoons, which he made last until the light moved. He brought his bow, and the scent of the forest with him, the way he always did: the bow shorter than you'd expect of something so old, recurved, horn-tipped, no taller than his own chest, carved its whole length too fine to follow, worn pale where his shoulder rode it, old red worked deep in the bindings. The wood long since lost its own unique smell, carrying instead those of its owners. A family piece, he'd told me once, when I was small enough to ask rude questions. Virek-made — *may they never be forgotten*; he said the charm the way everyone does, the way you respond to someone who sneezed. Older than the inn. Older than him. A Kro doesn't call a thing old just to be saying something. Roan had asked him once, early on, why he didn't carry a Boltcaster like every other man who'd soldiered — Boltcasters being common as boots, and a good deal less trouble to restring. Kern had looked at him the way you look at someone who has asked a perfectly reasonable question in entirely the wrong room. "The Charter sells the bolt," he'd said. "Not the bow." He hadn't explained further, and Roan hadn't asked twice. I understood it a little better later, when I'd seen more of the world and what the Powder Charter's hand was in all of it — that a man carrying a weapon two hundred thousand spins out of fashion, that answered to no licence and no toll and no supplier, was saying something with his shoulder that he'd never once said with his mouth. I'd wiped around it a hundred times and thought it handsome and in my way, that was the whole of what it was to me, then.

I made my round. Six tables, and I knew the wobble in each. The lockbox in the floor — lever down, lid true, the way Tondi had shown me the first night she trusted me with it, kneeling on the alcohol-preserved boards beside me as if she were teaching me something holy. I straightened chairs that were already straight, which was either professional habit or a way of not going home yet. I knew which.

Through the gap in the shutters, if you stood right, you could see the memorial pillar — knee-high, three-sided, every name the town had buried since its founding. Not a long list. We were young, as towns go. I stood there too long and looked at it in the last of the Scar's light, and felt the quiet come down the way it did some nights, and not as a comfort. I'd been as far as Dunarth once, spireward, on a caravan, and back, and that was the whole of everywhere I'd been that wasn't here. I wasn't unhappy. I was unhappy the way you aren't hungry until the food is carried past you — which holds up fine, right until something changes.

Nothing was going to change. That was the thing about quiet towns.

I banked the fire, took my coat from the peg, and stepped out as the last Scar-light went out of the sky and the true dark of Anka came down — flat and total, the wound turning slow overhead and shedding its black straight down. Far off, the Spire still held its light. I stood at the gate a moment, taking the temperature of out there before I trusted myself to it, then spun the lock. Sem's lamp burned over the carpenter's: working late, or just not going to bed, the same thing at his age. The boundary spikes at the town's edge had gone invisible in the dark, which had always struck me as a flaw in the design.

The ordinary kind of dangerous, which in our town meant probably fine.

I was three paces toward home when the sound came from the town entrance — not the patrol. Two of the militia called out, voices pitched a notch high, the way they go when the body decides something before the mind catches up. Then quiet. Then the careful talk of people working out what to do about a thing rather than running from it. In hand, then. I kept walking. Almost believed it.

Halfway home, past the dark bulk of the mill with its own smell of ground grains, boots came up behind me — quick, uneven, heavier on the left. Roan's gait; he'd favoured that leg since before either of us was old enough to know why.

"Tasha! Hold up—" A wobble in his voice I didn't like. "Tasha, I need—"

I turned. Roan was coming up the path at the pace that isn't quite running, because running would mean admitting something. And he wasn't alone. Beside him, keeping easy time with a stride that had nothing to do with Roan's hurry, came a shape I had no ready name for: thin, hooded, walking on something that didn't sound like feet on the packed dirt.

I was bad at leaving things alone. It was either a failing or the one interesting thing about me — and with my hand drifting toward my blade, I had the brief, unwelcome feeling I was about to learn which.

# Two

They stopped a few paces short of me — far enough to let me look at the thing without it looming, and looming was clearly something it could do without trying.

Wooden limbs, with just disturbingly too-long forearms, wrapped in something like leather, in the way of something built to suggest muscle without quite managing it. A cloak doing an earnest, unsuccessful job of concealing the wearer, mostly because the wearer had cloven hooves where feet should be, and the sole-less boots someone had pulled over them had long since worn through. And in the shadow of the hood, slightly off-centre, one eye: a hole in smooth wood, lit from somewhere inside, a steady blue. Not facing me — and then it was, the glow finding me, settling, brightening a fraction as it fixed in place.

Strapped across the back of its waist, only the leather-wrapped handles showing, were a pair of blades I'd later learn were Falkas. Each one as long as a forearm, delicately carved their entire length, leaf-shaped but angled slightly down, and weighted toward the tip. "Better for chopping" I was later told. The cloak hid the hooves badly, the hood didn't hide the eye at all, and the whole of it gave the impression of something that had learned how to be unalarming from a single description, once, and gotten every detail a little, and terribly, wrong. The way a large animal sitting still ans watching you, is worse than one already moving. Roan wore the scent of militia in old, barely used leather armor. His companion wore the scents of the world.

My hand was on my own blade. I left it there.

"They wouldn't let it through the gate on its own," Roan said, in a rush, like he'd been holding the words in his mouth the whole way over. "Course they wouldn't. But it asked — it talks, Tasha — it asked for Tondi by name, and Pek said bring it to whoever's at the inn and let them sort it, so I—" He gestured at the entire situation. "So I brought it. To whoever's at the inn. Which I was hoping would be Tondi."

"Tondi's gone to bed."

"Yeah." His face fell about as far as a face can fall. "I was afraid you'd say that."

The thing's eye stayed on me. The body hadn't moved to follow it — the whole of it as still as the mill behind us, which should have been reassuring and wasn't.

"It wants to come in," Roan added, when the silence had gone on long enough to need something in it. "Into the inn, I mean. Not just the town. That's the part I didn't want to decide on my own."

"I did ask," the thing said.

The voice arrived like the first stones of a rockslide — not loud, not sharp, felt as much as heard, happening in the chest before it reached the ears. I hadn't expected it to speak. I took my hand off my blade — not because I'd decided it was safe, but because I'd decided that if it wasn't, my blade wouldn't be the relevant variable.

"All right," I said, mostly to buy a breath. Then, to Roan, because it was easier than talking to the thing: "What did Tondi tell you to do, exactly? Her words."

He worked at it. "She said — because I explained it had already come yesterday, and asked, which she said meant it was reasonable — if it's reasonable, there's a condition. For letting it in. She said tell it to bring back the head of the Subsurface Shredder, the one that's been working the trail anti-spinward. Proof it's what it says it is." He glanced at it. "And bring the head to her. Not to take its word."

"You heard her," I said to the thing. "Proof. Do you have the Shredder's head?"

"I do."

Then it reached an arm around to its own back — not turning, not rotating at the shoulder the way a person would, the arm just going where arms don't go, at angles that put a sympathetic twinge in my own shoulder — and produced, from somewhere behind itself, the head. Held it out at arm's length, in a manner that suggested it found the object neither interesting nor troubling.

I looked at the head. Squid-like, but with six eyes, each pair a different size, all of them clouded now. The skin pale past the pallor of death — a thing that had lived mostly without light. By reputation it ambushed the unwary and was the last thing they ever met. By appearance, now, it was an oversized dead fish. Didn't smell much better either. Funny, how much death takes out of a thing.

Roan had the look of a man who's found a puppy and is hoping no one will tell him to put it down.

"Put that away," I said. "Please. And — fine. Come back to the inn. I'm not deciding anything out here in the dark."

It returned the head to wherever it kept such things, somewhere under that cloak, with that same wrong, unhurried motion, and said nothing, which I was starting to understand was how it agreed.

So the three of us went to the inn — me in front, because it felt important to be in front; the thing behind me, its tread landing wrong on the packed dirt; Roan at the rear with the air of a man escorting a prisoner he isn't sure is actually his. The town stayed dark and shuttered. If anyone watched, they kept it to themselves.

At the inn I unlocked the gate but didn't open it, and turned to face the thing properly, with the building at my back as though that would help.

"Tondi set the condition," I said, "and Tondi's asleep, and I'm not waking her for this. So. You can't come in until she's seen that head and said you're what you say you are. Her rule, not mine, and I'm not clever enough to overrule it tonight. But you can wait inside the gate. That much is my call."

The eye stayed on me, and the quality of its stillness shifted in a way I couldn't have named — not impatience; nothing so humin as that. More like a sum coming out even.

"Then I will wait," it said, and stepped through the gate, and stood beside the door, and went, as far as I could tell, completely still.

"What do I call you?" I said, because I had to call it something, and *it* was starting to feel rude to its face.

"Third."

That was all. Not a name so much as a place in a line, said the way you'd give a count.

I looked at Roan. Roan looked at the thing.

"You're the one who told it to come here," I said.

"Tondi told me to bring it. If it—"

"Tondi told you to bring the head. She didn't say anything about the rest of it."

He opened his mouth. Closed it.

I checked the lock — combination set, the hexwood bolt sliding home with its reassuring click — and gave Roan the look I hoped said *not my problem*, and left.

Behind me, rounding the mill, I heard him say, at about a third of his usual volume, "Do you need anything? Water, or—" and stop, presumably having remembered what he was talking to.

I didn't hear the answer, if there was one.

At home I sat on the edge of the bed a while without taking my coat off.

The thing had spoken. It had been polite. It had the Shredder's head, which meant the test was passed, or would be once Tondi was awake to call it passed. It had walked to a wall and stood against it like it could do that until the world ended and not mind.

None of which was, technically, alarming.

I took my coat off. Told myself I'd sleep, and was surprised, after a while, to find that I had.
