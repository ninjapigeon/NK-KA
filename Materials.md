# Materials of Anka

Twenty substances and goods that shape daily life on Anka — what people build with, fight with, light their homes with, and trade with, in a world where good metal is scarce and the gaps get filled with wood, glass, jade, and worse. As with the other compendiums, dice and roll mechanics from the source notes have been reworded into plain description, and entries marked *(new)* round out the list. A short appendix on construction and trades follows the main list.

-----

## Arcane Batteries

Made from powdered jade ignited with magefire dust and sealed into glass bottles, an arcane battery lights up with green-tinted lightning when its runes are properly aligned with whatever it’s meant to power. Three sizes cover nearly everything, standardized by the Dustmen and named for the jade wiring inside: multi-wire, keg-barrel-sized batteries, racked in numbers to drive a skybarge’s fans; double-wire, palm-sized “pickle-jar” batteries, sized for self-wheeled carts and similarly heavy-duty conveniences; and single-wire bottles for lanterns, tools, and anything else that only needs a trickle.

A single-wire bottle like this typically lasts somewhere in the range of sixty to eighty spins before it gutters out for good, which means anyone relying on them is never more than a stretch of spins away from needing another one — scrounging, buying, trading, or finding a replacement is a constant, background fact of life. Large vessels last considerably longer, but they’re rare and expensive enough that most people never own one outright; access to a keg-sized battery is usually a sign of real wealth, or a very generous patron. Even a pickle-jar battery isn’t cheap, which is part of why self-carts remain a luxury rather than the norm.

> [!tag]- Secret

How an arcane battery is actually made — what process turns powdered jade and magefire into something that holds a charge for sixty-plus spins rather than discharging all at once — is known only to Keth crafters. The same goes for the magefire “bolts” that load into a Buhar. Every legitimate battery and bolt on Anka passes through Keth hands at some point, sold on through the Standards Guild — which sets the prices, controls the supply, and exists, as far as most outsiders can tell, for more or less that reason alone.

## Armor & Shields *(expanded from “Serpent-Scale Armor”)*

Anka’s armor runs in a rough hierarchy, mostly dictated by what a person can afford and what a Carpenter or Alchemist — there being no smiths on Anka in the traditional sense — has on hand to work with.

At the cheap end is plain leather: boiled and layered goat-hide (see Goat-hide Leather), worn as-is or padded under something heavier. It’s unglamorous, but it’s everywhere, and for most people it’s the only armor they’ll ever own.

Above that sits scale armor proper — not a single item but a construction principle: overlapping articulated plates. Serpen scale armor upgrades this with small interlocking teeth on each scale’s edge. On compression (a blow landing), the teeth catch against adjacent scales and lock, distributing the impact force across a wider surface; on extension (the wearer moving), they release, preserving flexibility. The principle stays the same no matter what the scales are made of — only the material changes, and with it, the cost and the protection:

- **Leather scale** — typically goat leather, cut and layered into scales and bound together with jadevine, rather than left plain. Lighter and cheaper than wood, but the individual scales wear down fast and need regular replacing.
- **Wood scale** — plain, unanointed wood, carved into interlocking plates. Cheap and common, and exactly as durable as you’d expect from ordinary wood taking ordinary blows — most wood-scale armor doesn’t survive its owner’s first real fight.
- **Hexwood scale** — wood-scale plates anointed after carving, hardening them the way any other Hexwood item hardens, then linked with Vinewrap cordage at the joints. Lighter than it looks and significantly tougher than its unanointed or jade alternatives; this is what most people mean when they say “proper armor.”
- **Serpent scale** — functionally identical to Hexwood scale, but distinguished by its design: this term is reserved for the Keth pattern specifically (the resemblance to snake scales, and to the Keth’s own serpentine lower bodies, is where “serpent-scale” comes from in the first place). Keth crafters worked out the interlocking-tooth principle independently, roughly 10,000 spins after Titansfall — an original invention, not derived from anything older. Keth armorers remain the acknowledged masters of the form; their ceremonial work, with every tooth individually shaped and the whole piece balanced for its specific wearer, is the kind of thing that becomes an heirloom.

Shields, small and large, follow the same logic as everything else on Anka: anointed Hexwood holds up, and almost nothing else does. A cheap, unanointed wood shield is common enough — they’re fast and easy to make — but one that hasn’t been anointed rarely survives past its first real battle; the wood simply gives out under repeated impact. Anyone who can afford it anoints their shield along with the rest of their kit; anyone who can’t treats a shield as something closer to disposable than a plain wood-scale vest ever is.

## Blackglass

The smoky black material that makes up the Glass Sea, the Spire, and the glass “cracks” that run across Anka. Worked carefully, it takes an edge sharper than most metal and holds it a surprisingly long time, making it a favorite material for knives, arrowheads, and fine tools wherever it can be found close to the surface. The supply effectively never runs out: any blackglass removed from the Sea or the cracks seems to be replaced again over time, through whatever process the Titans left behind.

## Bonechalk

A pale, chalky powder ground from Titan Bones — the alabaster debris of the glutton Titan’s shattered teeth. Mixed with a binder, it makes a durable paint or marking compound, and because Titan Bones themselves are known to instinctively shy away from veins of black glass, lines of bonechalk are sometimes used to mark the boundaries of glass-vein territory — a rough-and-ready way of telling travelers (and certain creatures) where one kind of ground ends and another begins.

## Brackwater Pitch

A thick, tar-like substance harvested from the deeper parts of swamps and bogs, where decayed plant matter has compressed over long stretches of time into something close to natural tar. Heated and worked, it makes an effective waterproofing for boat hulls, roofing, and the seams of stilt-house foundations — about the only thing the swamps are reliably good for exporting, which towns on their edges are happy to do at a markup.

## Boltcaster
# The Bolt-Caster: Weapon Design Notes

*A speculative longarm for Anka, from a parallel civilization that never developed conventional gunpowder.*

### Premise

This civilization’s “gunpowder” is not a solid combustible propellant but a **binary reactive system** called **magefire**: a strange dry powder sealed in a glass vial, which reacts violently with water when the vial is shattered. There is no ignition, no spark, no flame — the trigger event is purely mechanical (a puncture), and the resulting pressure wave can be **100% directionally channeled**, eliminating the need for a heavy containment chamber. The discharge behaves less like a bullet pushed by expanding gas and more like a directed, lightning-like bolt — a coherent, violent pressure/energy release traveling down an open, fluted channel rather than a sealed bore.

### Caliber & Ammunition

- Bolt is shotgun-scale, roughly **.50 caliber**.
- Ammunition is a small sealed **cartridge-cell**: a glass vial of magefire powder plus a water charge, roughly cartridge/shotgun-shell sized.
- Cells are fed from a **revolving cartridge drum** mounted just ahead of the trigger, cycled automatically by the recoil-driven mechanism (see below).
- No muzzle ornamentation — a clean, flush bore opening (earlier design generations used a decorative flared muzzle ring as a stylistic holdover; current design drops it).

### Overall Configuration

Held **bazooka-style, over the shoulder**, with the weight and mechanism sitting *behind* the shoulder rather than a conventional in-line stock:

- A **shoulder yoke/saddle** roughly a third of the way back from the muzzle acts as the balance point and pivot.
- Forward of the yoke: a **slender, fluted, blade-like open-channel barrel** — not a closed bore, but a guided channel, since the weapon directs a pressure-wave discharge rather than containing gas behind a solid projectile.
- Behind the shoulder: a **substantial rear counterweight pod** housing the recoil/reload mechanism — proportionally dramatic against the comparatively modest bore, giving a strong “small deadly bore, big engineered machinery” visual contrast.

## Recoil & Reload Mechanism — Clockwork, Not Hydraulics

Rejected a simple butt-pad/padding solution in favor of an actual engineered energy-management system, styled as **dark-ages clockwork** rather than Victorian hydraulics:

- The barrel assembly has limited rearward travel on discharge.
- This recoil stroke **winds a heavy iron mainspring** housed in the rear pod.
- The mainspring’s energy is bled off slowly and safely through a **visible escapement and gear train** (the mechanical-clock solution to metering stored spring energy) — this is what actually converts a sharp, shoulder-jarring kick into a manageable, delayed release, rather than merely padding the impact.
- A small **notched iron tension-dial** on the pod indicates mainspring tension (period-appropriate stand-in for a pressure gauge).
- The same gear train **cycles the revolving cartridge drum**, chambering the next cell automatically — recoil-operated reloading, an emergent function of the recoil system rather than a separate hand-operated action.
- Iron is used **sparingly and only for moving clockwork parts** — springs, gears, axles. All structural elements are wood.

## Materials

- **Hexwood** — a dark, blood-anointed wood resembling deep mahogany, used for barrel, yoke, and housing. This is not a steel substitute for aesthetics only: Hexwood is established as **structurally equal to forged steel**, its strength tied to the ritual/runic carving rather than the material alone.
- **Dense, continuous runic carvings** cover every structural surface — the runes are load-bearing and integral to the wood’s supernatural strength, not mere decoration. Runes glow faintly with a dull ember-like light where carved deepest.
- Hexwood is darkened and worn smooth from handling and old bloodstains, reinforcing the “anointed/consecrated weapon” cultural framing.
- Iron (black, hand-forged) appears only in the clockwork internals.
- No brass, no polished steel, no glass sight-windows, no visible hydraulics — deliberately avoiding a “scientific instrument” read in favor of dark-ages craft.

## Vocabulary Note

Because the weapon has no ignition/combustion step, “fire” and “shoot” are poor organic fits for this culture’s vocabulary — those words derive from a flame-based weapon tradition this civilization skipped entirely. More plausible native terms, given the puncture-then-discharge mechanic and the “bolt” naming convention already in use:

- **“Loose”** (as in “loosing a bolt”) — likely primary verb; direct inheritance from crossbow/bow terminology, fits an archaic register, and describes releasing a triggered/stored action rather than igniting one.
- **“Strike”** or **“break”** — plausible technical term among weapon-makers specifically for the vial-puncturing action.
- **“Crack”** — onomatopoeic option for the discharge’s sound, similar to thunder.

If “fire”/“shoot” appear in-world at all, they’d read best as a fossilized borrowing from an earlier flame-weapon tradition, or as an outsider’s/mistranslator’s word choice — a useful detail for distinguishing POV or culture in dialogue.

## Buhar

For something with as much status attached to owning one, a Buhar is mechanically little more than a sealed tube: a magefire “bolt” loaded at the breech, and a small chamber of water just ahead of it. Squeeze the trigger at the top, and the bolt ignites — flash-heating the water to steam fast enough that the resulting burst drives down the tube and out the far end before the eye can follow it, carrying with it the same green-tinted lightning discharge seen in arcane batteries. There’s nothing solid in a Buhar’s shot — no ball, no dart, nothing left to dig out afterward — just superheated steam and a crackling charge, traveling in an unnaturally straight line. To anyone on the receiving end — armored or not — the impact gets described the same way, again and again: like being hit by a charging Tusker.

A Buhar is meant to be braced against the shoulder and sighted down the barrel, in the manner of the heavier weapons the old war-era stories describe. Reloading means seating a fresh magefire bolt and topping up the water chamber — neither step is difficult, but neither is quick, and a spent bolt is genuinely spent: there’s nothing to recover from the ground afterward.

Tri-Buhars also exist: three barrels arranged around a shared stock, each loaded independently, letting a single shooter put out up to three shots before reloading anything. The tradeoff is real, though — a Tri-Buhar takes roughly twice as long to reload as a single-barrel Buhar, and costs about twice as much to begin with, before factoring in the cost of keeping three bolts on hand instead of one.

The name itself is older than most who carry one ever bother to ask about — said to come from a trade-tongue word that simply meant “steam.” Whatever its origin, it stuck, the way practical names tend to.

## Coinage

Anka mostly trades in two kinds of coin. “Black” is silver at heart, though you’d be forgiven for not knowing it — every coin in circulation is a uniform, tarnished black, the tarnish being the same ambient Magefire dust that reacts with bare metal everywhere. “Teeth” are small, wooden, tricorn-shaped tokens, roughly ten to a Black, each struck with the likeness of whatever local authority minted it.

A freshly minted Black blackens within days of leaving the mint, and faster still once it touches another Black in the same purse. Every so often, a traveler arrives with a story about seeing a coin that was still bright, untarnished silver — and the story always ends the same way: the moment that coin touches another Black, it blackens too, spreading “like a disease,” as the old saying goes. Nobody disputes the stories, exactly; they just shrug, since by the time anyone goes looking, the evidence has already turned black like everything else.

A Tooth's minting happens in two steps. It's struck first, as an ordinary, uncured block of ordinary wood — only the blank itself is standardized (the grade of wood, the strike depth and pattern), never the face, which is left to local design. The actual mint act comes after: a freshly struck Tooth is anointed with wet titan blood, which is what hardens it into true Hexwood, mahogany-red and effectively unalterable. Before anointing, a strike can still be re-cut; after, it can't be without destroying the coin. Like any Hexwood item, the hardening fades without renewal — an un-reanointed Tooth softens back to ordinary wood after roughly a thousand spins, quietly aging out of circulation as hard currency on its own, no institutional recall needed.

Verifying a coin’s worth in the field is mostly a matter of trust and a practiced eye rather than anything formal. A Black is checked by scratching the tarnish to confirm real silver underneath, then weighing it. Teeth take more familiarity — someone who knows the trade routes and the local rune-styles can usually tell a legitimate Tooth from a counterfeit at a glance, or close to it. When a Tooth arrives from somewhere remote enough that nobody local can vouch for it, the honest fix is to hire a spellcaster who can confirm the anointing directly — but that costs more than most disputed Teeth are actually worth, so in practice, anyone unsure of a Tooth just insists on Black instead, and hands out “legitimate” Teeth as change to keep the argument from ever starting.

Anything bigger than either currency conveniently covers gets settled the old way: barter, in higher-value goods.

> [!tag]- Secret

Every so often, a coated Tooth turns up in an old hoard — one plated in gold or silver rather than left as bare wood. Gold-plated ones trade for the same as a single Black; silver-plated ones for half that. Both are easy to spot on nothing more than a careful look and a feel of the weight, so they’re more a curiosity for collectors than a real complication for trade.

Rarer, and a genuine curiosity even among people who understand how minting works, are Teeth that simply never soften — struck decades or centuries past their expected thousand-spin fade and somehow still hard as the day they were anointed. Nobody’s confirmed why; a few link it to teeth minted with unusually pure or unusually old titan blood, closer to the source than the going grade. As currency, a Tooth like this is worth exactly what any other Tooth is worth — the durability doesn’t add value, just a story.

## Glasswort Resin

Rendered down from the sap of the Glasswort plant, this thick resin works as both an adhesive and a rough waterproofing — useful anywhere, but especially valuable for travelers crossing the Glass Sea, where Glasswort is one of the few living things around to provide it. A sealed pack treated with Glasswort resin can shrug off the Glass Sea’s bone-dry days and frigid nights about equally well.

## Goat-hide Leather

Since nothing larger than a goat has ever been successfully domesticated on Anka, goat-hide is the leather — used for boots, belts, packs, jerkins, and the padding under whatever armor a person can scrape together. It’s unglamorous, but it’s everywhere, and a family’s goat herd doing double duty as both food and wardrobe is about as normal as it gets. Anything fancier — proper scale armor, tooled leatherwork — is either an heirloom, or a sign that someone’s done unusually well for themselves.

## Greybone Ink

Made from finely ground bone ash mixed with a trace of titan blood, greybone ink carries a faint blue shimmer that catches the light at certain angles — visible enough to be distinctive, but not so much that it looks unnatural. Scribes favor it for contracts, ledgers, and official records, since the shimmer is genuinely difficult to reproduce without the real ingredients; a document written in ordinary ink, where greybone was expected, is a quick tell that something’s been forged.

## Hexwood

Ordinary carved wood, anointed with wet titan blood while it’s still fresh, becomes Hexwood — the wood darkens and takes on a faint red sheen as the blood dries into it, and comes out harder than stone, the backbone of Anka’s weapons and tools in a world with precious little metal to spare. Hexwood items are treated with real respect: children aren’t allowed to play with them, family heirlooms carved by long-dead ancestors get passed down for generations, and a broken Hexwood blade is something a Mender fixes with real care, not just a quick patch.

Minor items — carving knives, utility blades — are often crafted and anointed by a family as coming-of-age gifts. The anointing doesn’t last forever, though: after roughly a thousand spins, the titan blood in a piece of Hexwood fades or evaporates, and the item needs to be re-anointed to keep its hardness. Before the Titans fell, their own minions carried something that looked like Hexwood but was actually titan-reinforced Jade — magic the Titans themselves maintained. When the Titans died, that reinforcement died with them, leaving those weapons as ordinary (if still poisonous) Jade. The handful of cases since where someone managed to magically reinforce such an item again are where the word “artifact” comes from.

*A note on timing:* Hexwood is a post-Titansfall material. The geysers — where wet Titan blood reaches the surface — are themselves a post-Titansfall phenomenon: the dying Titan’s circulatory system bleeding into the world. Before Titansfall, the blood was contained within a living being and never pooled on the surface at all. Anka’s pre-Titansfall peoples fought with jade, stone, and bone. The Forged were made to fight those enemies. Hexwood is one of the things that emerged *from* the Titan’s death, and changed the world’s balance of arms after the war was already over.

## Jade & Greensteel

A greenish stone, the color of oxidized copper and worked with something like copper's ductility, found throughout Anka's jungles, swamps, and forests — jade is the fallback for anyone who can't get their hands on Hexwood, or can't (or won't) work with titan blood at all. It isn't copper and doesn't behave like it chemically or metallurgically; the resemblance begins and ends with color and workability. On its own, jade tools are serviceable — softer than Hexwood, needing regular sharpening and upkeep, but workable without any of Hexwood's complications.

Coat a jade item in titan blood, though, and something different happens: rather than hardening like wood does, the jade becomes naturally poisonous — a paralytic that takes hold from even a touch (faster still from an actual wound), affects monsters as readily as people, and has no known antidote. Items treated this way are called Greensteel rather than Jade, and using one on an opponent is considered, by anyone who knows what they’re holding, roughly the same as delivering a killing blow with no possibility of mercy.

Popular belief holds that the anointing also leaves Greensteel as hard as Hexwood, on top of the poison — a notion widespread enough that most people simply take it as given. It isn’t true. Titan blood poisons jade; it doesn’t harden it, and a Greensteel blade is no tougher than the jade it was carved from. The same goes for copper or anything else someone tries the trick on, hoping for Hexwood-like results — it just turns poisonous, with none of the durability. Few who’ve actually tested the claim are eager to correct it, since admitting you know enough to have tried tends to invite more questions than it’s worth, so the myth persists. Few who understand what Greensteel really is are willing to make it in the first place, and fewer still are willing to admit to owning any. As for where jade itself comes from — some greybeards mutter that the veins running through Anka’s rock might be the blood of whichever Titan governed snakes and poison, though that’s never been more than a mutter.

## Lichen Dye

Made from dried and ground Lantern Lichen, this dye carries a faint, lasting blue glow that shows up clearly in the dark phase — used for trail markers, door numbers, and the occasional bit of decoration on clothing for those who want to be easy to spot at night (or, depending on the wearer’s intentions, easy to follow).

## Magefire

Magefire is the white, faintly blue-tinged powder that gives every Buhar its “bolt” and every arcane battery its charge — a crackling, blue-white glow that looks, more than anything, like lightning bottled and corked. Where the raw material actually comes from is a question with more folk theories than answers; greybeards argue about it the same way they argue about the dried, blackened residue left behind wherever a geyser finally dies, and about whether the two are secretly the same thing underneath. Nobody outside the trade has ever settled it, because nobody outside the trade has ever needed to: turning whatever the raw material is into the finished white powder — sealed into Buhar bolts, or packed into a battery’s casing — is a process known only to Keth, sold finished through the Standards Guild, and that’s where the question stops for almost everyone.

Magefire dust reacts with bare metal on contact, which is the quiet reason Anka’s metalworking has only ever leaned on Jade — anything else corrodes away faster than anyone could put it to use, so crafters fill the rest of the gap with Hexwood, stoneroot, and jade instead. And it was a Magefire-powered ritual, deliberately undertaken, that the Virek used in their final act — the one that split them into the Elves and the Orks.

## Pressed Coal *(new name for “Coal”)*

Not coal at all, in the old-world sense — pressed coal is made from Shard Tree branches mixed with a starch binder (potato or fungus-based, depending on what’s local) and compacted into dense, fist-sized blocks. A smith’s full day of work might go into producing a batch of these, sold to travelers as smokeless trail fuel, or to households for cooking and heating. A single block burns cleanly for around eight hours, and the same blocks fuel lamps in towns that have them — with lamplighters making their rounds to keep the lamps fed, much as they’d tend oil or candles elsewhere. Think of pressed coal as Anka’s answer to charcoal briquettes, not to actual coal.

## Sapglass Beads

Hardened beads of sap from the Sapglass Tree, collected and strung for jewelry, trade goods, and decoration. Depending on the tree and the soil it grew in, the beads can come out clear, or faintly tinted in greens, ambers, or blues — close enough to cut glass or semi-precious stone that traders from outside Anka have occasionally mistaken them for the real thing, much to the amusement of locals who know better.

## Titan Blood

Wet titan blood — dark red, shading toward black as it dries — wells up wherever the world’s old wounds reach the surface: the geysers. The common lore holds that the Titan’s heart, wherever it ended up, still beats somewhere far underground, and that the geysers are where its severed arteries finally reach daylight, pumping blood that’s been traveling through who-knows-what for who-knows-how-long. Nobody’s ever found the heart itself, and greybeards differ on whether anyone would want to.

Around every active geyser, the wet blood pools and runs off, and that pool feeds an overgrowth of dangerous flora that has to be cut back before anyone can safely work the site — an untended geyser chokes itself in its own monstrous garden before long. Wildlife that lingers too close to an unworked pool, drinking or wallowing in the runoff, doesn’t fare much better: long enough exposure corrupts an ordinary animal into something hungrier and harder to put down, which is reason enough on its own to keep a working geyser’s pool cleared.

The blood itself has uses beyond the obvious horror story, though. Distilled, it can be drunk for a short-lived (and ill-advised) burst of rage and strength. Anointed onto wood while still wet, it dries into the wood with a faint red sheen, hardening it into Hexwood. Mixed with jade, it makes the poisonous Greensteel.

Drinking the refined version is a real gamble. It might grant the drinker a noticeable, temporary surge of strength — or it might leave them shaky and weakened instead, with no way to know in advance which way it’ll go. Either way, the effects (strength or weakness) last about a quarter of a day, and either way, the drinker comes away from it a little colder, a little less able to feel for others, for that same stretch of time. It’s not a drink anyone takes twice without a reason.

When a geyser finally dies — when whatever artery fed it runs dry or seals over — the site falls silent, and the blood pooled around it dries down to something black and glassy. Enough of these dead sites, linked up across enough distance and time, are what most people are really looking at when they look at the cracks that run across Anka — and, by some accounts, the Glass Sea itself.

## Titan Bones

Mined from the scattered, alabaster-colored teeth of the glutton Titan that litter the Gnawing Fields, Titan Bones have an odd property: a fist-sized chip will pull away from a nearby vein of black glass, as if repelled by it, and a chip with a vein underneath it will happily hang in midair indefinitely, slowly turning. Larger pieces don’t do this — only the smaller chips manage it reliably, for reasons nobody’s explained, and even those stop floating if there’s too much black glass nearby all at once.

There’s a whole town at the rim of the Gnawing Fields that lives on nothing else: harvesting the floating chips — known simply as floatstone — sorting the ones that behave from the rarer ones that don’t (some chips, instead of drifting quietly, wake up when something moves nearby, which is its own kind of danger and its own kind of skill to spot), and selling the safe ones on as novelties and charms. A piece of genuine floatstone, visibly tugging away from any blackglass brought near it, is exactly the kind of small, strange, harmless-looking thing travelers like to carry home — though nobody’s ever managed to scale the effect into anything larger than a curiosity. Skybarges fly on fans and arcane batteries, same as everything else that flies; floatstone just floats.

## Vinewrap Cordage

Rope and cord made from stripped, dried Jadevine fibers — strong, flexible, and about as close to metal chain as most people on Anka will ever get their hands on. It’s the standard for hauling, climbing, rigging, and binding wherever metal isn’t an option, which is to say, almost everywhere.

## Whiteglow Wax

Candle wax rendered with a trace of Magefire dust mixed in, giving finished candles a faint blue-white glow that lingers even after the flame goes out — an “everlight” effect that fades slowly over many days rather than vanishing the moment the wick is snuffed. More expensive than plain wax, but popular for nurseries, shrines, and anywhere a little light through the dark phase is worth paying for.

## Appendix: Construction & Trades

A few notes on how Anka builds things and pays for them, reformatted from the original construction and trade notes.

**Building habits.** Most family homes are built with an eye toward keeping wandering monsters out: doors are set above ground level, reached by ladders or removable ramps, and stilt-raised housing with wraparound decks is common. A few towns take this further — corkscrewing entire houses into the ground at night, or building underground or into cliff faces outright. Flying threats are rare enough that a few militia with Buhars or spears are usually enough to discourage one. With metal scarce, doors tend to be sliding panels in greased grooves rather than anything hinged. Valuables go into an in-ground lockbox — a Hexwood-reinforced box that rises out of a hidden floor recess when the right lever is pulled, secured further by a combination lock that only becomes accessible once the box has risen. A simple physical key is a last resort, since such keys are delicate and easy to force.

**Trades.** Alchemists dye clothing and, away from a geyser, can anoint items into Hexwood themselves — which means they’re also the ones hoarding and buying vials of titan blood for that purpose. They sometimes hire outside help to acquire dangerous dye ingredients, like red ant hives (queen included) or certain strange blue rocks. Carpenters handle non-Hexwood woodwork and often specialize — weaponcraft, Hexwood-scale armor plates later sent for anointing — effectively filling the role a “smith” would in most other settings. Menders fix clothing, usually working out of an inn, and occasionally handle minor repairs on other goods that don’t require specialized skill.

**Everyday work.** Most townsfolk are simply busy — cleaning, building, repairing, resting. Taverns sell food and drink; inns rent rooms; most settlements need guards, and many keep goats or dogs for stabling and hauling. An inn or tavern often doubles as the town’s informal message drop, since it’s the one place with light where everyone passes through at day’s end. The “lawnmowing jobs” of more conventional fantasy settings — odd work for the young or unskilled — tend to cluster around these same taverns and inns.
