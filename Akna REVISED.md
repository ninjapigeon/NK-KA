The poet and philosopher Nev Rav Dal wrote, *“I have stood at the edge of the grey, and felt every tale I have ever told grow thin and quiet, as though it too were being read for the last time.”*

Akna — known to nearly everyone, in everyday speech, simply as the grey — is the place beneath and behind everything else. It doesn’t sit beside Anka so much as underneath it, closer to every other place than any of those places are to each other. Nothing so formal as a spell or a device is needed to reach it. Those who know how simply step sideways, and find themselves there. In the absence of that knowledge however, a good spell has still worked.

In the grey, Anka itself goes thin. Color drains out of everything — ground, trees, people — until they’re little more than shapes of smoke, with faint wisps rising off them and drifting away into the haze. Living things in Anka do have real 'substance' in the grey, but cannot be hurt in Anka from the Akna side. 
The wisps seem to be going somewhere, though no one who’s stood in the grey and watched them has ever followed one far enough to find out where. Faint whispers carry on no wind at all, in no language anyone recognizes, and somewhere below — always below, no matter which way is down — something drones on without ever quite stopping.

There is no gravity in the grey, no real up or down, and solid ground is rare and untrustworthy where it exists at all, save that there is always something to walk on. The place itself is a swirling, misty void of grey, purple, and dark blue, occasionally disturbed by mysterious winds or a mystical storm, but otherwise it sees few changes from one visit to the next. Time moves strangely there, too — something like a hundred times faster than in Anka, so a long stretch spent walking the grey might cost a traveler only a handful of spins back home. The longer someone lingers, the harder the way back becomes to find; the grey doesn’t release people so much as it eventually lets them go.

Akna is held to be the source of all elemental energies and beings on Anka, and of demons and stranger things besides — though few who claim this have gone deep enough to confirm it firsthand, and fewer still have returned to argue.

Stepping sideways is easiest at “thin places” — spots where the grey presses close against Anka, often marked by the world itself reacting: greywillow trees losing their color, sounds going muffled and distant, even the Scar’s light seeming to dim a shade. Most people can walk through a thin place and feel nothing more than a chill or a moment’s unease. Those with the knack barely have to try; for them, a thin place is closer to falling than stepping.

The deeper grey holds more than wisps and whispers. Greybeards say the grey is where stories go to die — and not only as a turn of phrase. Things that are lost completely, with no one left to remember them and nothing left to mark where they stood, sometimes simply aren’t, afterward: the ground, the bodies, even the grief over them, taken by the grey rather than left to rot. No one has ever found where these things go. And whatever lives deepest in the grey — if anything does — has never been seen by anyone who came back to describe it.
