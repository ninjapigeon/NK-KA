# Fauna of Anka

This compendium splits into two parts. The **Bestiary** covers roughly twenty creatures and monsters — the things that bite, swarm, burrow, or otherwise complicate a traveler’s day — sorted alphabetically. The **Kindred & Peoples** section covers the sapient races (already catalogued in more depth elsewhere in the World Bible, e.g. *Kin.md*) and is included here only because the source notes mixed the two together; it isn’t part of the “~20” count.

As with the Flora compendium, every entry has had dice, stat blocks, and roll instructions translated into plain description of what actually happens. Entries marked *(new)* are additions written to round out the Bestiary to about twenty.

-----

## BESTIARY

### Air Jellies

Strange, translucent, fleshy bubbles about the size of an open palm, with long thin feelers trailing beneath. Lighter than air, they drift wherever the wind takes them, entirely at its mercy. Anything the feelers brush against gets coated in a sticky liquid — harmless to skin, but it eats through worked jade and metal alike, leaving pitted, ruined surfaces behind. A drifting cloud of them looks almost beautiful until someone’s armor starts dissolving.

### Axe Beak

Enormous, flightless, carnivorous “chickens” standing about a humin and a half tall, with beaks full of teeth and a thick covering of feathers tough enough to be laminated into armor or shields. They hunt in packs and are rumored to bring down prey considerably larger than themselves. Their bite is the obvious danger, but their kicks are just as capable of opening a humin or Virek up in a single pass — anyone fighting one should watch both ends.

### Causeway Crab *(new)*

A broad, flattened crab-like scavenger that wedges itself into the gaps and undersides of the Emperor’s Causeway’s arches, its mottled shell an uncanny match for the weathered stone. It feeds mostly on whatever falls or is thrown from the roadway above — refuse, carrion, the odd unlucky animal — but during the dark phase, anything that lingers too close to the edge of an arch may find a causeway crab isn’t picky about whether its meal is already dead. Travelers who’ve heard the stories tend not to rest directly beneath the arches after dark.

### Chestnut Bugs

Small, round, beetle-like creatures about the size of a child’s fist, usually found clustered near discarded walnut husks. Individually they’re well-armored — almost comically so for their size — and any single one is more annoying than dangerous. The real trouble is that they swarm anything warm-blooded that wanders too close, and a swarm is much harder to fight off than the math on any one bug would suggest. Fire is about the only thing that reliably clears them out.

### Fire Wrack

Fire Wracks were the war’s shock troops — forest fires given shape and sent screaming into the Virek’s ranks, with no particular concern for how many Forged got caught in the same attacks. What’s left of that legacy are creatures that look like dragons or great bat-winged serpents (though wingless variants exist, flying on the updrafts their own heat creates), with hearts like volcanoes and an inner fire that shows plainly through their eyes.

Simply standing near one is dangerous — the heat radiating from a Fire Wrack’s body is enough to scorch anyone who gets close, on top of whatever damage its claws, bite, or lashing tail do directly. When truly cornered, a Fire Wrack can unleash a single, much larger flare of flame that engulfs everything nearby — a last resort it doesn’t use lightly, since it leaves the creature spent afterward. For all their fury, old stories agree on one weakness: a Fire Wrack driven into deep water, or smothered under enough sodden, rotten wood, can be killed in a way that no blade manages easily.

### Flailing Dreadnoughts

Huge, floating, egg-shaped creatures armored in overlapping serpent-scale plating — interlocking articulated plates, bone-pale and Titan-willed, that lock together on impact and flex apart on movement. Built as weapons of war, they’re ferocious but not especially clever, and they’re drawn to the smell of cooked or prepared food: rendered fat, grain-smoke, the particular smell of a camp kitchen or a cooking fire. The Virek maintained organized armies with supply lines and hot meals, and the Titan built the Dreadnoughts to find armies — which is what cooked food has always meant, in a world where raw meat in the wilderness is everywhere and the smell of civilisation’s kitchen is not.

An experienced traveler in Dreadnought territory eats cold, carries dried and sealed provisions, and lights no cooking fire. Most people know the rule without knowing the reason; the mechanism itself has been mostly lost to folk knowledge. An inexperienced traveler who lights a fire has rung a dinner bell in the wrong direction and will find out quickly.

Between fights, the serpent-scale plates shift and part just enough to reveal chains tipped with hooks and clubs, which a dreadnought can fling out to drag a target closer. Against a group of enemies, it sometimes opens every plate at once and lashes out with all its chains in every direction — a devastating attack, but one that leaves the creature’s plating wide open afterward. Most things it hits don’t survive long enough to take advantage of that.
### Geyser Moth *(new)*

Pale, broad-winged moths that gather in clouds around active titan-blood geysers, where their wings slowly soak up the diluted vapor rising from the spray. The moths themselves are harmless — but a wing crushed against bare skin, or a moth swallowed by an animal hunting them, delivers a brief jolt of the same effect that drinking refined titan blood does: a short-lived surge of strength followed by an equally short fit of temper. Geyser-moth season (such as it is, on a world without seasons — really just whenever the local geyser is most active) is notorious for unusually short tempers in nearby settlements.

### Giant Hornbug

Wagon-sized bugs, found in black or blue coloration, usually in forested areas. Their oversized mandibles are strong enough to flip a wagon, bring down a tree, or tear through a wooden wall without much trouble. Some varieties carry a corrosive bite that does extra harm to anyone already caught and held by them. On the more practical side, their larvae — found in nearby dead trees — are edible, and considered a delicacy by most races willing to overlook where they came from.

### Giant Spider

Despite the name, these “giants” are only about the size of a Humin or Hin’s head — though that’s plenty large enough to be unsettling. Their bite poses little direct threat to a healthy adult, but the real danger is what happens to anyone who’s paralyzed by the venom: something about a paralyzed victim’s body chemistry draws every other giant spider in the area, and a paralyzed traveler can end up buried under a writhing mass of them, slowly dissolved where they lie. As if that weren’t bad enough, larger predators are known to stake out giant spider hunting grounds and simply wait for the venom to do the work for them.

### Grask

An enormous worm-like horror, with six long-fingered clawed hands sprouting at random points along its body and a mouth that splits into four tendril-like jaws around a pair of eyes set just above its throat. “There you are…” is, by reputation, usually the last thing its prey hears — its whispering, half-heard voice carries a paralyzing effect on anything with a mind to be unsettled.

When it bites to grapple a target, the wrapping of tendrils across its own face leaves it effectively blind, though that rarely matters much to whoever’s caught. If its burrow is threatened, it can vanish back underground almost instantly, abandoning a fight rather than risk itself.

### Giant Spider — see above; *Grinding Teeth* below.

### Grinding Teeth

Found only in the Gnawing Fields, these hovering clouds of splintered rock swirl like the leftover magic of a summoned whirlwind. Anything that moves within close range of one draws it in immediately — whether out of some kind of malicious intelligence or pure instinct, nobody’s been able to say for certain, since once the cloud reaches whatever moved, it simply stops again unless something else moves nearby.

Stationary or active, a Grinding Teeth cloud is dangerous to be near — it doesn’t “attack” so much as exist as a permanent, swirling hazard of slashing rock fragments. Spotting the dangerous clouds before getting close requires a careful eye; from a distance, they look exactly like the harmless drifting rock clusters that are common throughout the Fields.

### Land Oysters

Walking across a field of fist-sized rubble is hazardous enough on its own — and somewhere among that rubble, the very slowly moving Land Oysters are waiting. Usually they’re content to feed on passing insects, but a traveler who doesn’t notice one underfoot gets a painful surprise: the oyster snaps shut in alarm with a grip far stronger than its size suggests, clamping onto a foot or leg even through armor.

Freeing yourself — or killing the thing, which isn’t easy given how tough its shell is — at least comes with a small reward, since the meat inside is worth keeping. Once a patch of land oysters has been found, a careful forager who knows what to look for (their camouflage among the rubble is excellent, but they’re far too slow to avoid someone actively searching) can usually gather a few more.

### Ravagers

Also called Herdukii, after the Hunter Titan Herduk, these are speculated to be wolves twisted into something worse — possibly by Herduk’s own design, possibly (as some claim) simply from ordinary wolves drinking from pools of his spilled blood after his fall. Either way, Ravagers run with wolf packs, and there’s a persistent and uncomfortable rumor that any wolf that feeds on a Ravager’s kill becomes a Ravager itself within days.

They act rabid, nearly feral, with little to nothing in the way of speech — just snarling, howling, and whatever passes for coordination in a wolf pack. Armed, when they bother, with nothing more than thrown spears and crude bludgeons or blades.

### Spider-eye Goblins

Small, six-eyed (solid black), six-limbed humanoids that look uncomfortably like a child crossed with a spider — often called “spider-goblins” for short. They’re widely believed to have been a joke from the shaper Titan, one whose punchline the Titan never stuck around to appreciate.

They chew wood with mandible-like teeth into a pulp-paste, tougher than papier-mâché if not quite up to Hexwood standards, and use it for everything: weapons, armor, and their wasp-nest-like homes. They lack true silk glands, but their extra limbs and the way they’re raised give them remarkable climbing and brachiating ability — moving through a forest canopy is second nature to them. Their communication among themselves, via clicking and branch-tapping, is real but very rudimentary.

### Subsurface Shredder

Often just called “Shredders,” these look like an enormous squid that’s wandered out of water — four long, horn-tipped tentacles and a six-eyed head that barely break the surface during an attack. They ambush from below, capable of striking up to four targets at once within a short radius of the body, though each tentacle can only reach things close to itself. If the body itself is struck, the whole creature will reposition to stay just out of arm’s reach while remaining close enough to keep attacking.

The tentacles have to be dealt with individually before the body becomes vulnerable; once they are, the body tries to retreat, but can be caught and finished off. Despite six eyes (in three mismatched pairs), Shredders seem to rely on sensing movement through the ground far more than sight — they can find prey perfectly well in total darkness. Both the body and the tentacles provide a surprising amount of genuinely good meat, for anyone willing to do the butchering.

### Swamp Rats

Oversized rodents, blamed by every nearby village for every misfortune imaginable — ruined crops, ruined weddings, stolen livestock, you name it. Most people insist they don’t actually exist outside of stories, even while reflexively blaming them for the next inconvenience. In truth, they’re exactly what they sound like: unusually large rats that have learned to thrive at the edges of swampland, no more sinister than that — though try telling the locals.

### Tuskers

Bull-like beasts with four horns — two ordinary ones, and two more like an elephant’s tusks, though straight rather than curving — along with bison-like manes. At rest they’re placid, even boring. Charging, they’re a different story entirely: a Tusker at full charge hits with enough combined weight and momentum to make its size alone the weapon, regardless of what its horns do on the way in.

### Wood Wrack

During the war, Wood Wracks served as generals — granted more intelligence than their Fire Wrack cousins, and rarer for it. Once the Titans fell and the Titanspawn were abandoned to fend for themselves, that intelligence gave Wood Wracks a unique advantage: where most Titanspawn simply went feral, Wood Wracks turned to scheming. Those in the know claim it was Wood Wracks who first planted the idea, among the Virek, of purging their own “dark seed” — quietly hedging against the chance the Titans might ever return.

Physically, a Wood Wrack is a draconic shape built from the trees of swamps, bogs, and frozen forests, its eyes burning with witch-fire and an unpleasant stench hanging around it always. Anyone who lingers too close finds the cold radiating from its presence numbing — despite Anka having no real winter, prolonged exposure to a Wood Wrack’s chill can leave a person sluggish and eventually frostbitten. Its bark-like hide turns aside most blows, though a sharp eye can sometimes spot a weak seam where a well-placed strike might bypass it entirely — finding that seam in the middle of a fight is another matter. The one thing every Wood Wrack shares with its fire-born cousins, despite everything else, is a vulnerability to flame: fire hurts a Wood Wrack far more than anything else does, cold aura or no.

### Yrrip

An enormous spider, only distantly related to ordinary hunting spiders, with the singular trick of pulling all its legs together and going utterly still — at which point it looks exactly like a dead, hollow tree. Close inspection reveals two large eyes peering out from what looks like a knot-hole, the kind that’s normally just a hiding spot for squirrels or stashed goods.

Some varieties mimic Shard Trees convincingly; none, so far, have been found mimicking Squid Trees — whether because no Yrrip mimicking a squid tree has ever let prey escape to tell the tale, or because something about squid trees keeps Yrrip away in the first place, nobody knows. Its bite carries a powerful paralytic poison. As for its mind — or lack of one — Yrrip seem to have nothing that magic aimed at thought or emotion can actually take hold of; spells of that kind simply find nothing there to affect.

## KINDRED & PEOPLES

*(Reference only — not counted toward the Bestiary total. Fuller treatments exist elsewhere in the World Bible.)*

### Elves (Kr)

Born from the Virek’s final, self-inflicted ritual, Elves emerged pale-skinned — the “light” half of what the Virek chose to become. They carry their ancestors’ memories forward, and are the keepers and guardians of that knowledge, sharing little of it with other races. Elves and Orks share a deep, mutual hatred that everyone is aware of and almost no one can explain — though the truth, that they’re two halves of the same severed whole, is not exactly common knowledge.

### Forged

Constructs created by the Titans as an army of attack-drones, deliberately built to mock the Virek’s appearance even as they were sent to destroy them: wood-and-vine bodies as tough as Hexwood, a single eye set off-center on the face, arms that can split at the elbow into a four-armed configuration, hands ending in two thumbs and four fingers between both halves, and cloven, goat-like wooden hooves in place of feet.

When the last Titan fell and its dust touched the battlefield, many Forged “awakened” — gained genuine self-awareness for the first time. Most couldn’t cope with it, and retreated into a long torpor in swamps, caves, and other forgotten places. In the current era, a few have begun stirring again, having apparently spent their dormancy working through what awareness did to them.

### Giants

The first race the Titans made — immortal, but sterile, and the creators of the Virek in turn. After the Titans fell, the Giants vanished into hiding so thoroughly that even the Forged regard them as little more than rumor and legend.

### Goblins, Orks & Ogres (Rk)

Goblins are small, vicious, green nuisances — pack hunters with poor odds alone, who hate dogs, believe writing steals the soul, and love both fire and terrible songs in roughly equal measure. Left to mature, a goblin becomes an Ork, who typically seeks out others of its kind and settles into a tribe. Orks who live long enough become Ogres — and Ogres, often feeling pushed out by their old tribes, tend to wander off alone. Very occasionally, age mellows an Ogre just enough that it ends up leading a band of Orks after all; when word of that gets around, Elven hunting parties tend to form almost immediately, treating the whole arrangement as something close to an insult that demands correcting.

What’s not common knowledge: this entire lineage — goblin to Ork to Ogre — is what became of the “dark seed” the Virek purged from themselves during the splitting ritual that also created the Elves. Two halves of one severed whole, even if neither side would thank you for pointing it out.

### Hin

The “wee folk,” rarely over a meter tall and sometimes well under it. Folk wisdom sorts them by trade and appearance — the dirty, bearded ones doing the mining are “Dwarves,” the proud-hatted tinkers are “Gnomes,” and everyone else gets called “Halfling” — though Hin themselves seem largely indifferent to the distinction. Their origins are genuinely uncertain, and they don’t seem inclined to settle the question for anyone. One consistent Hin custom — gift-giving for “friends” after travel, at arrivals, or after victories, but never predictably or for every occasion — baffles outsiders without ever quite causing a war over it.

What’s less known: Hin aren’t a creation of the Giants or the Titans at all. A Hin is, at the root of things, a story — one that survived being told long enough to become real. Because of that, no Hin can ever set foot in Akna and come back. The grey is where stories that are no longer remembered finally come to rest and fade away — and a Hin who enters it doesn’t return from it, any more than an untold story does.

### Keth

Reptilian, near-serpentine folk with humanoid upper bodies and snake-like lower halves — most assume the shaper Titan made them from a mix of Humins and serpents, though the Keth themselves rarely confirm or deny it. Keth society is rigidly caste-based: each city is led by a Daimyo, served by life-loyal soldier-retainers in the Samurai mold, and Keth craftspeople are renowned for weapons and armor of exceptional, almost ceremonial quality. Among the adult population, “witch” druids are common, and the larger settlements support sorcerers as well — among the Keth, magical talent and political power tend to go hand in hand. They are famously, almost obsessively devoted to mastering magic wherever they can get access to it.

What’s less known: Keth crafters hold the only working knowledge of how raw Ptah becomes magefire — a closely guarded secret that, over centuries, has made them quietly indispensable to nearly everything on Anka that flies, fires a Buhar, or lights a battery. Few outside the caste structure ever see how that knowledge is actually used, or how far it reaches. “Scalykind” persists as an old Humin field term — blunt, and not what the Keth call themselves.

### Humins

The primary people of Anka in the current era, now that the Virek are gone. Their origin is contested even among greybeards — some traditions hold that the Giants made them, others that the Titans reforged Humins out of the Virek, or out of the Giants themselves, after the fact.

### Virek (may they never be forgotten)

A painful story of love, grief, abandonment, and ultimately, dissolution. The Virek were the first humanoids made on Anka, set loose by uncaring Titan “parents” with no more thought given to them than to the landscapes those same Titans built and destroyed on a whim.

As other races followed — races their creators seemed to actually care about, however little — the Virek watched cities and peoples rise and fall under casual Titanic indifference, and tried, when they could, to save what survivors they found. Eventually they realized what their “parents” truly were, and began studying them in secret: stealing magic from Methos, craft from Goltha, and the arts of hunting and war from Herduk. In time, they convinced the rest of Anka’s peoples that the Titans had to be overthrown — not as an act of vengeance, but because nothing else would survive if they weren’t.

They succeeded. And having succeeded, the Virek turned on what they saw as their own darkest impulses, the part of themselves they blamed for everything that had gone before — and in a final ritual, destroyed themselves to be rid of it. The magic involved was lost with them, and remains a cautionary tale for spellcasters everywhere.

What’s less known: the ritual worked exactly as intended, splitting the Virek into the pale-skinned Elves and the green-skinned Orks. The “dark seed” they tried to purge wasn’t destroyed, though — it became the goblins, who in time become Orks, and eventually Ogres. The Elves kept the ancestral memories; the Orks lost them. And the skin-color split — pale Elves, green-skinned goblins, Orks, and Ogres — followed the same line as everything else that was divided that day.
