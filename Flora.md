# Flora of Anka

A field guide to the plant life of Anka, drawn from traveler accounts, greybeard lore, and the hard-won experience of those who had to learn the hard way. 
-----

## Bellow Reed

Tall, hollow-stemmed reeds that favor open plains and the embankments along old causeways. When the breeze that follows the Scar’s light-to-dark (or dark-to-light) transition moves through a stand, the reeds let out a low, mournful moan — locals call it the “evening bellow” or “morning bellow,” and use it as a rough clock when the sky itself gives no other clue. A large stand in a strong wind can moan loud enough to cover the sound of someone approaching, which canny raiders along the causeways have been known to use to their advantage.

## Bogs

What looks like a welcoming meadow of knee-high reeds, with berry bushes at the margins and a gentle warmth from above, is often nothing of the sort. These reed-glades are nature’s way of slowly reclaiming a lake: floating seeds catch on still water, and within a surprisingly few spins a small-to-medium lake is entirely carpeted in green. Even a careful, experienced traveler can walk right up to one without realizing the ground ahead isn’t ground at all.

Once in the reeds, what looked like a field becomes a slow, exhausting wade through tangled roots and brackish water — closer to swimming than walking. Anyone who tires completely while struggling through risks being held under by the root system, and without help nearby, that’s how people drown in a “field.”

## Bone Moss

A pale, chalky moss that grows almost exclusively on titan-bone debris — the alabaster rubble scattered across the Gnawing Fields and similar sites. It gives off a faint white glow during the dark phase, bright enough that experienced foragers can spot fresh bone deposits from a distance just by watching for new patches. Chewed raw, it numbs the mouth and gums — useful for travelers without a Mender nearby — but regular use leaves a permanent grey stain on the teeth, a telltale sign of someone who’s spent too long scavenging the Fields.

## Cinder Bramble

A blackened, heavily thorned bramble that thrives at the edges of geyser fields and other scorched ground. In dry conditions its thorns become brittle and snap off at the slightest touch, throwing up a fine ash that stings eyes and throat — clearing a path through a dense patch without covering your face is a miserable, coughing business. The bramble’s berries are edible, but only after roasting; eaten raw they cause violent vomiting, a lesson every local child learns exactly once.

## Driftcane

A bamboo-like cane that colonizes the loose, wind-scoured soil along causeway embankments. Its roots stay shallow and wide rather than deep, which works well enough most of the time — but during the sustained high winds the causeways tend to funnel, entire stands can tear free and go tumbling down the road like a loose haystack, startling travelers and spooking pack animals. Once it’s been cut, dried, and anchored down, though, driftcane is light, strong, and a favorite for quick repairs and travel gear.

## Emberleaf

A tree whose broad leaves carry a slow chemical warmth that glows a dull orange through the dark phase, brightening faintly as the temperature drops. A handful of leaves, cupped in the hands or tucked into a glove, can keep fingers from going numb on a long dark-phase watch, or provide just enough light to read a map by. The catch: once a leaf is picked, its glow fades for good within a day or two, so groves near settlements are harvested in careful rotation rather than stripped all at once.

## Glassvine

A wiry, very dark green vine that grows shockingly fast — close to a finger’s width an hour — with a particular hunger for anything that isn’t moving. Leave a cart, a fence post, a fallen branch, or worse, sitting in one place too long, and it starts disappearing under a thickening wrap of glassvine, stem laid over stem, until the original shape is barely visible under the green. Once it’s taken hold of something — or of an area — getting rid of it for good is close to impossible.

From a distance the vine looks unremarkable, even soft; what isn’t obvious until too late is that its stems are threaded through with hair-fine splinters of something glass-like, packed in just beneath the bark. Brushing against an established patch, or handling it bare-handed, leaves a traveler with a scattering of shallow cuts — more an itching, bleeding annoyance than a real wound, but enough of them at once will wear a person down over a day’s travel.

Cutting something free of glassvine is worse than just walking through it. Sever a stem and it lets go of its splinters all at once, in a spray not unlike a dropped pane shattering — and a thoroughly wrapped object can mean dozens of stems to get through, each one its own small explosion of glass. It’s also notorious for dulling blades — jade ones especially — far faster than the cutting alone should warrant, and for dulling the patience of whoever’s been assigned to free a wagon wheel from a glassvine patch even faster than that.

## Glasswort 

A low, fleshy succulent that wedges itself into the cracks at the margins of the Glass Sea, one of the only living things that bothers to grow there at all. Its thick stems draw up moisture from far below the glass, making a glasswort patch a genuinely useful (if unglamorous) water source for anyone whose skins are running low while crossing that dry expanse. Reduced over a fire, the sap thickens into a sticky resin that doubles as glue or a rough waterproofing for boots and packs.

## Greywillow

A drooping, pale-barked tree that grows near the “thin” places where Akna presses close against Anka. Regardless of anything else going on around it — weather, soil, war — a greywillow’s leaves lose all color and turn a flat, matte grey whenever a thin spot nearby is active. Locals treat a sudden grove of greywillows as a warning sign and build elsewhere; old, established greywillow groves are generally left alone and given a wide berth out of long habit, whether or not anyone remembers exactly why.

## Hollow Cane

A reedy plant whose stems grow in long, naturally hollow, segmented lengths — practically pre-made piping. Refiners working geyser sites prize it for distilling and diluting titan blood, and it doesn’t react with or corrode from contact with the stuff. Alchemists further inland use it for much the same reason, running it through their stills and tinctures while saving money that may have been spent on jade or hexwood tubing.

## Jadevine

A fast-climbing vine with tough, fibrous, green-tinged stems that smother deadfalls, old ruins, and neglected fences in jungle and swamp country. Dried and stripped, the fibers twist into cordage nearly as strong as the jade stone it’s named for — for anyone needing a cord stronger than plant or animal fibres, jadevine rope is the standard for hauling, climbing, and binding.

## Kettle Fungus

A large, bowl-shaped fungus that grows on fallen logs in damp forest, its broad cap capable of holding a genuinely useful amount of rainwater or dew. Travelers short on water learn to check fallen logs for a mature kettle fungus — though it’s worth a glance inside before drinking, since the little reservoirs are popular with frogs, insects, and the occasional larger lodger who got there first.

## Lantern Lichen

A faintly blue-glowing lichen found on rocks near veins of black glass, and dead geysers, its glow a pale, harmless echo of magefire itself. Dried and ground, it makes a long-lasting dye with the same soft glow; left whole, a few clumps wedged into a crack make an excellent trail marker that stays visible all through the dark phase without needing to be lit, fed, or replaced for up to ten spins at a time.

## Occluding Trees

At a glance these look like ordinary conifers — cone-shaped, covered in green needles — but a second look shows just how wrong that impression is. The needles covering their trunks and branches are short, fleshy, and look more like rose-thorns than anything from a healthy conifer, and the trees themselves always look faintly sickly even when thriving.

That’s because they are, in a sense, feeding on everything around them: an occluding tree draws the life out of nearby plants, leaving a perfectly circular patch of sterile dirt beneath it, exactly as wide as its lowest branches reach (which, underground, is also exactly how far its roots reach). They spread through underground runners like fungus, and a mature tree will usually have new saplings sprouting at its perimeter within a few spins — fewer if it’s already crowded by other occluding trees nearby. Farmers despise them, for obvious reasons.

## Sapglass Tree

A slow-growing relative of the squid tree, found in drier ground than its swamp-dwelling cousin prefers. Where a squid tree’s sap darkens to ink on contact with air, sapglass sap does the opposite — it hardens into clear or faintly tinted beads that look uncannily like cut glass, and are collected and strung by jewelers and traders alike. The trees don’t take kindly to over-tapping, though; a sapglass tree drained too often, or too deep, will be dead within a few hundred spins, and a grove that’s been carefully tapped for generations is worth real money to whoever inherits it.

## Shard Trees

Shard trees grow like bamboo — close to a thumb’s-length a day — with palm-like trunks that are easy to carve and cut, making them a building and crafting staple. Their leaves are the giveaway: thin, diamond-shaped, about three centimeters long, with an amber sheen, and hard enough that they tinkle like glass when they brush against each other in a breeze.

That hardness is also the danger. In anything stronger than a gentle breeze, the leaves snap off easily and go flying — a sudden gust through a grove can send a stinging hail of glass-sharp shards in every direction, enough to draw blood through light clothing. The stronger the wind, the worse it gets, and anyone standing directly beneath a violently shaking trunk risks taking the worst of it full in the face. Clear-cutting a stand, by contrast, is one of the gentler things you can do to Anka’s landscape — they grow back fast enough that it barely counts as deforestation.

## Sleepshrubs

A low, unremarkable-looking shrub, easy to walk past without a second glance — which is exactly the danger. When disturbed (brushed against, stepped near, or otherwise startled), it releases a thin green smoke from somewhere in its own foliage, more exhaled than emitted, drifting rather than bursting. Breathing creatures caught in it don't so much fall unconscious as fall deeply, suddenly asleep — hours of it, sometimes the better part of a day, with no memory of the moment it happened. Forged aren't affected at all; the shrub has nothing to offer something that doesn't breathe.

It isn't aggressive. It doesn't hunt, doesn't pursue, doesn't do anything at all unless disturbed first — closer to a startled animal playing dead than a predator laying a trap. The real risk isn't the shrub itself but what finds a sleeping traveler first.

## Spider Trees

Often mistaken for a Yrrip at a glance, spider trees are a far less clever — and far more rooted — mimic of the same general shape. They can’t move from where they grow, which is the only real mercy about them; closer to a carnivorous plant than a true tree, a spider tree’s “body” is tough, woody, and shrugs off most ordinary blows, though fire burns through it readily and without much resistance.

A careless traveler who doesn’t notice the scattered belongings of previous victims nearby is the one most at risk — concealed limbs can grab and drag a target toward a knot in the trunk that serves as a kind of mouth, where the tree will slowly consume whatever it’s caught. It isn’t picky about what it grabs, but it is picky about what it eats: hexwood gear and other inedible items get spat back out without any ceremony at all.

## Squid Trees

Squid trees look something like swamp mangroves — twisted, visible root systems below a trunk three to four meters high, topped with a frond of palm-like leaves. Nested low among the roots of nearly every mature tree sits a single stark yellow tuber, large, waxy, and unmistakably eye-shaped — round, faintly domed, and unsettlingly capable of turning. It isn't sight, or isn't only sight — the tuber seems to track warmth, or motion, or both, rotating slowly and smoothly toward whatever's moving nearest it, the way a sunflower tracks the sun, except a sunflower doesn't make you feel hunted. A single tree is disquieting. A grove is worse: dozens of them, turning one after another as you pass, so the feeling of being watched doesn't fade the way it should once you're a few trees clear of the last one — something new has already picked you up.

It's a plant. It has no mouth, no evident means of attack, and does nothing on its own that could plausibly hurt a traveler who simply keeps walking. Nearly everyone badly hurt in squid-tree country was hurt by something else, drawn in by the tree's real defense — but a fair number of travelers never get that far, because a slow-turning yellow eye that seems to be watching you get closer is more than some nerves can stand, and the instinct to make it stop, however it gets stopped, is exactly the kind of violent disturbance the tree is built to answer.

Their real claim to fame, though, is what’s inside the trunk: a clear, sap-like fluid that rapidly turns to gas and hardens on contact with air, changing color to a deep blue or black in the process (the exact shade may depend on local soil). Anything caught in the resulting cloud is plunged into effective darkness, for better or worse. Usually only the immediate area around the tree is affected — but a sufficiently violent disturbance can also set off neighboring trees in sympathy, blanketing a much wider area in inky gloom. Predators in squid-tree country know this, and use the trees’ defensive bursts to cover ambushes of their own.

## Stoneroot

A slow, gnarled tree that sinks its roots deep into soil long soaked with titan blood, drawing up minerals until its heartwood becomes dense and stone-hard with age. With so little metal able to survive long in air thick with magefire dust, stoneroot timber has become one of the most reliable substitutes going — used for axles, tool heads, hinges, and anything else that needs to take a beating without wearing away to nothing. A mature stoneroot takes generations to reach its best wood, so old-growth stands are valuable and closely managed.

## Whipwood

Whipwood looks like nothing more than a humin-sized stalk with a cluster of short, spiky branches at the top — like a long-handled mace that’s been planted shaft-first in the ground. It tends to grow near black glass.

What makes it dangerous is the sheer amount of kinetic energy stored in that stalk. Bending one back is easy enough; releasing it sends the spiky top whipping forward with tremendous force, slamming into whatever’s in its path. Used against simple animals, or anyone not paying attention, a whipwood “trap” is a genuinely effective weapon — though most people with any experience in the wild recognize the danger the moment they see one bent back and tied off. Once set as a snare, a whipwood will hold that stored energy for several days before it finally relaxes.
