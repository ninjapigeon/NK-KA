## The Giant’s Graveyard

Also called the crawling cliff, or the crawling rocks — names that undersell just how unsettling the place is once you’re standing in front of it.

The Graveyard is a single sheer, pale cliff — locals call it the spine — roughly five kilometers wide and a hundred meters high, with broad, flat slabs of the same pale stone peeling away from its face at every angle. The image that sticks with most people, once they’ve seen it, is an enormous hardcover book lying open on its back: the cliff is the spine, and the slabs are its pages, caught mid-turn. Near the face, the pages crowd close together and stand nearly upright; further out, they lean more; further still, they lie flat on the ground, half-buried in ferns and moss. Each slab is enormous — causeway-scale — yet from a distance the whole cliff resolves gradually, the way a true horizon does, rather than announcing itself as a wall. About a kilometer out in any direction, the greenery gives way to bare rocky desert, and the desert is where most travelers first realize just how big the thing in front of them actually is.

The pages move. Not while anyone’s watching — campers who’ve tried to keep a slab in view report it happening the moment they look away, or fall asleep, or simply blink too long. Sometimes there’s a crack like thunder, and a new page has appeared near the spine; sometimes a smaller pop, followed by a crash, as a page far out from the spine finally falls flat. The cliff never seems to get any smaller, the field of fallen pages never seems to get any larger, and the ring of desert never expands — whatever’s happening here balances itself.

People have tried to live here anyway. Small folk occasionally build into or onto a standing page; goblins occasionally nest on one. Both eventually leave, one way or another — usually because the page they were living on chooses, without warning, to become a fallen one.

The cliff is the exposed root of something far larger — the same mountain-root that, ground down across an age, became the Anvil the Forged were made on. What’s left of it now sits half in Anka and half in the grey, which is the real source of everything strange here: the slabs “crawling” when unobserved, the thunderclap arrivals and departures, even the way the cliff resolves like a horizon instead of a wall. A Buhar carried here goes strange and unreliable. And anyone who can step sideways finds the crossing far rougher than it should be — the grey pulls here in a way it doesn’t anywhere else.
