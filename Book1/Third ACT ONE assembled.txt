
The last customer left when the Scar was about a hand-width from full dark, which meant
Klar and I had exactly long enough to clean up before the light went entirely and we'd be
doing it by firelight, which Klar didn't mind and I did.

"You don't have to stay," I told him, which was true, and also the kind of thing you say
when you want someone to stay because the alternative is doing it alone.

"I'm not in a hurry," he said, which was his way of saying he knew that, and was staying
anyway, without making anything of it. Klar was about 5,000 spins and already a better
cook than anyone who'd ever worked the inn before him, and he had the social instincts of
someone twice that age, which made him simultaneously easy to work with and slightly
unnerving.

We worked in the comfortable way of people who have done the same tasks in the same order
enough times that conversation isn't required. I wiped down the tables. He dealt with the
kitchen. The fire burned down to coals. Outside, through the shuttered window that faced
the square, I could hear the militia's boots on the packed earth — the patrol heading out,
which meant the Scar was almost fully across, which meant I had about thirty minutes before
I needed to be somewhere with a locked door between me and out there.

The greybeards called them Titanspawn. The militia called them problems. Most of us didn't
call them anything — you don't name the thing that took the Pell family, not so long ago,
you just make sure your ladder's up before the Scar finishes its crossing and you don't
think too hard about the sounds you sometimes hear from the other side of the wall. After
long enough, the not-thinking becomes its own kind of skill.

Our town was not remarkable. I had been told this, not unkindly, by everyone who had ever
passed through it, which was enough people to constitute a reliable sample. The trail it
sat on connected two larger settlements and so we got travellers, which meant we got trade,
which meant we had an inn at all. I had been working it for about 1,200 spins, which was roughly when Tondi decided I was old enough to be trusted with the closing routine. Before that I had been growing up in it — she had taken
me on at around 4,800 spins, when I was old enough to carry things without dropping them
and my father had stopped being someone you could count on to be anywhere in particular.

He was still alive, as far as anyone knew. He was just elsewhere.

I didn't think about that. I was good at not thinking about things, which is a skill that
gets undervalued.

"Done," Klar said from the kitchen doorway, untying his apron.

"Go on then." I waved him off without looking up. "Lock the back."

He did, and I heard his feet cross the yard and the tall gate clatter behind him,
and then it was just me and the last of the fire and the sound of the patrol fading around
the corner of the mill.

The last table was the one under the window, and that one was Kern's. He was the Kro who'd
been coming to the Notch longer than I'd been closing it up — a fixture, like the memorial
pillar: in most afternoons for a single cup he'd make last until the light moved, keeping his own counsel and his own calendar. He'd had that bow of his with him, the way he always did — a tall thing, taller than the man, carved
down its whole length in a pattern too fine to follow, worn pale and smooth where his shoulder rode it, with old red worked deep into the bindings the way the Kro put care into their fine work. A family piece, he'd told me once, when I was small enough to ask rude questions: older than the inn, older than him, and a Kro doesn't call a thing old just to be saying something.
I'd wiped around it a hundred times. It was handsome, and it was in my way, and that was the
whole of what it ever was to me. He'd taken it home hours back. I wiped his table down, and
that was the last of them.

I checked the lockbox in the floor — combination set, lever down, lid secured, the way Tondi had shown me the first time she'd trusted me to close up alone, treating the demonstration with the gravity of someone passing down something worth passing down. I straightened the chairs. I did a slow circuit of the room the way I always did,
checking nothing in particular, which was either a professional habit or a way of not going
home yet, and I was honest enough with myself to know it was probably the latter.

The inn was not large. Six tables, a bar, a stool in the corner as Tondi's mending station
during the day, a kitchen, and three rooms for rent above which were empty tonight. The
memorial pillar outside was visible through the gap in the shutters if you stood at the
right angle — knee-height, three-sided, the names of everyone who had ever died here since
the founding. Not a long list. We were a young town, as towns went. A few thousand spins
at most, probably less.

I stood at the gap in the shutters for a moment and looked at the pillar in the last of
the Scar's light.

A young town and a quiet one, I thought, which was either a comfort or a complaint
depending on the day. Tonight it felt like the latter, which happened sometimes, which I
had no particular solution for. I was about 6,000 spins old and I had been to Dunarth, spireward, once
with a trader caravan and that was the sum of my experience of anywhere that was not here.
I was not unhappy. I was not unhappy the way you are not hungry when you have not yet
smelled food — a condition that works fine until something changes.

Nothing was going to change. That was the thing about quiet towns.

I left the view by the shutter. I banked the fire. I got my coat from the peg by the door and
shrugged into it, and spun the combination lock on the front door, and stepped out into
the cooling dark just as the last sliver of Scar-light faded from the sky above and the
particular darkness of Anka settled in — the flat, total dark of the wound, rotating slowly overhead and shedding its black straight down. In the distance, you could still see the light shining on the Spire.

I stood outside by the gate for a moment, the way I always did, taking the temperature of out there before committing to it. The patrol was audible on the far side of the mill. A light
was on in the window above the carpenter's — old Sem working late, or just unwilling to
go to bed, which amounted to the same thing at his age. The boundary spikes at the
town's edge were invisible in the dark, which always struck me as a design flaw.

Nothing unusual. The regular version of dangerous, which in our town meant probably fine.

I had just stepped out from the gate and was three paces toward home when I heard the sound from the town entrance — not the patrol, something else, something that made two of the militia
call out in voices pitched slightly higher than usual, the way voices go when the body has
decided something before the mind has caught up. Then quiet. Then the particular kind of
talking that happens when people are deciding what to do about a thing rather than reacting
to it. Whatever it was, the militia had it in hand.

Probably nothing, I told myself, and I almost believed it, and I kept walking.

I had got perhaps halfway home, past the dark bulk of the mill, when I heard boots behind
me — quick, uneven, heavier on the left foot. Roan's gait. I'd have known it anywhere; he'd
favoured that leg since before either of us was old enough to know why.

"Tasha! Hold up—" His voice had a wobble in it that I didn't like. "Tasha, I need—"

I turned around.

Roan was coming up the path toward me at the kind of pace that is not quite running because
running would mean admitting something. And he was not alone. Beside him, keeping easy time
with a stride that did not match Roan's hurry, came a shape I had no ready category for —
tall, hooded, walking on something that did not sound like feet against the packed earth.

I was bad at leaving things alone. It was either a character flaw or the only interesting
thing about me, and standing there in the dark with my hand drifting toward my blade, I had
the brief and unwelcome thought that I was about to find out which.

---

They stopped a few paces short of me, which I appreciated, because it gave me a moment to
look at the thing without it looming, and looming was clearly something it could do without
trying.

Wooden limbs. Leather-woven, in the way of something designed to suggest muscle without
quite achieving it. A cloak that was doing an earnest but unsuccessful job of concealing
the wearer, mostly because the wearer had cloven hooves where feet should have been, and
the sole-less boots someone had put over them had long since worn through at the bottom.
And in the shadow of the cloak's hood, slightly off-centre, one eye. A hole in smooth
wood, lit from somewhere inside, glowing a steady blue. Not pointed at me when they stopped,
and then it was — the glow finding me, settling, brightening a fraction as it fixed in
place.

And a staff — or what I took for a staff, in the first half-second,
before the rest of it caught up. A tall shaft of dark, close-grained wood,
held point-down in one wooden hand the way a tired man leans on a walking
stick. Except no walking stick I had ever swept around the legs of carried
a hand's width of wide flat blade at its head, carved down its whole
length with a flourish finer than anything I'd seen come off a mortal
tool, the wood so dark and dense it might have been wet stone. A glaive.
Held grounded, point to the dirt, in the one posture a thing can hold a
polearm that does not read as about to use it — which somehow made it
worse, the courtesy of it, the way a big animal sitting still and watching
you is worse than one already moving. The cloak hid the hooves badly, and
the hood did not hide the eye at all, and the staff was not a staff, and
the whole of it gave the impression of a thing that had learned how to be
unalarming from someone describing it to it once, and gotten every single
detail a little, and terribly, wrong.

I became aware that my hand was on my blade.

I left it there.

"They wouldn't let it through the gate on its own," Roan said, in a rush, as if he'd been
holding the explanation in his mouth the whole way over. "Course they wouldn't. But it
asked — it talks, Tasha — it asked for Tondi by name, and Pek said bring it to whoever's at
the inn and let them sort it, so I—" He gestured, helplessly, at the entire situation. "So
I brought it. To whoever's at the inn. Which I was hoping would be Tondi."

"Tondi's gone to bed," I said.

"Yeah." Roan's face fell about as far as a face can fall. "I was afraid you'd say that."

I looked at the thing. The thing's eye was on me. The body and shoulders had not moved to track the eye —
the rest of it was as still as the mill behind us, which should have been reassuring and
was not.

"It wants to come in," Roan added, when the silence had gone on long enough to need
something put in it. "Into the inn, I mean. Not just the town. That's the — that's the part
I didn't want to decide on my own."

"I did ask," said the thing.

The voice arrived like the first stones of a rockslide — not loud, not sharp, but felt as
much as heard, something that happened in the chest before it reached the ears. I had not
been expecting it to speak. I was not sure what I had been expecting, but speaking was not
it.

I took my hand off my blade. Not because I had decided it was safe. Because I had decided
that if it wasn't safe, the blade wasn't going to be the relevant variable.

"All right," I said, mostly to buy myself a breath. Then, to Roan, because it was easier
than addressing the thing directly: "What did Tondi tell you to do, exactly? Her words."

Roan's brow furrowed with the effort of precise recall. "She said — if it's reasonable,
there's a condition. For letting it in. She said tell it to bring back the head of the
Subsurface Shredder, the one that's been working the trail anti-spinward. As proof it's
what it says it is." He glanced at the thing. "And she said bring the head to her. Not to
take its word."

"She said to bring the Shredder's head," I said, to the thing. "As proof. Do you have the
Shredder's head?"

"I do," it said.

Then it reached an arm around to its own back — not turning, not rotating at the shoulders
the way a person would, just the arm going where arms are not designed to go, moving at
angles that made something sympathetic happen in my own shoulder — and produced, from
somewhere behind itself, a Shredder's head. Which it held out, at arm's length, in a
manner that suggested it found the object neither interesting nor troubling.

I looked at the head. Squid-like, but 6 eyes, each pair a different size, and now clouded over. A pale coloration to its skin, beyond that of death, suggesting a mostly light-free existence while it was alive. It had a terrifying reputation as a titanspawn that ambushed the unwary. Currently, it had the appearance of an oversized, dried-out, dead fish. Funny how much death can change a thing.

I looked at Roan, who had the expression of a man who has just found a puppy and is
hoping no one will tell him to put it down.

I looked at the thing, which was patiently holding a severed head at arm's length in the
middle of the path as though that were a normal way to wait out a conversation.

"Put that away," I said. "Please. And — fine. Come back to the inn. I'm not deciding
anything out here in the dark."

The thing returned the head to wherever it kept such things, with that same wrong,
unhurried motion, and said nothing, which I was beginning to understand was its way of
agreeing.

So the three of us walked back the way Roan had come — me in front, because it seemed
important to be in front, the thing behind me with a tread that landed wrong on the packed
earth, and Roan bringing up the rear with the air of a man escorting a prisoner he is not
certain is actually his prisoner. The town was dark and shuttered around us. If anyone
watched from a window, they kept it to themselves.

At the inn I unlocked the gate but didn't open it, and turned to face the thing properly,
with the building at my back like that would help.

"Tondi's the one who set your condition," I said. "And Tondi's asleep, and I'm not waking
her for this. So here's where we are. You can't come in until she's seen that head and said
you're what you say you are. That's her rule, not mine, and I'm not clever enough to
overrule it tonight. But you can at least stay inside the gate. That's my call."

The thing's eye stayed on me, and the quality of its stillness shifted in some way I
couldn't have named precisely — not impatience, nothing so human as that. More like a
calculation completing.

"Then I will wait," it said, and stepped through the gate, and stood next to the inn's door, and became, as far as I could tell, immediately and completely stationary.

I looked at Roan.

Roan looked at the thing.

"You're the one who told it to come here," I said.

"Tondi told me to bring it. If it—"

"Tondi told you to bring the head. She didn't say anything about the rest of it."

Roan opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at the thing against the wall, which was
continuing to be stationary in the way of something that had decided this was the correct
place to be and had no strong feelings about how long that took to resolve.

I checked the lock. Combination set, numbers correct, the reassuring click of the hexwood
bolt sliding home. Then I looked at Roan, who still had that expression, and I gave him
the look that I hoped communicated not my problem before heading home.

Behind me, as I rounded the corner of the mill, I heard Roan say, at roughly a third of
his normal volume: "Do you need anything? Water, or—" and then stop, presumably having
remembered what he was talking to.

I did not hear the response, if there was one.

---

At home, I sat on the edge of my bed for a while without taking my coat off.

The thing had spoken. It had been polite. It had the Shredder's head, which meant Tondi's
test had been passed, or would be passed when Tondi was available to receive the result.
It had walked to a wall and stood against it and given every indication that it intended
to remain there until morning with the patience of something that found waiting neither
difficult nor notable.

None of this was, technically, alarming.

I took my coat off.

I told myself I was going to sleep, and was surprised to find, after a while, that I had.

---

I found out later what Third did while I was asleep.

He told me in pieces, the way he told most things — not reluctantly, but precisely, giving
me exactly what I asked for and waiting to see if I wanted more. He told me about the
burglar when I asked why he'd ended the night standing at the window instead of the door. He told me about the cold
when I mentioned the frost on the step that morning. He did not tell me about the rest of
it until much later, when I asked directly, and even then I had the sense that he was
leaving some of it out — not the important parts, just the parts he hadn't decided how to
describe yet.

What he did, as best as I can reconstruct it, was this:

He stood outside for a while, after Roan and I had gone, and watched the last of the
Scar's light finish going. He told me the dark didn't bother him, which I believed, and
that the quiet was preferable to the alternative, which I also believed, and that he found
himself wondering about the structural integrity of the inn's upper floor from the outside,
which I believed entirely because it was exactly the kind of thing Third would do in a
quiet moment when he had nothing else to occupy the part of him that was always, on some
level, assessing.

The town went to sleep around him. Lights went out. The patrol made its circuit and came
back and made it again. Third told me he could track each of them by the sound of their
boots — Roan's slightly heavier on the left foot, the way people favour an old injury
without knowing they're doing it; the other two by their pace, one quick, one slow,
neither varying much. He did not tell the militia he was there. They didn't ask.

Somewhere in the middle of the dark, a figure came along the side of the inn. Moving carefully. Not Roan's gait, not either of the other two.
Somebody who wanted to be quiet and was succeeding at about half of what they were
attempting.

Third, who had been standing beside the door, moved to the window between them — not blocking
it, simply standing where a person coming at the inn would have to account for him. Just
present.

The figure stopped.

There was a long pause during which, Third told me, he said nothing and did nothing, and
the figure presumably reassessed the situation. Then the figure left, at a pace somewhat
faster than the one it had arrived at.

He told me he did not think the figure was dangerous — probably opportunistic, probably
hoping the inn's lockbox had been left accessible, probably no worse than hungry and short
on Black. He said he might have handled it differently if he'd thought otherwise. I asked
him what differently would have looked like and he thought about it for a moment and said
he wasn't sure, which I appreciated for its honesty.

Then, he said, he spent the rest of the dark considering the memorial pillar.

Not looking at it — he'd read it before I'd arrived, all the names, the founding plaque
worn smooth at the edges. He was considering it from memory, which for Third was not a
figure of speech. He had the names of everyone who had ever died in this town stored
somewhere in whatever served him for a mind, and he was cross-referencing them against
what he knew of the region, the trail, the kinds of things that tended to happen to small
settlements within a certain range of certain other things.

I asked him what he concluded.

He said the town was not in immediate danger. That the kind of threat he was currently
occupied with tended toward specific targets rather than casual ones, and this town was
not specific enough to be a target yet. That yet was doing a lot of work in that sentence,
and he knew it, and I knew he knew it.

I asked him why he hadn't said any of this at the inn before I went home.

He said I hadn't asked.

I said that was technically true and also a very annoying answer.

He said he knew that too.

---

He stayed at the window the rest of the night. Not, he said, for any reason he could name
precisely. The night was the same. The patrol was the same. The town was the same. It had
simply seemed the right place to be when morning came, if morning was going to bring what
he thought it might.

I asked him what he thought morning was going to bring.

He said: someone willing to ask how soon.

---

When I came around the corner of the mill with my coat still half-buttoned and my eyes
not fully decided about being open, he was there. Something about the way he was standing
— not the stillness, which was always there, but the quality of it — made me stop walking
about a dozen paces out.

I stood there for a moment. He stood there for a moment.

His eye found me — that slow deliberate rotation, the glow settling, one lid dropping fractionally as it found me.

"Did you sleep?" I asked, because I was not yet awake enough for a more sophisticated
opening.

There was a bellows-wheeze, slow and considered, from somewhere inside the thing before the avalanche spoke. "I do not need sleep. However, during the dark I did advise someone that attempting a non-front-door entry during non-open hours would not be in their best interest. They then left."

I thought about that for a moment. My heart was doing what it had been doing since I'd
turned the corner and seen him — the specific rhythm of a body that hasn't yet caught up
to the decision the mind already made.

"If that's the truth," I said, "I think we can offer you something on the house. Or
whatever you need for—" I looked at his face, which had no mouth. "—nourishment."

"I require only to be indoors where I do not need to consider night attacks from
directions I am not watching." The eye didn't move from me. "Tondi was to arrange this.
It appears I have circumvented that requirement by standing watch outside your inn."

"You are," I said, "genuinely resourceful."

I walked the remaining dozen paces and stopped at the door. He came aside in the way
he had — the whole body moving as a single considered unit, not the unconscious shuffle
of someone making room. He chose to move. That was the thing about Third, I was already
beginning to understand. Everything was chosen.

I spun the combination lock and slid the door aside.

"Looks like it might get stony today," I said, which was true — the sky had that
particular flat quality that usually meant falling rocks by mid-morning. "You'd better
come in."

He followed me inside.

I started the morning routine — shutters, stove, stew selection — and did not think about
the fact that there was a Forged Titanspawn standing in the corner of my inn wearing
its worn cloak, and watching me with one blue eye.

I was not thinking about it very hard.

---

I'd got as far as selecting the vegetables for the day's stew when Tondi came down, and
that was when the morning stopped going the way mornings usually went.

I should explain about Tondi. She was the town's mender, which meant she stitched clothes
and stitched people and was, by a margin nobody disputed, the most capable person in the
settlement at both. She had grey hair that she wore loose and falling across her face,
which made eye contact difficult, which I had eventually understood was the entire point.
She did not touch money — she had a bucket, and you put your Black or your Teeth in the
bucket, and that was the transaction. Nobody knew why. Nobody asked. You did not ask Tondi
things. Tondi was a curmudgeon of such reliable and total commitment to the craft that the
town had simply organised itself around the assumption that she would be unpleasant and
correct, in roughly that order, about everything.

She had raised me, more or less, since I was old enough to carry things. I would not have
said she was warm. I would have said she was the most dependable fixed point in my life,
which is a different thing, and arguably better.

She came down the stairs from her room with the particular shuffle of someone who is awake
but has not yet decided to be, took three steps into the main room, and saw Third. The rocks were just starting to bounce off the roof, creating a loud plinking sound off the wood shingles.

I have heard Tondi raise her voice perhaps four times in my life. This was one of them.

"Gah! What in the name of the Titans—"

And then she stopped. The plinking continued, but abated slightly, as if waiting for our mender to gather herself.

That was the thing I would think about afterward, when I had time to think about things.
The scream was real — she was genuinely, fully startled, a grey-haired woman finding a
Titanspawn standing in the corner of her inn at first light. But the stopping was just as
real, and faster than it had any right to be. One breath of pure shock, and then something
behind her eyes simply went to work, sorting the impossible thing in the corner into
categories, weighing it, and arriving — visibly, in the space of about two heartbeats — at
a conclusion.

The conclusion was: this requires information, not panic.

"Tasha," she said, in a voice that had already climbed all the way back down to its normal
register. "Did you let that in here."

It was not really a question. I answered it anyway, from the kitchen doorway, with a
vegetable still in my hand.

"Morning, Tondi. Yes. It scared off a burglar last night, and it—" I gestured with the
vegetable, which I realised partway through was not helping. "It's got the Shredder's head.
The one Roan was supposed to ask about. So I figured I'd trust it with a chair."

Tondi looked at me. Then she looked at Third. Then she looked back at me with an expression
that communicated, with great economy, that we would be discussing the limits of my
judgement at a later date.

Third, to its credit, did the thing it always did when it became the subject of a room's
attention, which was nothing. It simply turned its head toward Tondi, and waited.

Then it produced the Shredder's head.

I had seen it do this the night before and it had unsettled me then. In the morning light,
with Tondi watching, it was somehow worse — the arm going around to the back without the
body turning, the angles wrong, the whole motion carrying the casual competence of
something that had been retrieving objects from impossible places for longer than the town
had existed. The head came out and was held forth at arm's length, a gruesome offering
between two intelligent beings, neither of whom was entirely sure what the correct etiquette
was.

"This was what you sought," Third said. The voice did the thing it did — arrived in the
chest before the ears. "As I understood it. If it is not sufficient, I will leave."

There was a silence then that I would not wish on anyone. Tondi standing very still. Third
holding a decapitated Shredder head at arm's length. Me in the doorway with a vegetable. The
fire not yet lit, the room cold, the light coming grey through the shutters I hadn't opened
yet. The Stony had passed.

Tondi closed her eyes for a breath. I watched her hands, which had balled into fists at her
sides, slowly uncurl. When she opened her eyes again, she had decided something, and Tondi
deciding something was a force roughly equivalent to weather.

"Thank you," she said, "for ridding us of that beast." A pause, during which she was clearly
choosing her next words with the care of someone disarming a trap. "I can see you're a
reasonable... being."

The word being cost her something. I could hear it. But she said it, which was Tondi all
over — she would give you the truth even when the truth was inconvenient to her own
prejudices, because the alternative was being wrong, and Tondi would rather do almost
anything than be wrong.

"That said," she went on, "some of the townsfolk—" She stopped, reconsidered, and corrected
herself with the flat honesty that was the closest thing she had to a personality trait.
"Most of the townsfolk will not understand. Would you mind setting yourself somewhere more
out of the centre of the room. And—" she looked at the cloak, which was doing its usual
poor job. "—covering yourself a bit more thoroughly. Scar's sake."

She did not wait for an answer. She walked to the corner she always sat in, the one with
the best light for mending, and she picked up the cup of water I put out for her every
morning, and she drank from it, and she almost visibly relaxed.

Third looked down at its cloak, considering the problem of how to cover itself better. This
required the use of an arm, so it set the Shredder's head down on the nearest table —
without any apparent concern for the fact that it was a decapitated head on a table where
people would later eat — and began trying to arrange the inadequate cloak into something
more adequate.

I put the vegetable down and went over to Tondi, not for conversation, which one did not
seek with Tondi, but because there was a tablecloth on the pile of her current projects that
was past saving, stained and fraying, not worth the Black it would take to mend.

"That old cloth," I said. "It's done, isn't it. Not worth fixing."

She didn't look up. She made a sound that I had learned to interpret, over many years, as
assent delivered grudgingly enough to preserve her dignity.

I took the cloth.

"Here," I said to Third, and draped it over its back and shoulders, on top of the failing
cloak. With the tablecloth added, and Third positioned away from the door and out of the
direct sightline of anyone walking in, you would have to get fairly close to notice the hooves, or the
wrongness of the arms, or the glaive it had stood point-down in the angle of the wall behind it — close enough that its hand could have found the shaft without looking — or the single blue glow where a face should have been.

Klar arrived for his shift just then, which was its own small mercy, because Klar arriving
meant the morning had to resume being a morning. I took the Shredder's head off the table
before he could see where it had been sitting and handed it to him.

"Customer brought us a trophy," I said. "Think you can do something with it?"

Klar's whole face changed. He had not yet looked up enough to register the Forged
Titanspawn sitting in the corner under a tablecloth, because he was only 5,100 spins and a Shredder
head was the most interesting thing that had happened to him in a tenday.

"A Shredder head? You don't even—" He was already turning toward the kitchen, already
planning. "Let me get a skewer."

And he was gone.

I looked at Third, in its corner, under its tablecloth, with its one blue eye, watching the
room with the patience of something that had decided this was an acceptable place to be and
had no particular feelings about how long it took.

I looked at Tondi, mending, her water at her elbow, behaving as though there were not a
Titanspawn in her inn.

Then I went and lit the stove, because the stew was not going to make itself, and because I
had apparently decided, somewhere in the last few hours, that this was simply how the
morning was going to go.

I did not examine that decision very closely either.

---

The day did what days do. Customers came for the stew and left having eaten it; the Scar
crawled its slow arc overhead; Tondi mended and grumbled and eventually went up to rest;
and through all of it the thing in the corner sat under its tablecloth and did not move,
until the light began to fail and the inn emptied to nothing and I found that I had run
out of reasons not to go and sit with it. So i brought my stew, just in case.

The tablecloth had done a passable job of hiding the worst of it, but up close, where I
was sitting, there was no pretending the thing across the corner table from me was
anything other than what it was. The glaive leaned in the corner at its shoulder, point on the floorboards, near enough to its hand that it never had to look for it. It had sat there through a whole day of customers and stew and Tondi's grumbling and had not once reached for it — but it had not let it out of reach, either, the way you keep a thing you have carried so long that setting it down would feel like setting down a hand. The wooden hand with its too-many joints resting on the table's edge. The faint sound, every so often, of something mechanical inside a chest
that didn't move. And the eye — one blue eye, set slightly off-centre in a smooth
featureless face, which had not so much looked at me as found me, the glow of it fixing
in place with a weight I could almost feel.

I'd served odder, maybe. Tondi, for one.

What I kept coming back to was the weapon. Slung across its back, under the failing cloak,
was a Bolter — a real one, a lightning-gun, the kind the militia talked about the way some
men talk about women they'll never meet. Ours had exactly none. And here was one riding the
back of a Titanspawn that fought with a curved wooden blade it kept in its hand, while the
Bolter stayed where it was, unslung, unfired, gathering the same dust as the rest of it.

"You don't use that," I said, before I'd decided to. I nodded at the weapon. "The Bolter.
You've had a blade in your hand twice since you got here and that thing hasn't come off your
back once."

For a moment I thought I'd asked the wrong thing, in the way you can feel a
wrong question land before anyone tells you it was wrong.

"No," it said. "I do not use it."

"Is it broken?"

"It functions."
A pause — not the kind where someone decides what to say, the kind where someone does
arithmetic on a sum they have done many times and still do not like the answer to. "It
belonged to Dorn. I carry it because putting it down would mean something I am not prepared
to mean yet."

I didn't say anything. There are sentences you don't step on.

"Dorn travelled with me," it went on, when it had decided I'd earned the rest. "He told
jokes. There was one he was fond of, concerning a Mender, a Crafter, and a Grask. I will not
attempt to reproduce it. The delivery was the entire substance of the thing, and the
delivery was his." The glow dimmed a fraction. "He was killed by a Subsurface Shredder. It
came up through the ground where we had made camp, and it was very fast, and I was not fast
enough, which is a thing I have had occasion to be more than once and have never learned to
care for."

"I'm sorry," I said.

"Thank you." Stated simply, the way it said most things — not performed, just received and
set down, like a toll paid. "There was a third of us, also. Adura. She was not taken by any
creature. She had been unwell for a long while, and one day the being unwell finished what
it had been doing slowly." The eye held on me. "I mention them because you should understand
what travelling with me has tended to cost the people who have done it. It would be dishonest
to ask without saying so."

I turned my cup in my hands. The stew had gone cold while we'd been talking and I hadn't
noticed. "So you're looking for new ones. Companions."

"I am."

"Here." I gestured vaguely at the inn, the town, the general smallness of everything.

"It seemed a reasonable location to begin. Frequently travelled. A mender in residence
suggests a degree of civilised infrastructure. And the memorial pillar outside is—" The
eye moved to the shuttered window facing the square — the glow sliding across the dark,
unhurried, the head not following — then back to me. "—modest. Which suggests the town has not yet suffered the kind of loss that makes people reluctant to risk further loss."

I stared at him. "You read the pillar."

"Before you arrived this morning. Yes."

I thought about that — Third standing in the pre-dawn, reading a knee-high plinth with
one blue eye while I'd been asleep, unbothered by the dark and apparently making
strategic assessments about grief thresholds. "That's very—" I tried to find the word.
"Methodical."

"I have found it useful to understand a place before asking something of it."

Before asking something of it. I looked at the tablecloth they'd draped over him — my
idea, technically, though Tondi had grunted permission — and tried to remember if I'd
ever thought of this town as something that could be asked things. I'd mostly thought of
it as somewhere I'd been born and hadn't left.

"What are you asking of it?" I said. "Specifically."

"For someone — or several someones — willing to travel with a Titanspawn toward a problem
that will likely become violent, with no guarantee of return, in service of preventing
something most people do not yet know is happening."

The stew was definitely cold. I pushed it aside. "And what is happening? The thing most
people don't know about."

The eye did not move from me. Whatever considering was happening, it happened behind that
fixed blue point. Something settled in the set of the wooden shoulders — a decision being
made about how much to say.

"Someone is attempting to resurrect a Titan," Third said. "I use the word attempting
deliberately. I do not know how far along this attempt is. I do not know, yet, what they
are collecting to achieve it. But I know they are collecting something, and I know that
whatever it is, it has already cost one town everything it had."

The inn was very quiet. Klar had stopped scraping.

"The Titan," I said carefully. "As in — one of the ones from the war."

"As in the ones from the war. Yes."

"The ones that—" I gestured at the general state of everything. The world with a wound
in its sky. The buildings with no ground-floor doors. The militia that drank before going
out at night.

"Yes," Third said again. "Those ones."

I sat with that for a moment. Then: "And you're looking for companions. To stop this."

"I am already committed to stopping this. I am looking for companions because—" A pause.
Longer than the arithmetic one, shorter than the one before he had said Adura's name. "Because
I have found, over a considerable period of time, that I am better at most things with
company. And because the last time something like this threatened everything living on
Anka, it required a coordinated effort by an entire people to address it." The eye was
steady on me. "That people no longer exists."

May they never be forgotten.

I didn't say it aloud. I didn't know why. It didn't feel like mine to say.

"How many people are you looking for?" I asked instead.

"I am not certain. Enough. People who can think as well as fight. People who are not so
committed to surviving that they become useless when survival seems unlikely."

"People like Roan," I said, before I'd decided to say it.

A long beat. "The militia member who found me at the gate."

"He's braver than he looks. And he doesn't drink for courage, which around here means
something."

"I had noted that," Third said. "Yes. People like Roan." The bellows-sound again —
slower this time, something gathering itself. "People like you, if you are willing."

I looked at my cold stew. I looked at the shuttered window. I thought about the pillar
outside, knee-high, the town's whole history in a dozen names, and I thought about the
fact that Third had stood in the dark reading it before the sun came up, before I'd even
opened the inn, already deciding whether we were worth asking.

"You said whoever's doing this already cost one town everything," I said.

"Yes."

"And you don't know how many more before you can stop them."

"No. I do not."

I nodded slowly. Outside, the patrol's footsteps had faded. The Scar was almost fully
across now — in another few minutes the light would go and the dark would come down.

"How soon?" I asked.

---

The traveller came in a little after midday, which was the wrong time for travellers. They
arrived in the evening, mostly, when the dark was coming and a roof seemed worth paying for.
A midday arrival meant someone who had been walking through the night, which meant someone
who had not felt safe stopping, which meant news, and news that came in on foot at the wrong
time of day was never the good kind.

He was Humin, middle-aged, with the particular grey-faced look of a man who has used up
everything he had and is running on the memory of it. There was a wound on his forearm,
bound with a strip of his own shirt, and the binding had gone stiff and dark. He came
through the door and stopped just inside it, the way people do when they have been moving
toward a destination for so long that arriving leaves them with nothing to do.

I got him into a chair before he fell into one. Klar brought water without being asked,
which was Klar. Tondi, in her corner, set down her mending and watched, which was Tondi.
And in the other corner, under his tablecloth, Third did not move at all, which by now I had
stopped finding remarkable.

The traveller drank. His hands shook. When he could speak, the first thing he said was the
name of a town I half-recognised — a settlement maybe two days spinward, on the same
trail as ours, the kind of place that was to us what we were to Dunarth. Smaller. Further
out. The next notch down the line.

"Gone," he said. "It's gone."

"Gone how," Tondi said, from her corner. Not unkind. Just direct, the way you are direct
with someone in shock, because vagueness is a thing they cannot afford and neither can you.

"Ravagers." He said the word and then had to stop and breathe around it. "A whole — there
were so many of them. They don't come in numbers like that. They don't. They come in twos
and threes, you lose a goat, maybe a man if he's careless. This was—" He looked at his bound
arm as though it belonged to someone else. "They came all at once. Like they'd been told to."

I felt the room go quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when several people have all
had the same thought and none of them want to be the one to say it.

"Like they'd been told to," Tondi repeated.

"I got out because I was anti-spinward at the mill when it started. I ran. I didn't—" His
voice did something then, and he didn't finish, and nobody made him. There are sentences you
let a man leave on the floor.

I refilled his water. It seemed like the only useful thing available to me.

"There's more," he said, after a while. "I didn't see this part. I heard it from a woman on
the trail, going the same way, faster than me." He wiped his mouth. "There was another
town. Spinward of the one that — of mine. The Ravagers were going for it too. And a
skybarge came down. The Porter."

I knew the Porter. Everyone on the trade did, even those of us who'd never once set foot up its
ramp — it was the only inn in the world that came to you. A whole tavern slung under a skybarge's
fans: Waj Hool's travelling house, food and drink and a dry bed for sale a hundred paces off the
ground, here twice a year if you were lucky and gone again on the wind. The whole town turned out
to watch it settle, the time it came; I'd spent more than one shift at Tondi's after that wishing
I worked a floor that moved. It was the last barge in the world I'd have looked to come down into
trouble instead of sailing wide of it.

Tondi's mending had not moved in some time.

"Came down right over the square," the traveller said. "In the middle of it. Fans going,
the whole — you could hear it to the next town, she said. And it just hung there. And the people
were running for it, getting underneath it, and it wouldn't open. The barge wouldn't drop
its ramp. Just hung there with the whole town screaming under it."

"Why wouldn't it open," Klar asked. He had not meant to ask. It had come out of him the way
questions come out of the young, before they have learned that some answers cost more than
the question is worth.

"She said there was someone arguing with Waj — with the master, down on the ground. Someone the
master didn't want to listen to. She couldn't hear what was said. But the ramp came down in
the end." He turned his cup in his hands, an echo of a gesture I recognised because it was
mine. "Most of them got up and in before the Ravagers reached the square. Most of them. The
woman said the thing that argued the ramp open was the last one up, and it walked up that
ramp backward, facing the Ravagers, the whole way."

I did not look at the corner where Third sat. I want that on the record. I had every reason
to and I did not, because some part of me had already done the arithmetic and did not want
to confirm the sum.

But I heard it. Across the room, from under the tablecloth, the bellows in Third's chest
drew breath — slow, the sound it made when something had landed with weight — and then
nothing. No comment. No movement. Just the one long mechanical inhalation and the stillness
after it, which was a different quality of stillness than the stillness before.

The traveller hadn't noticed. He was looking into his cup.

"There's one more thing," he said. "The part I keep — the part I can't—" He stopped.
Started again. "When I went back. To my town. After. To see if anyone — I went back."

"You shouldn't have," Tondi said, gently, which was a register I had heard from her perhaps
twice in my life.

"I know. I did anyway." He looked up, and his eyes found mine, and I wished they hadn't,
because there was something in them that I did not have anywhere to put. "There was
something there. Walking through it. Through what was left. Not a Ravager — the Ravagers
were gone, they'd moved on, they don't linger. This was after. Something walking through the
dead, slow, like it was — looking for something. Going building to building. And the
survivors, there were a few of us hiding, and it walked right past us. Right past. Didn't
look. Didn't care. Like we weren't worth the turning of its head."

The bellows again. Slow. Across the room. I kept my eyes on the traveller.

"What did it look like," I asked, and my voice came out steadier than I felt.

"I don't know. Cloaked. Hunched. There was a — a light on its back, blue-white, like a
bottled storm, like a whole rack of them, glowing through the cloak." He shuddered, and it
was not from the wound. "It found whatever it was looking for in the headman's house. I
heard it — there was a sound like a building being unmade. And then it left. Anti-spinward.
Away from everything. And it never once looked at any of us."

He went quiet then, all the way quiet, the quiet of a man who has finally set down the thing
he had been carrying and discovered that setting it down does not make it lighter, only
shared.

I gave him the room. I gave everyone the room. The fire ticked. Klar stood in the kitchen
doorway with a cloth in his hands, not using it. Tondi's mending lay in her lap.

And in the corner, Third stood up.

He did it slowly, the tablecloth sliding off his shoulders, and he did not bother to catch
it, which was the first time I had seen him fail to account for the cloak in any way. He
stood in the corner of the inn at midday with his one blue eye gone bright — brighter than
I had seen it, the glow of it hard and steady — and he looked at the traveller, and the
traveller looked up at the thing that had been sitting six feet away the whole time, and
made a sound.

He was not the only one to make it.

I had forgotten them the way you forget furniture — but the news had
drawn a few of the regulars in off the lane and then kept them, the way
bad news keeps people, and they had spent the last hour with their backs
to the corner, listening to a town die, while the corner sat six feet
behind their spines and did not breathe. Old Sem was there, and a carter
I half-knew, and a woman who'd come for stew and stayed for the worse
thing. And now the corner had stood up, and shrugged its cloth onto the
floor, and become a wooden monster with a burning eye, and it had been
there, that close, the entire time.

The room came apart the way a startled flock does, all at once and in
every direction. A bench went over backward. The carter was on his feet
with a paring knife he'd lifted off the bar without deciding to, holding
it the way you hold a thing you already understand will not help. Sem had
the door half open. The traveller had shoved himself clear off his chair
and fetched up against the wall, and the noise coming out of him had
stopped being a noise a man chooses to make.

"Sit."

Tondi did not stand. She didn't need to. She had a voice she kept for
about once a year, and she spent it now, and it landed on the room like a
dropped lid. The knife stopped. The door stopped.

"That," she said, into the ringing after, and she did not so much as
glance at the corner, "is the reason there's meat in your bowls and not a
Shredder on your road. It came in off the dark and laid the head of the
thing on my table — the Shredder that's been taking travellers off the
anti-spinward trail, the trail half of you walk to market. I examined it
myself. Klar's been feeding it to you since the pot got hot." She licked
her thread and went looking for her needle. "It has sat in that corner
behind a cloth, troubling no one, doing you the courtesy of not
frightening the very people who are now frightening themselves over a
tipped bench. So. Sit down and finish your bowls like grown folk, or take
your fright out the door and leave the rest of us to hear the man — but
either way you'll do it without the knife, Bevin, before you cut yourself
and I'm obliged to stitch you for nothing."

It was not kindness. Tondi had no use for kindness; she found it
sentimental. It was the thing she kept instead, which was worth more — the
flat, bottomless certainty of a woman who has decided a thing is true and
will not be argued off it by anybody's heartbeat. Bevin looked at the
knife as though it had got into his hand by witchcraft, and set it on the
bar. Sem let the door swing to. The woman sat, because Tondi had said
sit and there are people in every town who have been waiting their whole
lives for someone to tell them what to do in exactly that voice. Nobody
else quite managed it. But nobody ran, either, and I was starting to
learn that nobody-running was the whole of what you got to ask of people
the first time they met him.

Through all of it Third had not moved. He had not turned his head, had
not marked the knife or the door or the going-over of the bench; he had
stood with his one bright eye fixed on the traveller and the traveller
alone, as untroubled by a room deciding whether to kill him as the Scar
is by the things it passes over. He had been waiting, I understood, for
the noise to finish — not out of patience, but because the noise had
nothing to do with the single thing he had stood up to learn.

"Anti-spinward," Third said. The voice filled the room the way it always did, but there was
something underneath it now, something I had no name for and was not sure Third did either.
"You are certain. Anti-spinward."

The traveller could only nod his frightened head.

Third turned his head — the head, the whole head — toward me. The eye was very bright.

"You asked how soon," he said.

I had. The evening before, at the corner table, I had asked exactly that, and he had not
answered, and I had gone home thinking I'd have days to decide whether I'd meant it.

"Yes," I said. "I did."

"Now," Third said. "It is now."

---

We got the traveller into one of the rooms upstairs, and he was asleep before Klar had
finished setting down the second cup of water, the deep instant sleep of a body that has
been refusing itself for too long. Tondi had looked at the wound on his arm and made the
face she made when something was going to need more attention than it was going to get, and
then she'd dressed it properly anyway, because that was the difference between Tondi and her
reputation.

When she came back down, the inn had emptied of its few midday customers. It was just the
four of us. Me. Tondi in her corner. Roan, who had come in off his rest and who had been
standing by the door for the better part of an hour with the expression of a man who has
not been invited to sit but does not want to leave. And Third, who had put the tablecloth
back on without being asked, and who now sat at the corner table with his one blue eye on
the middle distance, waiting, the way he waited for everything.

"All right," I said, and sat down across from him. "You said now. So tell us. All of it."

The bellows drew. Once. Then he began.

"I have been following this for some time," Third said. "Long enough to be certain it is
not chance. Towns along the trails — small ones, mostly, the kind that do not appear on a
trader's map and are not missed when they cease to appear anywhere at all. Each one struck.
Each one in the same manner. Ravagers in numbers they do not naturally gather in, directed
against the settlement all at once. And then, after, something walking through the
wreckage. Searching. Taking one thing and leaving everything else, including the survivors,
untouched."

"The thing the traveller saw," Roan said, from the door.

"Yes."

"With the lights on its back."

"Yes." Third's eye moved to Roan — the glow sliding across the room, unhurried, the head following. "Magefire. A great many bottles of it, more than I have ever seen one body
carry. I do not know why it needs so much. I know that it does."

I thought about the traveller's words — a bottled storm, a whole rack of them — and I
thought about how Third had gone still when he heard it, and I made myself ask the question
I had been not-asking since midday.

"You were there," I said. "At the town with the Skybarge. You're the one who argued the
ramp open."

The inn was very quiet. Roan looked at me, then at Third.

"Yes," Third said. No weight on it. No reluctance. He stated it the way he stated the
hour. "The master of the barge — a Hin called Waj Hool — did not wish to descend into a
Ravager assault. This was a reasonable position. I persuaded him that it was less
reasonable than the alternative." A pause. "I was the last aboard. I prefer to keep a
Ravager pack in front of me where I can account for it."

"You walked up backward," Roan said, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard
from him before. Not quite awe. Closer to recognition.

"It is simply sound practice," Third said, which I was learning was the thing he said when
the truer answer was something he did not have words for.

Tondi, in her corner, had not picked her mending back up. "You saved a town," she said.
"And you did not stay. You came here instead."

"I saved the people of a town. The town itself is gone." The eye moved to her. "And I came
here because the thing doing this moved anti-spinward, and anti-spinward of that town, on
the trail it is following, is here. And then beyond here. I did not come to your town by
accident, mender. I came because I believe it is in the path."

I felt that land in my stomach, cold and specific. "You think it's coming here."

"I think it is going somewhere, and that the line it is travelling passes near enough to
here that I would not wish to be elsewhere when it does." He let that sit a moment. "But
your town is not the destination. Of that I am reasonably certain. Your town has nothing it
wants."

"And what does it want?" Tondi asked. "You keep saying a thing. The thing takes one object
and leaves the rest. What is the object?"

This was the question. I could feel it was the question, the way you can feel the weight
of a room turn toward a single point. Third was quiet for long enough that I thought,
again, he might not answer. Then the bellows drew a second time — the second, I noted, the
last one I would get from him tonight — and he set both his bifurcated hands flat on the
table, which I had never seen him do, and which had the effect of making the wooden parody
of a humin look, for a moment, like a man gathering himself to say a hard thing plainly.

"You know that the Titans made the world," he said. "This is told to children. It is also
true, which is rarer than you would think for a thing told to children." The eye dimmed
slightly — consideration, not grief. "What the children's telling leaves out is how. The
Titans did not make the world by wishing it. They made it the way anything is made. With
tools. With instruments. Things to cut with, and shape with, and bind one thing to
another. Vast things, suited to vast hands."

"Tools," Roan repeated.

"When the Titans fell, the tools did not fall with them. They remained. They are scattered
across all of Anka, and most of them are not recognised for what they are. A piece of titanic forge tongs, used as a shield; a titanic bowstring used as support for a bridge across a chasm; three gears that placed in proximity to each other begin rotating without external power. People walk past the instruments of creation every day and call them
landmarks, or curiosities, or hauntings, and they are not wrong to, because the truth is
not available to them and the landmark serves them better." The eye came back to me, bright
again. "But someone has learned to recognise them. And someone is gathering them."

I had grown up in a world with a wound in its sky and doors on the second floor and a
spire on the horizon that everyone navigated by and no one questioned. I had never once
thought of any of it as a thing somebody made. It had simply always been the shape of
things. To have Third say it plainly — that the spire was a tool, that the world was a
workshop someone had walked away from — did something to the floor of my understanding that
I did not enjoy.

"Why," I said. "Why gather them. What do you do with a — with the tools that made the
world?"

"That," Third said, "is what I do not know. Not with certainty." And I believed him,
because Third did not pretend to certainties he lacked, which was rarer and more valuable
than I had words for at the time. "But I have a fear. The tools that made a thing are the tools that can make it whole again — not mend it, the way you’d mend a chair, but make it, entire, a second time, out of whatever is left of it. And the largest thing that was ever unmade—”
“Was a Titan,” Tondi said.
“They made the world… They made me, too, in their way — I am one of their tools… I watched it come apart: not into a body you could bury, but into red dust, and into something else — something that went sideways out of the world, and did not entirely leave it… It scatters. And if someone is gathering the tools of creation… whether they mean to gather a fallen Titan back — to make it whole out of its dust and its absence, and call it down off the far side of the world into this one. To undo the one thing the Virek spent an entire people to do.”

Nobody said anything.

The fire ticked. Upstairs, faintly, the traveller turned over in his sleep and was quiet again.

"You don't know that's what it's doing," I said, finally. I don't know why I said it.
Maybe because someone had to put a hand against the size of the thing. "You said yourself.
You don't know."

"I do not," Third agreed. "I may be wrong. I hope to be wrong. I have been wrong before,
about smaller things, and the relief was considerable." The eye found me. "But I have
learned that when I cannot rule out the worst version of a thing, it is unwise to plan for
the comfortable one. So I am going to find whoever is doing this. And I am going to learn
what they want. And if it is what I fear, I am going to stop them, because the last time
this was necessary it required an entire people, and that people is gone, and so it appears
the task has fallen to whoever is still standing near it." A pause. "Which, at present,
is me."

"And us," Roan said.

He said it from the door, and then looked faintly astonished that he had said it, the way
you look when a thing you had been deciding turns out to have already been decided somewhere
below the place where you do your deciding.

Third turned his head toward him. The whole head.

"That is not a small thing to offer," Third said. "I would have you understand what you are
offering before you offer it."

"I heard you," Roan said. "Towns that don't make the maps. People walked past like they
weren't worth turning a head for. I'm militia in a town on a trail. If that thing's path
runs through here, then standing at the gate with a spear isn't going to be the brave
choice. It's just going to be the choice where I die at the gate." He swallowed. "I'd
rather go meet it as someone who chose to. With someone who knows what it is."

I looked at Roan, who did not drink for courage, and who had just talked himself into the
end of the world with his back against the inn's door, and I understood that whatever
happened next, I was not going to be able to watch it from here. That had stopped being
available to me somewhere around the word now.

"How soon," I said, for the second time in two days. But this time it wasn't really a
question about Third's schedule.

Third's eye came to me, and the glow did the thing I would come to know as close as he
came to gentleness.

"That," he said, "is up to you. But I would leave at first light."

---

I closed the inn that night the way I'd closed it a thousand times, except that I was aware
of doing it, which was the difference between a thing you do and a thing you are doing for
the last time.

I wiped the tables that did not need wiping. I straightened the chairs. I checked the
lockbox in the floor with the day's Black earned — combination set, lever down, lid secured — and I thought about the
traveller upstairs, and the town two days spinward that had a lockbox and a memorial
pillar and a militia and a closing routine just like this one, right up until it didn't have
anything at all. And then I made myself stop thinking about it, because that was a skill I
had and there was no sense owning a tool you refused to use.

Tondi was in her corner. She had not gone up to bed, which she always did before I finished,
which meant she was staying down on purpose, which meant she had something to say and was
working her way around to the angle she wanted to say it from. I let her work. You did not
hurry Tondi. Hurrying Tondi was how you got the short version, and the short version was
always worse.

Klar had gone home an hour ago. Kanot had come by earlier — Kanot, who had wanted my job
since before I'd realised it was a job a person could want, and who had said yes before
Tondi finished asking, and who had looked at me with an expression caught between sympathy
and the poorly hidden delight of someone inheriting a thing they'd coveted. I hadn't minded.
It is easier to leave a place when you know the place will be fine. That is, I think, why
Tondi had arranged it so fast. Not to replace me. To make it easy.

"You've packed," she said. Not a question.

"Roan helped me work out what to bring. Third corrected most of what Roan suggested." I
hung the last cloth on its hook. "Apparently I was going to bring far too much of the wrong
things and not nearly enough of the right ones, which Third says is the most common way the
inexperienced die. Not the monsters. The packing."

"He's not wrong." Tondi's needle went in and out of whatever she was mending, the rhythm of
it unbroken, the way it had been unbroken through every important conversation of my life.
"Cheerful, that one."

"He grows on you."

"Most things that grow on you should be scraped off." But there was no heat in it. She'd
made up her mind about Third somewhere around the second time he'd produced a severed head
in her inn without disturbing the room, and Tondi's made-up mind was the most fixed object
in the settlement, the spire included.

I finished the room. There was nothing left to do, which meant there was nothing left to do
but the thing we were both not doing.

I sat down across from her. She did not look up. The hair across her face did its work, the
way it always had — Tondi conducted the important conversations of her life from behind a
grey curtain, so that you could never be entirely sure she was having them.

"I could ask you to come," I said. "You'd be useful. You're more use than Roan and me put
together."

"You could ask." The needle did not pause. "I'd say no. So don't, and we'll both save the
breath."

"Why?"

It was, I realised as I said it, the first time in my life I had asked Tondi to explain
herself. The needle stopped. Just for a moment. Then it went on.

"Because somebody who knows how to fix things has to be where the people are," she said.
"Your wooden friend is walking toward the trouble. Good. Somebody should. But there'll be
people here when you're gone, the same as there were before you came, and they tear their
clothes and split their hands and take fevers, and they need somebody who stays." She pulled
the thread taut. "Brave is going. I've nothing against brave. But somebody has to be the
thing you come back to. And I have always been better at staying than at almost anything
else."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't a safe thing to say.

She set the mending down — which she did not do, which I had perhaps seen her do twice — and
reached into the basket at her side, and came up with a small roll of waxed cloth. She put
it on the table between us and pushed it across with two fingers, the way she pushed
everything she didn't want to be thanked for.

"Needles," she said. "Three. Hexwood, anointed proper — not the bone kind that snaps when
you breathe on it. And thread, the waxed gut, it'll hold through wet. You'll tear something
out there — a pack strap, a coat, your own skin, doesn't matter — and there won't be a
mender, because the mender will be here, being the thing you come back to." She still hadn't
looked up. "I'd rather you didn't bleed out because you lacked the tools to close a seam. It
would reflect poorly on me."

I picked up the roll. It weighed almost nothing. Hexwood needles were not nothing — anointing
wood that fine took a Crafter's whole patience and a measure of titan-blood besides, and a
mender's needles were the kind of thing that got named in wills, handed down, guarded like
teeth. She would have had these longer than she'd had me.

"Tondi—"

"Don't." The needle was back in her hand; the curtain of hair had closed again. "I mean it.
Whatever you're winding up to say. Don't. You'll do it badly and then we'll both have to
remember it."

So I didn't.

I put the needles in my coat, the inside pocket, the one over the chest, where you keep the
thing you cannot afford to lose. And I stood up, because Roan was going to be at the door
before first light and I still had not slept, and standing up was the only way the night was
going to agree to end.

At the door I stopped. I couldn't help it. I turned around.

"Tondi."

She was mending. She did not look up. But the needle had stopped, and stayed stopped, which
for Tondi was the same as another woman crossing a room with her arms open.

"Don't come looking for work here when you get back," she said, to the shirt in her lap.
"Kanot's got your job, and I won't put a good worker out to take back one who ran off chasing
Titanspawn across the world. You hear me? There's no job here for you."

When you get back. Not if. She had built the whole gruff little speech around a word she
would have died before saying outright, and she had handed it to me anyway, wrapped in an
insult so I could carry it without either of us having to look at it directly.

"I hear you," I said.

"Good." The needle started again. "Go on, then. Mind the seam on that coat, the left
shoulder's going. I'd have got to it if you'd given me another day."

"I know."

"Go."

I went.

Roan was at the door, as it turned out, just lifting his hand to knock, with the particular
look of a man who had also not slept and had decided that arriving early was a way of being
brave about it. Behind him the sky had that first grey thinning at the edge of the Scar that
meant the dark was an hour from breaking.

I did not look back at the inn as we walked toward the gate. I want that on the record too.
I had looked back once already, at the only thing in there worth looking back at, and she
had told me to go, and I was not going to spoil the single clean thing either of us had
managed by doing it twice.

But I put my hand flat against my chest, over the inside pocket, where the needles were.

Just to be sure they were still there.

They were.

---

We left at first light — the Scar's edge going from black to charcoal to the flat grey that passes for morning, the change creeping across the sky the way water creeps across a floor, without any particular moment
you could point to and call the start of the day.

The gate was a different thing in the light. At night it had been a threat held off by
spikes; now it was just a gap in a wall, with Pek leaning on the frame the way the militia
always leaned on things, and the packed earth of the trail running out through it toward a
horizon I had looked at my whole life and never once walked into.

There were not many to see us off. That suited everyone. A Forged leaving with two of the
town's own was not the kind of thing you gathered to celebrate; it was the kind of thing
you watched from a window with the shutter open just enough. I could feel the town doing
exactly that around us, the way you can feel a room you've turned your back on.

Third stood a little apart, as he always did, his cloak doing its inadequate work and his
one eye already turned toward the trail, toward the part of the morning that had not
arrived yet. He had done this before. I understood that without being told — that for Third
a gate was a gate, and a leaving was a leaving, and he had crossed more of both than there
were names on every memorial pillar in the world. He was waiting for us to be finished
being people about it.

Klar came running up just as Pek pushed the gate the rest of the way open.

"You almost left," he said, accusingly, out of breath, shoving a wrapped bundle into my
arms. It was heavier than it looked and warm at the bottom. "Travel bread, the dense kind,
it keeps. And the rest of the Shredder, smoked — don't make that face, it's good, I
brined it. And there's a twist of the blue salt in the middle, don't lose it, that's the
expensive one." He stopped, having run out of inventory, and then out of the thing under
the inventory, which was the part he didn't have words for. "Just. Eat properly. He won't
remind you." He jerked his head at Third. "He doesn't eat."

"I'll eat properly."

"You won't, but I tried." He stepped back, suddenly awkward, five thousand spins old again instead of the
competent thing he became around a stove. "Bring the salt twist back if you don't use it.
It's really expensive."

"I'll bring it back."

It was the second time in two mornings someone had handed me a thing I was meant to bring
back, and I was beginning to understand that this was how the people who couldn't come with
you said the thing they couldn't say: they gave you an errand. A reason shaped like an
object. Come back, because the needles are mine. Come back, because the salt is expensive.
Nobody said come back. Everybody said it.

Then we went through the gate.

I had expected it to feel like something. A line crossed, a door shut, some interior click
of before and after. It didn't. It felt like walking, which is all it was — the spikes
sliding past on either side, sharpened logs I'd seen ten thousand times and never from this
angle, and then the open trail, and then the town was behind us and the morning was in
front of us and my legs were just doing the ordinary thing legs do.

The Spire was on the horizon where it always was. From the town you mostly saw it over the
rooftops, a thin dark line you navigated by without thinking, the way you navigate by a
thing you've decided is permanent. Out here, with nothing between us and it but a great deal
of distance, I could see the gold catch at its tip where the Scar's grey light found it.
Spireward was that way. Everything was measured from it. I had known that since before I
could walk and had never once had a reason to care, and now I was walking across the actual
world it stood in the middle of, and the knowing turned over in my chest into something that
felt strange and large and not entirely comfortable.

We walked. Roan beside me, Third a few paces ahead at a pace he'd clearly chosen to be
followable. The trail rose toward a low hill — the first hill, the one that from town was
just the edge of the world you could see, the place the trail went to disappear.

I knew that when we crested it, the town would go behind us. The pillar, the inn, the gatehouse,
all of it, dropping below the line of the land like a coin into a pocket.

I did not look back.

So I kept my eyes on Third's back and the gold on the horizon and the dense warm weight of
Klar's bundle in my arms, and I walked over the hill and did not turn around, and I was, I
think, a little proud of myself for it.

Roan looked back.

I saw him do it — at the crest, just before the town would have gone — a quick turn of the
head, the way you check whether someone is watching you leave. I knew the look. I'd worn it.
It was the look of a person who needs the leaving to have been witnessed, because a brave
thing done where no one sees it doesn't yet feel, to a certain kind of young man, entirely
real.

I didn't know yet whether anyone had been at the gate to see him. I didn't ask. It seemed
like the kind of thing a person should be allowed to keep to himself, at least at the start,
before the road taught him what I suspected it was going to teach him — that the looking back
stops mattering somewhere out there, and that the not-needing-to is a thing you earn rather
than decide.

Then the hill was behind us too, and the town with it, and there was only the trail and the
grey morning and the gold pin of the Spire holding the whole flat world in place.

Some part of me had spent my whole life believing nothing would ever change in a quiet town.
That part had been right, in its way. The town hadn't changed. The town was exactly where I'd
left it, doing exactly what it had always done, and would go on doing it without me, with
Kanot wiping my tables and Tondi mending its torn shoulders and Klar feeding whoever was
hungry.

It was just that I was no longer in it.

"How far to the first stop?" I asked, because the silence had gone on long enough and
because I had discovered that I did not want to walk inside my own head any further just
then.

"Four days to the causeway," Third said, without turning. "If the weather holds and you
keep up." A pause. The glow of his eye angled back at me, just slightly, the head not
following. "You will keep up. You are already doing better than most."

"That's almost encouraging."

"It was meant to be entirely encouraging. I am told I lack the range."

Roan laughed — surprised out of him, the first real laugh since the gate — and Third's eye
held on us a moment longer, bright in the grey light, before turning back to the trail.

We walked on toward the causeway, and the Spire, and the thing at the end of it that none
of us yet understood, and behind us the town did not change at all.
