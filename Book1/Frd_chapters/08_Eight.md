# Eight

I closed the inn that night the way I'd closed it a thousand times, except that I was aware of doing it, which was the difference between a thing you do and a thing you are doing for the last time.

I wiped the tables that did not need wiping, feeling their time worn smooth tops for possibly the last time. I straightened the chairs, brushing the marks off the packed earth floor as I went. I checked the lockbox in the floor, the day's Black inside it — combination set, lever down, lid secured — and I thought about the traveller upstairs, and the town two days spinward that had a lockbox and a memorial pillar and a militia and a closing routine just like this one, right up until it didn't have anything at all. And then I made myself stop thinking about it, because that was a skill I had and there was no sense owning a tool you refused to use.

Tondi was in her corner. She had not gone up to bed, which she always did before I finished, which meant she was staying down on purpose, which meant she had something to say and was working her way around to the angle she wanted to say it from. I let her work. You did not hurry Tondi. Hurrying Tondi was how you got the short version, and the short version was always worse.

Klar had gone home an hour ago. Kanot had come by earlier — Kanot, who had wanted my job since before I'd realised it was a job a person could want, and who had said yes before Tondi finished asking, and who had looked at me with an expression caught between sympathy and the poorly hidden delight of someone inheriting a thing they'd coveted. I hadn't minded. It is easier to leave a place when you know the place will be fine. That is, I think, why Tondi had arranged it so fast. Not to replace me. To make it easy.

"You've packed," she said. Not a question.

"Roan helped me work out what to bring. Third corrected most of what Roan suggested." I hung the last cloth on its hook. "Apparently I was going to bring far too much of the wrong things and not nearly enough of the right ones, which Third says is the most common way the inexperienced die. Not the monsters. The packing."

"He's not wrong." Tondi's needle went in and out of whatever she was mending, the rhythm of it unbroken, the way it had been unbroken through every important conversation of my life. "Cheerful, that one."

"He grows on you."

"Most things that grow on you should be scraped off." But there was no heat in it. She'd made up her mind about Third somewhere around the second time he'd produced a severed head in her inn without disturbing the room, and Tondi's made-up mind was the most fixed object in the settlement, the Spire included.

I finished the room. There was nothing left to do, which meant there was nothing left to do but the thing we were both not doing.

I sat down across from her. She did not look up. The hair across her face did its work, the way it always had — Tondi conducted the important conversations of her life from behind a grey curtain, so that you could never be entirely sure she was having them.

"I could ask you to come," I said. "You'd be useful. You're more use than Roan and me put together."

"You could ask." The needle did not pause. "I'd say no. So don't, and we'll both save the breath."

"Why?"

It was, I realised as I said it, the first time in my life I had asked Tondi to explain herself. The needle stopped. Just for a moment. Then it went on.

"Because somebody who knows how to fix things has to be where the people are," she said. "Your wooden friend is walking toward the trouble. Good. Somebody should. But there'll be people here when you're gone, the same as there were before you came, and they tear their clothes and split their hands and take fevers, and they need somebody who stays." She pulled the thread taut. "Brave is going. I've nothing against brave. But somebody has to be the thing you come back to. And I have always been better at staying than at almost anything else."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't a safe thing to say.

She set the mending down — which I had perhaps seen her do twice — and reached into the basket at her side, and came up with a small roll of waxed cloth. She put it on the table between us and pushed it across with two fingers, the way she pushed everything she didn't want to be thanked for.

"Needles," she said. "Three. Hexwood, anointed proper — not the bone kind that snaps when you breathe on it. And thread, the waxed gut, it'll hold through wet. You'll tear something out there — a pack strap, a coat, your own skin, doesn't matter — and there won't be a mender, because the mender will be here, being the thing you come back to." She still hadn't looked up. "I'd rather you didn't bleed out because you lacked the tools to close a seam. It would reflect poorly on me."

I picked up the roll. It weighed almost nothing. Hexwood needles were not nothing — anointing wood that fine took a Crafter's whole patience and a measure of titan-blood besides, and a mender's needles were the kind of thing that got named in wills, handed down, guarded like teeth. She would have had these longer than she'd employed me.

"Tondi—"

"Don't." The needle was back in her hand; the curtain of hair had closed again. "I mean it. Whatever you're winding up to say. Don't. You'll do it badly and then we'll both have to remember it."

So I didn't.

I put the needles in my coat, the inside pocket, the one over the chest, where you keep the thing you cannot afford to lose. And I stood up, because Roan was going to be at the door before first light and I still had not slept, and standing up was the only way the night was going to agree to end.

At the door I stopped. I couldn't help it. I turned around.

"Tondi."

She was mending. She did not look up. But the needle had stopped, and stayed stopped, which for Tondi was the same as another woman crossing a room with her arms open.

"Don't come looking for work here when you get back," she said, to the shirt in her lap. "Kanot's got your job, and I won't put a good worker out to take back one who ran off chasing Titanspawn across the world. You hear me? There's no job here for you."

When you get back. Not if. She had built the whole gruff little speech around a word she would have died before saying outright, and she had handed it to me anyway, wrapped in an insult so I could carry it without either of us having to look at it directly.

"I hear you," I said.

"Good." The needle started again. "Go on, then. Mind the seam on that coat, the left shoulder's going. I'd have got to it if you'd given me another day."

"I know."

"Go."

I went.

Roan was at the door, as it turned out, just lifting his hand to knock, with the particular look of a man who had also not slept and had decided that arriving early was a way of being brave about it. Behind him the sky had that first grey thinning at the edge of the Scar that meant the dark was an hour from breaking.

I did not look back at the inn as we walked toward the gate. I had looked back once already, at the only thing in there worth looking back at, and she had told me to go, and I was not going to spoil the single clean thing either of us had managed by doing it twice.

But I put my hand flat against my chest, over the inside pocket, where the needles were.

Just to be sure they were still there.

They were.

We left at first light — the Scar's edge going from black to charcoal to the flat grey that passes for morning; creeping across the sky like water darkening a dirt floor, without any particular moment you could point to and call the start of the day.
