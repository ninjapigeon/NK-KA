# Nineteen

Third changed after the boundary stone.

I didn't have the why of it — I thought it was the question we'd asked, the Kro and the Ork, some old soldier's wound I'd blundered a finger into — but the shape of the change I could see plainly enough. He walked faster. He stopped asking whether we needed to rest and started telling us when we would. He spent the night watches further out, and came back from them later, and twice I woke to find him not at the fire at all and then there at the edge of the dark before I'd finished being afraid, as if he'd been somewhere and come back, lessened, the way he came back lessened from the carts.

And he started drawing in the dirt.

He'd squat at the cold morning fire and lay out the country with a stick — here the town that died, here the road, here the line the collector had walked through it, dead straight, and then he'd extend the line with the stick, on and on, the way you'd extend any straight thing, and he'd look at where it went.

"He does not wander," Third said, the fourth morning. "He is told where each one lies, and he goes to it in a straight line, and the next will be here." The stick stabbed a point ahead of us and anti-spinward, up in country that rose toward the rim. "A high place. Old. There has been a keeping there a long time — I have heard of it, the way you hear of a thing for three hundred — for a very long time, without ever needing to go." The eye came up to us. "If we go hard, we reach it before he does. And then, for once, we are not reading what he has done. We are standing between him and the next thing he wants."

"And then what," Mathen said. Not a challenge. A storyteller making sure he had the stakes right before he committed them to memory.

Third looked at the line in the dirt for a moment.

"Then we find out what he is," he said. "I have been chasing a hole in the world the shape of the things it has taken. I would like to see the thing that fits the hole."

He scuffed the map out with his hoof, and we went hard.

We went hard into rising country, and the country answered, the second afternoon, by throwing rocks at us out of a clear sky. There was no cloud. That was the part I could not make peace with — the Spire was shining, the day was fine, and then a pebble cracked off the trail at my feet, and another, and Third said one word, "stony," and put us at a dead run for an overhang he'd already marked without telling anyone he was marking it. By the time we reached it the air was full of them, stones the size of knuckles and then the size of fists falling out of nothing at all, hammering the grass flat, hammering Roan's other shoulder hard enough to drop him before Mathen dragged him in under the rock. They say it's the glutton's teeth, Mathen told me, hunched beside me while the world cracked and rattled outside our little shelter — that the broken teeth of one of the Titans are scattered out ahead of us in fields, and that the sky over that country never quite forgives it, and drops stone the way other skies drop rain, for no reason and to no schedule and on whoever happens to be under it. It lasted the length of a short tantrum and then simply stopped, the last few stones pattering down into a silence that rang, and we crawled out onto a trail strewn white with rubble that hadn't been there an hour before, and went on, because the place all the lines pointed was that same teeth-strewn country, and it had just told us, plainly, what it thought of visitors. 
I'd seen them before, back in town, but maybe something about being in w town softened it. out here a Stony was brief, untamed, and gave no warnings of its passing.

The keeping was a tower of dry-laid stone on a tor, the kind of high bare place where the wind has opinions, and it had been there long enough that the path up to it was worn into the rock a hand's-depth, and the keeper who met us at the top was as worn as the path.

She was old in the way that stops being about how many thousands of spins. She watched the four of us climb the last of it — a Forged, a swordsman, a guardsman, and a girl who served stew — and she did not reach for the bell-rope by the door, and she did not look afraid, and that was the first thing that frightened me, because everyone is at least a little afraid of Third, and she wasn't, because she had clearly decided some time ago that there was a worse thing coming and we weren't it.

"You're late if you're help," she said, "and early if you're him. Which."

"Help," Mathen said. "Late, possibly. How long have you known he was coming?"

"All my life. It's the keeping. You keep the thing, and you keep the knowing that one day someone comes for the thing, and your whole use is to have kept it that long." She stood aside from the low door. "My grandmother's grandmother kept it. I have no one to hand it to. I had started to hope I'd die first and make it someone else's morning." She looked at Third as we passed, and something moved over her face, recognition without fear. "You're old enough to know what it is. Tell them, if you like. I'm tired of being the only one who carries it."

Inside, on a stone shelf worn to a dish by the centuries of hands that had checked it was still there, lay the thing. It was not impressive. They never are, Third said once — the load-bearing things never look like much, because the Titan had no need to make them look like anything. It was a short rod of something that was almost glass and almost not, pale, the faint blue-white of the dried stuff in the geyser field, with a grain to it like wood. I reached to pick it up the way you'd pick up anything, and it would not come. Not stuck — fixed, as though the rod and the stone shelf and the hill beneath the stone were one piece and always had been. I pulled harder, feeling foolish, and it was like trying to lift a line drawn on the floor.

"You have to wake it," the keeper said.

"Tap this end, once." She touched the near end of the rod.

"Then it remembers it's a thing, and a thing can be lifted. Tap the far end and it forgets again — settles back into being a place, and a place does not come up for anyone." She watched me.

"This end, girl." I tapped the near end with one finger. Something let go — I felt it in my teeth more than heard it — and the second time I closed my hand around the rod it lifted easy, and had a weight to it then, like a held breath.

"It is a peg," Third said, very quietly, turning it in the firelight without taking it from my hands. "A small one. The Titan made the world and tied it down, the way you'd peg a tent against a wind, so that it would not — drift, unmake, I do not have the word, the word is older than words. There are pegs like this all through the world. Most no one will ever find. He is finding them." The eye dimmed. "I had thought he was gathering tools to build something. He is not building. He is pulling pegs."

"And when you pull all the pegs out of a tent," Mathen said slowly.

"You do not have a tent," said Third. "You have cloth, and wind, and whatever the tent was keeping out."

The keeper laughed, a dry awful sound. "There," she said. "Now there's two of you who know. I feel lighter already." And then, because the wind had changed, or because a lifetime of keeping had taught her exactly what the changing of it meant: "He's here. Put it back, girl. Putting it back never helped, but I'd like it to be on the shelf. I'd like to have kept it to the end."

I put it back on the worn stone dish and tapped the far end the way she'd shown me, and felt it take — felt the rod stop being a thing under my fingers and settle back into being a place, fixed, part of the hill again. My hands weren't steady.

I had to tap twice before it caught.

I had built him, in my head, out of the wounded man's words and the wreck of the town. I'd made him huge. A figure of smoke and ruin, something that came on like weather.

He came up the worn path like a man.

That was the first wrong thing — that he was man-sized, man-shaped, walking on his own two feet up the same hand-worn path we'd climbed, unhurried, as if he had all the spins there had ever been. He wore something that had been clothing once and was now mostly the idea of it. And on his back, rising over his shoulders like the frame of a pack, were six jars of magefire. I knew it on sight; anyone raised in Anka would. The white-with-a-hint-of-blue that comes off the powder inside, caged lightning, the stuff the militia loads into a Boltcaster and calls a shot — the same cold clear glow that lived behind the seam of Third's chest. But a shot is a little thing, a vial you could hide in your fist, and there's a size a shot is and no other, and has been as long as anyone's been selling them. These were jars: great hand-shaped jars, each big enough to put up a hundred spins of preserves in, and older-looking than any shot I'd ever passed across a bar, as if they'd been made before anyone had decided what a vessel of the stuff should be. Six of them, lit from inside, casting no warmth, throwing his shadow six ways at once down the rock.

Third had said it back at the inn, and I had not truly heard it until now: *a great many bottles of it, more than I have ever seen one body carry, and I do not know why it needs so much.* I had imagined a rack of the little shots. I had not imagined six jars of caged storm riding the shoulders of a man strolling up a goat-path to rob a frightened town. He did not look at us. That was the thing I have never been able to make people understand. We were four armed and frightened people standing between him and the thing he'd come for, and his eyes — they were a man's eyes, that was the worst of it, just a tired man's eyes — passed over us the way yours pass over the furniture in a room you're crossing to reach the window.

Roan moved first.

I want it written down exactly, because I have heard it told wrong since, told as foolishness. It was not foolishness. Roan stepped into the doorway with his spear levelled and his voice shaking and he said "you don't go in," and he meant it, and he was more frightened than I have ever seen a person be, and he did it anyway, and that is the only thing bravery has ever been.

The man looked at him. For the first time, something behind the tired eyes engaged — mild, almost kind, the look you'd give a child who'd planted himself in your path with his arms out.

And then he stepped sideways.

I felt Third go rigid beside me. Not move — the opposite of move. He went rigid the way you go rigid when you see a thing that cannot be, and the not-being of it nails you to the spot, and in the half-breath that Third stood nailed, the man was simply — past. Past Roan's spear, past the door, not through them, beside them, gone out of the world at that angle my eye could never hold and back into it on the inside of the tower, between Mathen and the shelf, and he reached out and took the peg off the worn stone dish the way you'd take a cup off a table in your own house.

He did not wake it. I had set it back into a place with my own shaking hands, and a place does not come up for anyone — and it came up for him, no tap, no catch, as though it had never been fixed to anything at all. Then Third moved. Too late, knowing it was too late, crossing the tower in a stride with his glaive coming round — and the man looked at him.

At Third. Only at Third. The mild eyes found the Forged and for one instant they were not mild at all, they were *interested*, they sharpened on Third the way they had not sharpened on four armed strangers, and his head tilted a fraction, the way you tilt your head at a sound you half-recognise from a long time ago. His mouth moved. I was close enough to see it and not to hear it, and I have spent a long time since trying to read the shape of the word off the memory of his lips, and the nearest I can come is that it was not a word of threat.

It might have been the start of a name.

Then he stepped sideways again and the tower was only the tower, wind through the door, the keeper on her knees by the empty dish, Roan still braced in the doorway against a man who wasn't there anymore, and Mathen with his good blade half-drawn and nothing to draw it on, and Third standing in the middle of the round stone room with his glaive up and his single eye gone down to a coal, staring at the place where the air had closed.

Nobody had touched him. That was the whole of it, and it took us all a while to be able to say it. Four of us, and the worn old keeper, and her hand-deep path, and her grandmother's grandmother's keeping — and he had walked up like a man and stepped out of the world twice and taken the peg and not troubled to harm a single one of us, because harming us would have required us to matter, and we hadn't.

The keeper didn't weep. She knelt by the empty dish and put her hand in the worn hollow of it, the dish her people had worn smooth with checking, and she left it there, and she said, to no one, "well," in the voice of a person setting down a thing they have carried so long that the not-carrying is worse than the weight ever was.

Mathen got her up, and got water into her, and got a fire going, because that is what Mathen does, he tends the spaces between the standing and the falling. Roan sat in the doorway where he'd braced and looked at his own hands as if they belonged to someone he was disappointed in, and I went and sat by him, because I'd watched him do the bravest thing on that tor and I wasn't going to let him learn the wrong lesson from its uselessness.

"It didn't matter," he said.

"It mattered," I said. "It just didn't *work.* You'll want to keep those two apart in your head or they'll rot each other."

He didn't answer, but he didn't get up either, and we sat.

Third had not moved from the middle of the room.

I went to him last, because he was the one I understood least and feared most to ask. The coal of his eye had not come back up. He was looking at nothing, at the closed air, at the angle.

"Third," I said. "What did you see?"

And he was quiet for long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer, and when he did it was not to my question, not really, it was to some older one of his own.

"He went sideways," Third said.

"I — yes. He vanished. Both times. How—"

"He went sideways," Third said again, and there was something under the avalanche of his voice I had never heard there before, something I would not have a name for until much later, when I had learned what it cost a thing to be the only one of its kind for three hundred thousand spins and then, on a windy tor, in the middle of a robbery, to discover that it was not. "I have never," he said, "in all that time, met anything else that could do that."

He turned and looked at me then, and the coal of his eye had a question in it that I couldn't answer and he plainly couldn't either, and the glowing six-lamped shape of the man who'd walked up like a man was burned into both of us, and somewhere in the part of Third where the gears turned wrong, two cold blue-white lights I would not understand for a long time yet were quietly keeping him alive, the same cold blue-white as the six that had just walked back out of the world.

He didn't say that part. He didn't know to, or he knew and couldn't. But I think it was in him even then, the wrongness, the recognition with no shape — that he had finally seen the thing that fit the hole, and the thing that fit the hole was lit by the same light he was.

"We've lost it," I said, because someone had to say a true thing that was the right size.

"We have lost this one," Third agreed. "Yes." The eye came up, by degrees, to something like itself. "Now we stop reaching the pegs after him. Now we go to the place all the lines point, and we are there before him, and we do not stand between him and a thing on a shelf." He looked out the low door, anti-spinward, toward the country that rose toward the rim and the fields and, back spireward of them, the place the whole crooked map had been pointing at since the town that died. "We stand between him and the last peg. The one the whole tent hangs from."

"The Graveyard," Mathen said quietly, from the fire.

"The Graveyard," said Third.
