# Twenty Seven

The second night of the waiting was worse than the first, because on the first night the dread had been a new thing and you can stand almost anything once for its novelty, and on the second night it was just the furniture. The cliff drank the grey. The book turned its pages out in the dark. We sat our paired watches and ate cold and spoke little, and Roan had got to that brittle place a body gets when it has been afraid for longer than it can keep being interestingly afraid, where a person will either find something to do with the fear or start coming apart at a seam.

It was Mathen who saw it, of course, and Mathen who knew the cure, because the cure was the thing Mathen was for.

"Third," he said, into the cold, in the easy way he had of setting a hook so gently you didn't feel it go in. "You owe me a story. I've told every one I have twice over on this road, and you've told us exactly one, and it was a good one, but a man can't live off one. You've had — how long did you say? Don't answer that, it depresses the children. You've had longer than anyone. You must have a hundred you've never told a soul."

"I am not good at it," Third said. "You are good at it. I state things."

"Then state me one." Mathen settled back against the fallen slab, hat down. "You taught me yourself, near enough — no, *I* taught *you*, that's the one, a story's how you make more than one person carry a thing. You've a great deal you've been carrying alone, master Forged. Set some of it on the fire where the rest of us can warm our hands at it. That's all a story is. Spreading the load."

The coal of Third's eye sat on the fire a while. Then it did a thing I'd not seen it do — it went not to the cliff, not down into him, but somewhere sideways and warm, the look of a person opening a drawer they keep meaning not to.

"I will tell you about Dorn," he said.

I sat up at that. I knew the name. I'd known it since the inn, the corner table, the Boltcaster he carried and never fired — *Dorn's*, he'd said, the way you'd name a grave. I knew the name and the fact of the death and not one thing else about the man, and I found I badly wanted to.

"Dorn was a fighter," Third said, "the way some are born singers — without effort, and without much sense of it being remarkable, which made him very restful to be near. We travelled together a long while. He hunted things that needed hunting, monsters that were troubling the roads, and he never once did it for the bounty, though he took the bounty, because, he said—" and here Third's flat voice did something almost like an impression, a fond clumsy reaching after another man's warmth — "'because it is the right thing to do, Third. The coin is just the icing on the cake.' I did not know what a cake was. He drew me one. He was not a good artist either, but he was a patient teacher of useless things, which it turns out are the things you miss."

Roan, despite himself, huffed a small laugh. Mathen said nothing, watching Third the way one craftsman watches another work in a difficult medium.

"He told jokes," Third went on. "Badly, mostly. But he had one — one he was known for, that he would tell to anyone who would hold still — and it was, I am told, genuinely good, though I am the wrong judge of such things. I have remembered it exactly. May I tell it the way he told it."

"Please," said Mathen, and there was no mockery in it at all.

Third set down, deliberately, the thing he'd been holding without my noticing he'd taken it out — the Boltcaster, Dorn's Boltcaster, the squat ugly wooden thing he carried and never loaded — laid it across his knees like a man laying out a friend's coat. And he told the joke.

"A Mender and a Crafter started arguing over whose work matters most, and because of the local children's festival, they decide to settle it the way children settle things: hide and seek. Whoever is found last matters most. They both run for an old bridge, because it is the only good hiding place for a mile, and there is room for one under it, and so they crouch together in the dark, elbow to elbow. 'Who's counting?' whispers the Mender. 'I thought you were,' whispers the Crafter. 'Counting is mending. You put the numbers back in order.' 'Counting is making. You make each number out of the last.' And they are off again, in hisses, mending against making, making against mending, so absorbed that neither of them notices that somewhere a long way down, slow and patient, a count has been going on. Ninety-eight. Ninety-nine. A hundred."

Third paused, exactly the length Dorn must have paused.

"And from somewhere else in the dark, a Grask says: 'Ready or not.'"

There was a beat of nothing — and then Roan barked a real laugh, the first true one I'd heard out of him since the tor, the kind that surprises its owner, and Mathen laughed properly, head back, and even I felt the thing crack open in my chest that a good joke cracks, the relief of it, in that awful place, under that drinking cliff.

"Oh, that's *good,*" Mathen said, wiping his eye. "That's properly good. Ha ha! Your Dorn had timing, Third, you can hear it even through you, no offence. I'll have to remember that one." He was still smiling. "A Mender, a Crafter, and a Grask. I'll get a tenday's drinks off that, easy."

"He would have liked that you laughed," Third said. "He measured a day by how many he could make laugh. He would have counted tonight a good day, this far from anywhere, with the world the way it is."

And the smile on Mathen's face was the last easy thing about the night, though none of us knew it yet, and the joke about the Grask hung in the cold a moment longer than a joke should, and then the fire popped and we let it go.

"How did he die," I asked, when the laughing had settled, because I'd learned that the only respect worth giving Third was to ask the real thing.

"In the night," Third said. "The way they do." The fond drawer had closed; the voice was flat again, but it was the flatness I'd learned to hear *under* now, the flatness that was holding something down. "We were camped off the road. Hunting a Subsurface Shredder that had been taking travellers on a stretch outside a village — for the bounty, and because it was the right thing to do; he had me saying it by then. He was asleep. I was keeping the watch; I do not sleep, it should always have meant nothing could come on us unseen." The Boltcaster lay across his knees. "It did not come from where seeing helps. It came from below. A Shredder puts a limb up through the ground before any part of it you could watch for breaks the surface, and the first I knew of it was the sound, and the sound was already through him. One limb. Through the chest. Before I had finished standing up."

The fire ticked. Nobody moved.

"I killed it," Third said. "That part was not difficult; they are only difficult to the people who cannot see them coming, and I could see it, after. It is a small skill, killing the thing that has already done the thing. I have a great deal of it." The eye stayed on the fire. "He was alive a little while. Long enough to ask me to remember him, which he said would make him immortal, of a sort, and which I did not understand then and am, perhaps, beginning to. I burned what was left, the way you do. I kept the head of the Shredder, to show the village it was ended. And I kept this." His wooden hand settled on the Boltcaster, not gripping, just resting. "It has no rounds in it. I have never fired it. I do not carry it to use. I carry it because he carried it, and a thing a person carried every day for a life holds some of the shape of them, after, if you keep it near. It is not a sensible reason. I have stopped requiring my reasons to be sensible."

"And the Shredders," I said. Quietly. I had a sudden, cold understanding of a thing I'd been told in an inn a road-and-a-lifetime ago and not known the weight of. "You hunt them. Still. You've hunted them this whole time. The one outside my town — Tondi's bounty, the head you brought back — that's why you took it. Not the job. Them."

"Them," Third agreed. "Wherever I hear of one. I do not take payment for those, when I can avoid it; it feels like the wrong kind of icing." A pause, the nearest he came to the thing underneath. "It does not bring him back. I am aware. I am very old and I am aware of exactly how little it does. I do it anyway. It is the one piece of grief I have found that has somewhere useful to go, and I have a great deal of grief and very little of it goes anywhere useful at all, so I spend that piece where it spends." The eye came up, found me, found Mathen, found Roan. "That is Dorn. That is the story. Thank you for holding still for it. He would have liked you. He liked almost everyone; it was his great flaw as a judge of character and his great gift as a man."

Mathen reached over, after a moment, and put his hand flat on the Boltcaster beside Third's, just for a breath, the way you'd touch a coffin in passing to say you'd known the one inside.

"A Mender, a Crafter, and a Grask," he said softly, to no one, to the dead man. "Aye. I'll remember it."

We kept the rest of the watch in a better silence than the one before — the fire lower, the cliff still drinking, the book still turning its unseen pages — and I lay down when my half came and did not sleep for a long time, thinking about a man I'd never meet who drew cakes for a monster-killing machine because the machine didn't know what a cake was, and about how Third collected us, the warm restful ones, one after another across all that time, and kept our coats and our jokes and our Boltcasters when we were gone, and how the keeping was the whole of what he had, and how I had somewhere along this road become one of the ones he was going to have to keep.

I did not think, then, about the joke. About which of us was the Mender, and which the Crafter, and how the punchline always comes from under your own two feet while you're busy arguing about something else. I wish I could say I had. It would have changed nothing, but I would have had the warning, and I have since come to set a high value on warnings, having been given so few that did any good.
