# Fourteen

You smell the geysers before you see them, and the smell is the first argument the place makes that you should not be there. Hot copper and wet stone and something underneath that's almost sweet, the way meat is almost sweet just before it turns — a red smell, if a smell can have a colour, and this one could.

Then you come over the rise and there they are: a field of chimneys. Not built ones — grown ones, tapering spires of pale mineral four and five humins high, hollow, rough-sided, the spit and image of the great termite towers you sometimes find in dry country, except that these wept. From the open tops of the living ones a fine continuous spray went up, on no schedule and from no pump anyone could name, and fell back red, and ran down the outsides, and dried as it went to something else entirely — a crust the colour of nothing I had a word for, a blue-white that was almost clear, glassy, catching the grey light and throwing it back cold. The red ran. The blue-white stayed.

And the red collected, at the foot of each living tower, in a pool.

That was where the people were — a dozen of them, kneeling not under the spray but at the pools' edges, well back from the falling rain, dipping wooden things into the still red surface and drawing them out darker. Anointing. Turning a carved stick into a blade that could open you. Nobody, I noticed, let the spray touch them. Mathen showed us later, in the flat way he delivered the facts a body ought to have before it did something stupid: wet on a blade, the blood makes hexwood. Wet on a person, it makes a very brief and very dangerous lunatic. You take it from the pool, where it's gone still and you can choose how much of you it touches. You never, ever stand in the rain.

The pools fed things. That was the next thing I saw — that each red pool had a ring of growth around it, and the growth was wrong: too dark, too glossy, fat-leaved and faintly moving when there was no wind, fed fat on the blood of a dead god and grown into shapes that had clearly stopped asking permission of the ordinary rules. Around two of the pools the growth had closed over entirely, a thicket nobody was working, nodding to itself.

And the dead towers. The oldest had sealed at the top, capped in blue-white glass, gone silent among the spraying ones like grave-markers among the still-breathing — and at the foot of each dead one the pool had dried too, dried and gone black, a flat black sheet of the same glass veining away from the tower in cracks across the pale ground, reaching, I saw now, toward the black cracks of the next dead pool over, and the next, so that the whole field was stitched beneath its living red with a dried black web of all the red that had ever pooled here and stopped. I had seen those black cracks before. Everyone has. They run through the whole world. I had simply never stood at the place where one was still wet.

"This is where hexwood comes from," Roan said, quiet, the way you'd speak in a place that might be holy or might be a slaughterhouse and you hadn't decided which.

"It is one place," Third said. "There are many. They do not last." He looked across the field at the sealed silent towers, the dead ones, with something I would not have called grief on a face that could make the expression, but which sat where grief would sit. "Each one closes itself, in time. The spray builds the tower, and the tower builds its own lid, and one spin the red stops coming and the place is only stone. Then I find the next." A pause. "This one is free. I have kept a great many free, over the spins. They keep ending the argument by dying."

He said *free* with a small weight on it, and I learned the rest of why over the next while, watching him.

There were two reasons Third had brought us a day off our line to a field that smelled of the inside of a wound, and he gave them both without my asking, which was his way when a thing mattered to him — he stated his reasons plainly, as if daring you to find them foolish.

The first was the blood itself. He needed it. I watched him do a thing I'd never seen him do and have never quite stopped seeing since: he stood at one of the weeping towers, away from the others, and held a wad of fresh-cut wood into the red pool until it darkened and drew up all it could, and then he opened a seam along his own forearm — the vine-cord that served him for muscle parting under his fingers like a purse — and for half a breath, before he packed the soaked wood into the worn place beneath, I saw inside him.

I am not going to be able to tell it properly. There were gears in there, and rods, turning, and the trouble was not that they were strange — I had expected strange. The trouble was that they did not *work.* Not work as in broken; work as in *make sense.* They turned against each other in ways that should have locked solid or flown apart, drove nothing I could follow into nothing I could find, and the longer my eye tried to trace one rod to the thing it turned the more something behind my eyes began to ache, a low sick wrongness, like a word you've said so many times it stops being a word. I looked away. I had to. Whatever kept Third moving was not clockwork, however much it wore the shape of it. Clockwork you can follow. This you could only flinch from, and understand that it went on turning whether you flinched or not.

He packed the soaked wood into himself, into the worn place beneath, the way you'd pack a wound except he was packing the wound *with* the thing that had made the world bleed, and the seam drew closed over the part I couldn't look at, and I breathed again. He saw me watching.

"I am made of anointed wood," he said. "The same as their blades. I wear, as the blood drains and then the wood wears. So I mend, as wood is mended. The pool gives me that, and the pool is free." He pressed the forearm seam flat. "That is the easy mending."

Then he did the other thing, and the other thing was worse, because the other thing he could not finish.

He opened his chest. Not wide — a hand's-span seam down the centre of him, drawn back the way the arm had drawn back — and inside, past more of the turning that hurt to look at, were two small lights. Blue-white. The cold clear blue-white of the dried crust on the dead towers, the blue-white that stayed. They sat in him like a pair of held breaths, side by side, and one burned steady and one had gone dim, guttered down to a wick-end of itself, and Third looked at the dim one a moment the way you look at a thing you have no answer for.

"This, the pool cannot give me," he said. "The wet mends the body. The lights want the dried — and not the dried that crusts a tower, which is too young and too rough, but the made dried, the kind that comes only from places I cannot simply walk up to and dip a cup. So I check them, here, where it is quiet. I watch the dim one. I calculate how long it has." He drew the seam closed over the two lights, and they were gone behind him, and I understood without being told that I had just been shown the most private thing about him and possibly the most dangerous. "Long enough, for now. But not long. A bottle this size burns sixty spins, eighty if I hold still, and then it is a dead coal and I am down to one, hunting the next before that one goes too. The big vessels last — a made jar the size of your two fists would last me lifetimes — but those the makers keep, or sell to things that can pay in coin or worse, and a thing like me takes what it can scrounge: small ones, a few at a time, never enough ahead. I have been hunting the next since before your people had a word for the colour. They are the part of me I did not make, and cannot make, and that is a difficult thing to carry, in a world where the only people who make the dried keep the making a secret and would like very much to charge me for it."

I didn't have anything to say to that. The thing that hardened the swords kept his body whole, for free, at any weeping pool. But the thing that kept him *alive* — the two cold lights — came from somewhere else, somewhere fenced and priced and held close, and he carried two of them and one was going dim, and he had decided not to be afraid about it, the way he decided most things, by stating them plainly and walking on.

The second reason I worked out for myself, from the way the working people glanced at him and then eased, and the way two hard-looking men with a hand-cart and a ledger came over the far rise, looked at the field, looked at the Forged standing among the towers in the middle of it, and turned their cart around.

"The men with the ledger," I said. "You knew them."

"Not them. Their employer." The eye tracked the cart until it was gone. "There is a guild that would like to put a fence around this field and a gate in the fence and a price on the gate. They have done it to three fields I know of. They have not managed it here." A pause. "I find I am here a great deal, when I can be. I am difficult to fence. And a field that something like me visits without warning is a poor investment for an employer who wants to charge admission." The glow settled, level and dry.

It was more than standing there, though. I watched him do the rest of it before we left — go to the two choked pools, the ones ringed round with the fat dark growth that had closed over and stopped anyone working them, and take a Falka to the thicket. Not hacking. Methodical, the way he did everything, cutting the wrong-shaped stuff back to the dirt and dragging it clear and stacking it to dry for burning, until the red surface showed again and a person could kneel to it without putting a hand into something that moved. The growth had teeth of a kind — a vine came round at his arm twice and he simply held it till it gave up, untiring, the way he'd cleared the carts — and when he was done two dead pools were working pools again, free for whoever came next.

"They grow back fast on the blood," he said, when he caught me watching. "A fenced field, a man would clear them once and charge you to use what he cleared. An unfenced one, someone has to clear them for nothing, every time, or the field closes itself to the poor before it ever closes itself for good." He wiped the Falka on the grass. "It is a small thing. The world should have a few free things in it. I have decided this is one of them. It costs me a detour."

That was the most I'd ever heard Third say about wanting anything. He wanted there to be a place where the poor could harden a blade for nothing, and he kept it open by the simple act of being too strange and too old to bill. I was still turning that over when the shouting started.

It started as the ordinary shouting of a man who has done something stupid and is refusing, loudly, to admit it.

"I'm *not* touching it!" — this from a Humin a few towers over, on his feet, a freshly dark blade in one fist and the other fist trailing red drops he plainly hadn't noticed. He was arguing with Third, who had risen and taken a few unhurried steps back, his Falkas coming up not as a threat but the way you raise an arm between a child and a fire.

"You have it on your hand," Third said. "It is running off your knuckles. In a moment it will be in you, through the small cuts every hand has, and then we will not be able to continue this conversation, because you will be trying to end me. I would prefer we skipped that part."

"I've anointed a dozen blades, I know how to—"

He didn't finish. I watched it take him. It came up through him like a tide finding a low field — his face going from indignant to confused to something with no person left behind it, the jaw setting, the eyes flattening — and the man who'd been protesting his competence a breath before came at Third with the new blade and a sound I don't want to describe, all the fury a body can hold and none of the aim.

Roan started forward. Third said, "No," without looking, and Roan stopped, and we watched.

I have tried to describe what Third did and the words keep coming out wrong, because every word for it is a fighting word and what Third did was the opposite of fighting. The man swung to open him from shoulder to hip. Third wasn't there — not dodged, exactly; *declined*, the way water declines a fist. The new blade slid the length of one of the Falkas and found only air at the end of it, and Third had turned a quarter-turn so the man's own fury carried him past, and then it happened again, and again, the man spending himself in great wild arcs and Third spending nothing, a quarter-turn here, his Falka tilted there, a thing that looked less like combat than like a very patient person walking a large dog that believed it was walking him.

And the whole time — I only understood this later, when I knew to look — the whole time, Third's free hand kept closing and opening at his side. Closing and opening. The way it had in the Snake Forest, when he'd said *no* to something none of us could hear. The voice was in him even here, I think, telling him the eight quick ways to end the man, and Third was answering it the only way he ever did, which was to keep choosing not to, one quarter-turn at a time, for as long as it took.

It took about a minute. The man folded onto the stained stone, the blade thumping down beside him, his chest heaving, the fury gone out of him as fast as it had come.

"You will be yourself again after thirty deep breaths or so," Third told him, not unkindly. "Most Humins are merely felled by it. You stayed upright and swinging for some time. That is unusual. You should not feel badly about failing to land a strike — I have been studying the art since before this field had a name."

Pale moths had come up off the pools while we watched — I noticed them only now, broad soft wings the colour of the dried crust, drifting in slow clouds above the red where the spray kept the air warm and wet. They were the loveliest thing in that whole red field, and I had learned, by then, to distrust that. "They drink the spray and carry it," Third said, to me, though his eye was on Mathen, folded and breathing on the stained stone. "A crushed wing, a swallowed moth, and a man takes a smaller helping of what you just had — enough to sour him for an hour, not enough to fell him." He let that sit. "It could have been worse, swordsman. A moth might have found you somewhere I was not — a market, a road, among people who cannot decline a blade the way I can. You would not have failed to land your strikes there. You'd have succeeded, on a local, or two, before it passed — and woken thirty breaths later a different kind of man than the one who lay down." Mathen didn't answer. He looked at the moths a long while. So did I.

He came back to himself the way you surface from a bad dream, all at once and ashamed. He got an elbow under himself and looked at the blade, and at Third, and at the two of us, and I watched him decide what kind of man he was going to be about it, which is a thing you can actually watch some people do.

"I'd apologise," he said, "but I think a thank-you's the more honest coin. You could've split me like kindling and I'd have had it coming." He worked his jaw. "I thought I'd taken every safe step. Turns out I'd taken every safe step but one, and it's always the one."

"No apology is needed," Third said. "And you are welcome."

"Mathen." He got himself the rest of the way up, waving off the hand Third didn't quite offer. "Known far and wide as Mathen, swordsman extraordin—" He stopped. He looked at the blade on the ground, and at the Forged who'd just made his finest fury look like weather. "Known far and wide as Mathen," he amended. "The extraordinary part could use some paring, I'll grant you, having just paraded it about for the whole field."

"You were measuring yourself against an experienced opponent," Third said. "Against most others the moniker might hold."

It was, I realised, a kindness — Third doling out the exact amount of dignity the situation could bear and not a grain more, because more would have been a lie and Third did not lie. Mathen heard it for what it was. Something eased in his face. And I felt the thing happen that I didn't have a name for yet: the air of our small grim company changed, the way a room changes when someone opens a window you didn't know was painted shut.

He asked to see one of Third's Falkas. Third let him, on the condition he could see Mathen's blade in turn, and the two of them stood in the bloody field passing weapons back and forth like boys trading knucklebones. Mathen's was a good blade, an heirloom — his father's chip-carving under his own, he said, three generations of marks before he'd judged it worthy of the red. He was proud of it and trying not to be, which made me like him.

Then he turned Third's over in his hands, and went still, the way people went still around Third when the wrongness of him landed.

"This is old work," Mathen said slowly. "This flourish, here — nobody carves like this. This is from—" He looked up. "Who made this?"

"I did, over many spins, before anointing it," Third said.

"And who repaired it? Since."

"The same. I learned a thing or two on mu travels."

Mathen laughed, the easy laugh of a man waiting for the rest of a joke. Then he didn't. He looked at the blade, and at Third's forearm where I'd watched the seam close, and at the single off-centre eye of him, the body visibly mended a hundred times over and the weapon nearly as old.

"Your waking," Mathen said carefully. "When was your waking."

"When the last of the Titan fell," Third said. "I was on the field. I felt the dust come down. I have been awake since."

I knew it already — or I'd been told it, which I was learning is not the same as knowing. But I watched Mathen do the sum the way I should have done it long before, watched him reach for a number and find that the numbers ran out before the answer did, watched a swordsman who'd been proud of three generations of marks on a blade stand in front of a thing that remembered the day the sky was torn. He didn't go pale. He went *quiet*, which on Mathen, I'd come to learn, was the louder reaction.

"Right," he said, at last, very softly. "Right. So when you said I shouldn't feel badly about not landing a strike." A breath of something that wanted to be a laugh and wasn't quite. "You were being generous."

"I was being accurate," Third said. "The generosity was a separate thing, offered alongside."

Mathen looked at him, and smiled.

He didn't ask to come with us. That's the part I keep, looking back — nobody recruited anybody.

He helped us refill our water, because he knew the clean seeps at the field's edge from the tainted run-off and we didn't. He told us, unprompted, the trick of telling anointed water from clean. He walked us to the far edge of the field naming the people who came regular, which ones would trade fair and which would short you, and somewhere in the naming it stopped being directions for travellers passing through and started being the talk of someone who'd decided, without announcing it, to keep walking the same direction we were walking.

It was Third who said it, finally, in the flat way he said the things that mattered.

"We are going anti-spinward, to stop a man who is gathering the tools of the Titans, with which i believe he intends to call one back. It is dangerous, and most who hear it do not believe the danger, and of those who believe it, most very sensibly decline to come. You should know what you would be following, before you follow it any further."

Mathen was quiet a moment. The geysers breathed their red breath behind us.

"A man trying to wake a Titan," he said. "And a Forged who's been awake since the last one fell, walking out to stop him." He shouldered his pack. "Ser, I have been looking my whole life for a story worth telling, and you've just told me there's one walking up the road, and you expected me to let it walk off without me?" He fell in beside Roan as if the matter were long settled. "I'd never forgive myself. And I'm a *terrible* audience for my own regrets, you'll find."

And that was how Mathen came among us — not because Third sought a companion, and not because anything was written, but because some people, shown the edge of a thing too big to refuse, simply step over it and ask what's for supper.

I caught Third watching him go ahead with Roan, the two of them already arguing amiably about something. The glow of his eye was steady, ordinary, unreadable. But he stood a moment before he followed, and I'd learned to notice when Third stood a moment.

"What," I said.

"Nothing," he said. "I am only counting. It is a habit." He started walking. "I find I have acquired three of you. I had not expected to acquire any."

He didn't say it like a complaint. He didn't quite say it like a gift, either. But it was the nearest to the second that I'd heard from him, and I walked the rest of that day with it warming a spot under my ribs that the Snake Forest had left cold.
