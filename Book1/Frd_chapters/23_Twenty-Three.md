# Twenty Three

Mathen wanted a story. He always wanted a story — it was the engine that ran him, the way some men run on drink and some on grievance — but that night he wanted one badly enough to go after Third for it, which none of us had ever seen him do.

"You," he said, pointing across the fire with a spoon. "Three hundred thousand spins. Do not tell me you are not carrying a thousand of them. I have met mayflies with better stories than mine and they only got the one afternoon."

"I do not tell stories," Third said. "I report. You have remarked on the difference yourself, at length, the night we met."

"I remarked that you tell the *accurate* one when a person wants the *true* one. So tell me a true one. I'll allow the once. As a favour to an old man who cooked." He'd cooked; we'd eaten; the debt was real and he knew how to collect.

I expected Third to deflect again. He was good at it — a wall you could lean a whole evening against and come away with nothing. Instead the single light of his eye went down a little, the way a coal settles when you stop feeding it, and he was quiet long enough that Roan looked up from oiling a strap, and then Third said, in the voice like stone moving slow over stone:

"I will tell the tree story. It is not mine. But I have the right to it."

And he told it the way you'd tell it to a child, which I understood later was the only way it had ever been told.

There was a little one, he said, who would not sleep. Who cried in a way that had no bottom to it and no door out of it, and there was a person who did not know how to help, who had only ever known how to end things, and who was very frightened of the crying because it was the one thing in the world he could not stop. So one day, out of all his being-frightened, the person did the only thing he had ever known how to do, which was to end things. He broke the wood all around him — pulled the branches down, put his hands through the trunks, made a ruin of a quiet green place — because breaking was the only tool he had, and the crying could not be broken. And when the place was wrecked and the little one was crying still, for of course it was, he picked the little one up, and he stood under the last tree left standing, one of the golden kind, with the leaves that sing when the wind moves them, small and thin and ringing like the inside of a bell — and a bird came down and sat in the branches, not afraid, because the violence was all over by then and the wood had already forgotten it. And the person did not know what to do, so he swayed. Just swayed, under the singing tree, with the bird watching. And the little one stopped crying. And slept. And the person stood there for the whole turn of the dark, not moving, in case moving was the thing that had been wrong all along.

"That is the whole of it," Third said. "There is no battle in it. No one is clever. A frightened thing learns it can be still. That is the entire story."

Mathen, for once in his life, did not say anything.

I was the one who saw it. I'll put it down and you can make of it what you like.

Past the edge of the firelight, where the dark started, there was — I want to be careful — there was the *suggestion* of someone sitting. Small. Knees drawn up the way a child sits to listen, or how a small grown person sits, I couldn't have said which. Listening with its whole self, as you only listen to a story you already know and love anyway. I'd swear the firelight went a little way *into* it instead of stopping at it, the way light goes into smoke. And it was smiling — I could feel that more than see it — the easy fond smile of someone hearing their favourite part land right.

I looked away to find my own breath, and when I looked back the dark was only dark.

"Wally," Third said. Soft. Into the place where the someone had been.

It meant nothing to me. I had never heard the name. But the way he set it down — gentle, like a cup you didn't want to wake anyone with — told me it was a name, and that it belonged to someone, and that the someone was not at this fire and was somehow, also, not entirely not at this fire.

"He liked the tree story best," Third said, and I noticed he said *liked* and then, a breath later, fixed it, quiet, only for himself: "—likes. He says mine is not as good as it used to be." The light of his eye came back up to ordinary. "He may be right. A story wears where you hold it."

"Who's—" Roan started, with the open face of a boy who asks the question everyone's thinking, and I caught his eye across the fire and gave the smallest shake of my head, and bless him, he stopped. Because I'd understood something I couldn't have said out loud yet. That there are tellings a person does for the listener, and tellings a person does *as* a keeping — the way you'd carry water cupped in your hands a long way, not because you're thirsty, but because someone down the road is. That Third had not told us the tree story.

He had told it so that it would go on being told. So that whoever it was out past the light would have, for the length of a story, somewhere warm to be.

"It is a good one," I said, when the quiet had gone on long enough to start to hurt. "Your story. The true one. It's a good one, Third."

"It is the good part," he said. And he put another piece of wood on the fire, though the fire did not need it, and we sat with it a while, the four of us and the maybe-fifth, in the singing nothing where the leaves would have been if there'd been a tree, before Mathen cleared his throat and started, very gently, on one of his own.
