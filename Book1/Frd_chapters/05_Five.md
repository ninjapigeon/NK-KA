# Five

The day did what days do. Customers came for the stew and left having eaten it; the Scar crawled its slow arc overhead; Tondi mended and grumbled and eventually went up to rest; and through all of it the thing in the corner sat under its tablecloth and did not move, except to adjust the Falkas on its back, until the light began to fail and the inn emptied to nothing and I found that I had run out of reasons not to go and sit with him.

So I brought my stew, just in case. The tablecloth had done a passable job of hiding the worst of it, but up close, where I was sitting, there was no pretending the thing across the corner table from me was anything other than what it was. The wooden hand with its too-many joints resting on the table's edge. The faint sound, every so often, of something mechanical inside a chest that didn't move. And the eye — one blue eye, set slightly off-centre in a smooth featureless face, which had not so much looked at me as found me, the glow of it fixing in place with a weight I could almost feel.

I'd served stranger, maybe. Tondi, for one.

What I kept coming back to was the weapon. Slung across its back, under the failing cloak, was a Boltcaster — a real one, a lightning-gun, the kind the militia talked about the way some men talk about women they'll never meet. Ours had exactly none. And here was one riding the back of a Titanspawn that fought with curved wooden blades it kept in its hands, while the Boltcaster stayed where it was, unslung, unfired, gathering the same dust as the rest of it.

"You don't use that," I said, before I'd decided to. I nodded at the weapon. "The Boltcaster. You've had a blade in your hand twice since you got here and that thing hasn't come off your back once."

For a moment I thought I'd asked the wrong thing, in the way you can feel a wrong question land before anyone tells you it was wrong.

"No," it said. "I do not use it."

"Is it broken?"

"It functions." A pause — not the kind where someone decides what to say, the kind where someone does arithmetic on a sum they have done many times and still do not like the answer to. "It belonged to Dorn. I carry it because putting it down would mean something I am not prepared to mean yet."

I didn't say anything. There are sentences you don't step on.

"Dorn travelled with me," he went on, when he had decided I'd earned the rest. "He told jokes. There was one he was fond of, concerning a Mender, a Crafter, and a Grask. I will not attempt to reproduce it. The delivery was the entire substance of the thing, and the delivery was his." The glow dimmed a fraction. "He was killed by a Subsurface Shredder. It came up through the ground where we had made camp, and it was very fast, and I was not fast enough, which is a thing I have had occasion to be more than once and have never learned to care for."

"I'm sorry," I said.

"Thank you." Stated simply, the way he said most things — not performed, just received and set down, like a toll paid. "There was a third of us, also. Adura. She was not taken by any creature. She had been unwell for a long while, and one day the being unwell finished what it had been doing slowly." The eye held on me. "I mention them because you should understand what travelling with me has tended to cost the people who have done it. It would be dishonest to ask without saying so."

I turned my cup in my hands. The stew had gone cold while we'd been talking and I hadn't noticed. "So you're looking for new ones. Companions."

"I am."

"Here." I gestured vaguely at the inn, the town, the general smallness of everything.

"It seemed a reasonable location to begin. Frequently travelled. A mender in residence suggests a degree of civilised infrastructure. And the memorial pillar outside is—" The eye moved to the shuttered window facing the square — the glow sliding across the dark, unhurried, the head not following — then back to me. "—modest. Which suggests the town has not yet suffered the kind of loss that makes people reluctant to risk further loss."

I stared at him. "You read the pillar."

"Before you arrived this morning. Yes."

I thought about that — Third standing in the pre-dawn, reading a knee-high plinth with one blue eye while I'd been asleep, unbothered by the dark and apparently making strategic assessments about grief thresholds. "That's very—" I tried to find the word. "Methodical."

"I have found it useful to understand a place before asking something of it."

Before asking something of it. I looked at the tablecloth I'd draped over him — my idea, technically, though Tondi had grunted permission — and tried to remember if I'd ever thought of this town as something that could be asked things. I'd mostly thought of it as somewhere I'd been born and hadn't left.

"What are you asking of it?" I said. "Specifically."

"For someone — or several someones — willing to travel with a Titanspawn toward a problem that will likely become violent, with no guarantee of return, in service of preventing something most people do not yet know is happening."

The stew was definitely cold. The inviting smell it had while warm, had fled with my appetite. I pushed it aside. "And what is happening? The thing most people don't know about."

The eye did not move from me. Whatever considering was happening, it happened behind that fixed blue point. Something settled in the set of the wooden shoulders — a decision being made about how much to say.

"Someone is attempting to resurrect a Titan," Third said. "I use the word attempting deliberately. I do not know how far along this attempt is. I do not know, yet, what they are collecting to achieve it. But I know they are collecting something, and I know that whatever it is, it has already cost one town everything it had."

The inn was very quiet. Klar had stopped scraping.

"The Titan," I said carefully. "As in — one of the ones from the war."

"As in the ones from the war. Yes."

"The ones that—" I gestured at the general state of everything. The world with a wound in its sky. The buildings with no ground-floor doors. The militia that drank before going out at night.

"Yes," Third said again. "Those ones."

I sat with that for a moment. Then: "And you're looking for companions. To stop this."

"I am already committed to stopping this. I am looking for companions because—" A pause. Longer than the arithmetic one, shorter than the one before he had said Adura's name. "Because I have found, over a considerable period of time, that I am better at most things with company. And because the last time something like this threatened everything living on Anka, it required a coordinated effort by an entire people to address it." The eye was steady on me. "That people no longer exists."

May they never be forgotten.

I didn't say it aloud. I didn't know why. It didn't feel like mine to say — and then I understood it was stranger than that. I had never in my life heard anyone name that lost people without the words trailing after, the charm tucked in quick and unthinking: Kern over his bow, the militia, Tondi when she wasn't even listening to herself. Everyone said the rest of it. Third had set an entire people down — gone, all of them, that people no longer exists — and reached for none of it. As though they were not a thing you guarded with a charm. As though they were just his.

"How many people are you looking for?" I asked instead.

"I am not certain. Enough. People who can think as well as fight. People who are not so committed to surviving that they become useless when survival seems unlikely."

"People like Roan," I said, before I'd decided to say it.

A long beat. "The militia member who found me at the gate."

"He's braver than he looks. And he doesn't drink for courage, which around here means something."

"I had noted that," Third said. "Yes. People like Roan." The bellows-sound again — slower this time, something gathering itself. "People like you, if you are willing."

I looked at my cold stew. I looked at the shuttered window. I thought about the pillar outside, knee-high, the town's whole history in a dozen names, and I thought about the fact that Third had stood in the dark reading it before the scar went past, before I'd even opened the inn, already deciding whether we were worth asking.

"You said whoever's doing this already cost one town everything," I said.

"Yes."

"And you don't know how many more before you can stop them."

"No. I do not."

I nodded slowly. Outside, the patrol's footsteps had faded. The Scar was almost fully across now — in another few minutes the light would go and the dark would come down. "How soon?" I asked.
