# Thirty Six

He did not describe it. I think that is what I had braced for — a speech, a temptation laid out in words I could hear and hate and argue with. Instead the cold man simply opened his hand, palm up, toward the wall of standing grey behind him, and the grey thinned where he gestured the way frost thins on a window when you put your thumb to it, and Third made a sound.

I have heard Third make a great many sounds. The bellows-wheeze, the avalanche, the dry not-laugh. I had never heard him make this one. It was small, and it came from somewhere the bellows didn't reach, and it was the sound a person makes when they see something they had decided, for the sake of going on living, to stop letting themselves want.

I couldn't see it. That is the thing I have to keep telling people, the thing that makes them look at me like I failed him: I could not see into the grey. Whatever opened in that thinning, whatever stood in it warm and reaching, it was shown to Third and not to me. I fought the whole rest of it blind. I had a hand on his arm and his name in my mouth and a dead man's Boltcaster against my ribs, and I was holding him against a thing I could not see, by the one thread I had, which was that he had asked me to.

"There," said the cold man, soft as falling dust. "You know her."

"Liwon," Third said. Not to me. Not to the cold man. To the thinning in the air, to whatever stood in it. The avalanche had gone all to gravel. "Liwon."

"As she was," the cold man said. "Before the sundering took her. Whole. Small, and afraid of the dark, and certain, the way the very young are certain, that you would never let anything happen to her. She has been kept exactly so. The grey does not let go of what it holds; it only waits to be asked for it back." A pause, weightless, terrible. "You remember what was said to you, the night she died. A seed must die, and be buried, before it can blossom into something beautiful. You thought that a cruelty. It was a promise. This is the blossoming. The seed was planted three hundred thousand spins ago and I am offering you the flower. All of it. Her, and the swordsman with the squat Boltcaster, and the woman the illness took, and the one who taught you to want quiet, and — most lately, still warm, barely cold — the one who taught you to make your losses into stories. Mathen. All of them. Kept. Returned. The moment you open."

I felt Third lean. Not his body. The thing inside the body, the way it had leaned crossing the field, except the field had been a breeze against this. He leaned the way a drowning man leans toward air.

"You can't," I said — to him, low, the rope. "You're Third. You're here. I've got you. Whatever it's showing you, I've got you."

"It is Liwon, Tasha." He said it like a man explaining why he has to let go of the ledge. "She is holding up her arms. She does that — she did that — when she was frightened and wanted to be carried. She is doing it now. She is asking me to carry her. I have not been asked to carry anything in a very long time."

I want to be honest about how close it was, because the people who weren't there tell it like it was never really in doubt, like Third was always going to refuse, and that is an insult to what it cost him. It was in doubt. It was in doubt for longer than I will ever admit to anyone but this page. He stood at the foot of the spine with his dead daughter holding up her arms in a place I couldn't see, and every part of three hundred thousand spins of being alone reached back toward her, and the lights in his chest guttered low and lower, and his hand under mine had gone slack, and I talked, and talked, and felt the words going into him and falling through, like dropping stones down a well too deep to hear them land.

"Ask me what you are paying," Third said suddenly, to the cold man, and I heard him reaching — reaching for something, a handhold, a counter. "You always tell them the prize. You are too patient to tell them the cost. What is the cost."

"The world," the cold man said, without the smallest hesitation, and that was somehow worse than a lie would have been. "You know this. Everyone, everywhere, all that lives. I will not insult you by softening it. That is the price, and I am telling you plainly, because you are the first of your kind worth telling plainly, and because—" the worn face did something that might once have been gentleness "—because it does not matter. You will reach for her anyway. They always reach. I have watched a great many smaller souls than yours weigh the whole living world against one held face, and the world has never once been heavy enough. It is not a flaw in you. It is the shape of love. I am not your enemy, made thing. I am only the one who understands what you are for."

"Tell me your name," Third said — and I understood, dimly, that he was doing something, that asking was a way of staying, of keeping his feet, of making the thing in front of him a someone he could refuse rather than a force he could only fall into.

The cold man considered. "Sougad," he said. "It is the one thing I will give you for nothing. Everything else in the world has a price now; I have seen to it. But a name is cheap, and you have been honest with me, and I find—" the dust-dry voice paused over the unfamiliar shape of it "—I find I would like one person, before the end, to have known me well enough to use it. Sougad. There. Now refuse a thing with a name, made thing, if you can. It is harder. I know. I have a name too, and it is why I am still fixed to that Anvil and have not let myself fall into the grey and end, all these ages. A name is a small hook, but it holds."

"Liwon was not a seed."

Third said it slowly, and I felt the first thing in a long time that was not slack — a tension coming back into the arm under my hand, a man finding, in the dark, the edge of something solid.

"You say it as if it were a kindness. The seed, the blossom. But she was not a seed. A seed is a thing that is for something else, that has not happened yet, that is only worth what it becomes. Liwon was not for anything. She was small, and afraid of the dark, and entirely herself, already, finished, real. She was not waiting to become beautiful. She was beautiful, and then she was killed, and the killing was real, and the losing was real, and they were the last true things left of her." The gravel was firming back toward stone. "If I take her back as your flower, I agree with you that she was only ever a seed. I let you make her into raw material for a thing that comes later. I unmake the one true thing I have — that she lived, and was hers, and not yours, and not the world's, and not a price. A woman in the snow taught me that, once. I did not understand it until just now, with my daughter holding up her arms."

And for a moment — one bright unbearable moment — I thought that was it. I thought he'd done it. I felt him straighten, felt the armour hold, felt the rope go slack in my hand because he didn't need it, he was standing on his own—

And then Sougad, gently, did the cruelest thing in this whole cruel book. He did not argue.

"I know," he said. "You are entirely right. She was herself, and the loss was true, and taking her back dishonours all of it. I am not going to pretend otherwise; you have earned better." The grey thinned a little further. "Knowing that changes nothing. You are going to reach for her anyway, because being right has never once stopped anyone from being lonely. I told you. It does not matter if she is real. It does not matter if it is true. I do not need you to believe me. I need you to *want* her. And you do. You are right, and you want her, and the wanting is the door, and the door is opening, and I am sorry — I find, to my surprise, that I am actually sorry — but I have waited so very long."

And Third stopped talking.

Not the begging-quiet. I knew the difference; he'd carved the difference into me two nights gone. This was the other one. This was the going-still. His hand slid out from under mine. His head turned, slow, toward the wall of grey, toward the small reaching shape only he could see, and he took one step toward it, and he had nothing left to say to me at all.

I had a breath. Perhaps two.

And my hand, Scar forgive me, would not go to the Boltcaster. It went tighter around his empty arm instead, because he was my friend and he was walking away from me into the arms of his dead and the thing I had sworn to do in this exact moment was the one thing my whole body refused to do.

"ROAN," I screamed. It was not the plan. It was the opposite of the plan. It was the only thing in me.
