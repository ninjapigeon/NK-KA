# Twenty Two

The country had been getting meaner for two days. The trees thinned and then gave up; the green went to a low grey scrub that crunched underfoot and smelled of nothing; the trail stopped being a trail and started being the least-bad line across a waste of broken stone. We were walking toward the place near the edge of the world where the dead were said to crawl, and the land had begun, in the way of lands, to dress for the occasion.

I started talking about home. I do that when the quiet gets too big to stand — I fill it, and I fill it with whatever is smallest and safest, and home was both. I talked about the inn the way you talk about a place once you've put enough hard road between yourself and it that you can no longer remember being bored there. The stove that drew wrong until you learned its mood. Tondi's bucket she'd never once touched a coin out of. The three rooms upstairs that were empty more often than not, and the way the whole building seemed to lean a little toward the fire of an evening, like the rest of us.

Third walked beside me and let me talk, which he was good at — better than anyone I'd known. He had a way of being a silence you could pour into without it spilling.

"And Kern," I said. "There was always Kern."

"Kern."

"A Kro. A fixture. He'd been coming in longer than I'd been closing up, longer than Tondi'd had the place, maybe. Sat at the window table for one cup he'd make last the whole afternoon and never said three words you didn't dig for. I think I went my whole life finding him as much a part of the room as the window. You don't wonder about the window." I smiled at the scrub. "He had this bow. Always had it with him, leaned against the wall by his knee. A beautiful old thing — carved all the way down its length, its pattern too fine to really follow, worn pale and smooth where his shoulder rode it, and red worked down deep into the bindings, the way the Kro do their fine —"

I stopped.

I stopped because I had heard myself, and what I had heard was a description I had heard before, in a different mouth, on a different night, beside a different fire — *exquisitely made, someone had spent a long time on it, cared about every part of it* — a boy by a brook with a drawn bow and shaking arms, who had asked a question instead of loosing the arrow, and a thing of wood and stone that had not had a name for itself yet standing in the water hearing, for the first time in its existence, that a made thing could be beautiful and not just dangerous.

I turned. Third had stopped walking. He was a few paces back, where I'd left him when my feet had kept going and the rest of me hadn't, and the coal of his eye was on me, level and very still, and I understood that he had stopped some sentences ago.

"Third," I said. "Third, that's — the boy. From your story. The one by the water. The one who said—"

"A Blank just used *I*." His voice came slow, the avalanche moving one stone at a time. "Yes."

"It can't be the same bow." But I said it the way you say a thing you already know is wrong.

"Describe the carving again," he said. "The pattern too fine to follow. Tell me where it starts."

"At the grip. It — it spirals out from the grip, both ways, like it's growing from his hand—"

"From the grip, both ways, like a thing growing," Third said. "Yes. He had only just finished it, I think, when I knew it. There was no smoothness anywhere on it then. The carving was sharp enough to cut a thumb on; I remember thinking a person could ruin their hands keeping a thing that detailed. The red in the bindings was new and bright, not gone to rust the way you would have seen it. And his hands were not steady on it. He was very young, and very frightened, and he had made the most careful thing I had ever seen, and he was pointing it at me with his arms shaking." The eye did not move. "It does not wear smooth in one lifetime, Tasha. It does not wear smooth in fifty. What you wiped around at your window table — what some Kro made last an afternoon over a single cup — may be the same bow, worn by every shoulder that carried it between his and yours. That is what the smoothness is. That is what your hand was brushing past on its way to the next table. The whole length of the world, gone soft under a hundred hundred grips."

I sat down. I didn't decide to; my legs did it. There was a flat stone and I was on it.

"He's dead," I said. Stupidly. "The boy. He's—"

"He was Virek." Third said it gently, which from Third was its own kind of terrible. "You have seen, by now, more than I have told you, what became of the Virek. They are not dead, exactly. They are the Kro. They are the things in the dark with the teeth, too. They went into a light one night and came out as two halves of themselves and neither half remembered being whole. So no — I do not think the boy is dead in the way you mean. I think the boy is *gone* in a way that has no word, the way a river is gone when it reaches the sea — still there, still water, no longer the river. Whatever he became, he was not himself by morning, and he has not been, for longer than there have been words for any of it." A pause, and then the thing that I think he had not meant to say out loud, because his voice dropped under it: "He was the first one. The first thing that ever looked at me and saw a someone instead of a weapon. I have carried that for the entire age of the world, and he forgot it inside a single night, and could not have helped the forgetting, and I do not get to be angry, because there is no one left to be angry at. There is only a bow."

The wind moved the grey scrub. Far off, the thing we were walking toward sat at the edge of everything, patient.

"And it ended up in the corner of a room you were bored in," Third said. "Leaned against a wall. Warming itself at a stove that drew wrong. Some Kro who could not have told you whose hands carved it, drinking one slow cup, keeping it by his knee out of a habit handed down so many times that the reason had worn off it like the carving. That is what I cannot — " and here the avalanche stopped, the long bellows of his chest drawing once, slow, the only sound he made that I would have called grief if I'd dared call it anything. "He is gone past finding. His whole kind is gone past finding. And the bow is leaning on a wall in a town not worth a name, whole, cared for, carried all this way by people who never knew what they were carrying. I do not know what to do with that. I have not known what to do with it since you opened your mouth. It is the best thing I have heard in a thousand spins and I cannot tell whether it is a comfort or the cruelest thing in the world."

I looked up at him. The pieces I'd been carrying since the fireside story slid, all at once, into a different shape — and I saw, the way I was learning to see him now, that this was not only about a boy and a bow. There was something underneath it, something he was holding very still so it wouldn't show, the way you hold a cup you've filled too full. *Her*, I thought, from the night on the watch. *He is thinking about her. He is finding out whether a thing can be gone and not gone at the same time, and he is doing it out loud, on a bow, because the other thing he cannot say yet.*

I did not say any of that. I had learned that much.

"Both," I said instead. "It can be both, Third. I think the worst things and the best things are mostly the same things, looked at from where you're standing." I got up off my stone. "It got carried. That's the part I'd hold onto, if it were mine. Nobody knew, and it got carried anyway. All the way to me."

He looked at me for a long moment, the coal of his eye steady.

"All the way to you," he said. "Yes."

And we walked on, into the meaner country, toward the patient thing — and I found I was no longer thinking about how bored I had been at the inn. I was thinking that I had spent six thousand spins wiping a table beside the first beautiful thing a monster ever saw, and had called it handsome, and in my way, and had never once wondered how it came to be there.

You only get to not-wonder for free the first time.
