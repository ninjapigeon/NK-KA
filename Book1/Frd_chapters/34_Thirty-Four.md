# Thirty Four

The first thing he did was check his hands.

He did it the way he did everything that mattered, without hurry and without any sign that it mattered: a palm laid over each Falka at his hips, the two curved blades he'd spent thirty spins of the Scar learning to dance with, the only weapons he had ever carried that were his choosing and not his making. The small unconscious touch of a man making sure of his hands. There was nothing else on him to set down. He had done that a very long time ago, by a river, in front of a boy with a bow, and he had never gone back for it.

I understood it without being told, which is a thing that happens between people who have walked far enough together. Other people come to the end of the world carrying what they were given. He was coming to it carrying only what he had chosen, as he had for three hundred thousand spins, and there was nothing left to put down that he hadn't already.

"Tasha," he said. "The last of it. The part I have not told you."

"I know. I saw it on your face. Get it said; he's nearly here."

He was. Down the converging aisle the cold man had grown from a far mark to a near one, close enough now that I could see the lights riding his back had a faint and constant tremor, like something breathing. We had a hundred breaths, perhaps. Perhaps fewer.

"You are the rope," Third said. "That is true and it remains true and it is the first thing. As long as you hold me, I have a chance to hold myself. But you are going to need to know the second thing, and the second thing is worse, and I have not let myself say it until now because saying it makes it real and I wanted, selfishly, a few more spins of it not being real." The eye was very steady on me. "If the rope fails — if it gets so far into me that I begin to open, and you cannot pull me back — then holding me is no longer the job. Ending me is the job."

I had known, somewhere, that this was coming. Knowing did not help. It never does; he'd taught me that himself, coming back out of the grey with Mathen's face still on him.

"No," I said.

"Tasha—"

"I heard you. I said no. Tell me the rest of it anyway, because I'm going to need it, but you should know my answer first so you understand what it costs me to change it."

Something in the make of him that was not quite the bellows let go, a small mechanical sigh of a thing that had been braced for a fight and been handed honesty instead. "Thank you," he said. "That is exactly why it is you."

And then he reached around to his back, with that wrong boneless ease that still put a flutter in my chest after all this time, and he brought out the Boltcaster.

Dorn's Boltcaster. He told me he had never once loaded it, having carried it a very long time and never fired it, because it was not a weapon to him. It was a person. It was the last weight of a man he had failed to save by the width of a single breath.

He held it a moment longer before he gave it over — and then, with the same wrong boneless economy, he brought a single magefire shot up from somewhere on him, a little vial of caged lightning, and he loaded it. I watched him do it and felt the floor go out from under me a second time, because at the fire, the night he told us about Dorn, he had said it plain to the whole table: *I do not carry it to use.* He had carried it unloaded across more road than I could imagine, kept it the way you keep a grave tidy, and now, here, he was thumbing a shot into it with his own two-fingered hands.

"I have never loaded it," he said, as if he'd heard the thought. "Not once. Loading it would have made it a weapon again, and it stopped being a weapon the day it became Dorn. I am loading it now, because this is the one thing left in the world he would forgive me for aiming it at." Then he put it in my hands. It was heavier than it looked, but cold where his grip had been.

"There is a seam down the centre of my chest. You have seen the lights through it. Those lights are the whole of what keeps the door a door — they are what the wall wants, the same as the cold man carries, the same make, the same dying glow. If you put one of Dorn's shots into that seam, into the lights, the door stops being a door. Permanently. There is no repairing it; I have repaired everything I am, a hundred times over, and I could not repair that. It would be the one wound I have ever taken that I could not stand up from."

"You're asking me to kill you."

"I am asking you to unmake a door. That the door is also me is the cruelty of it, and I will not pretend it is anything else, because you have earned better than pretending."

"How do I know," I said. My voice was doing something I did not give it permission to do. "How do I know when. You already told me you'll beg. You already made me promise not to believe the begging. Now you're telling me to — and the begging version is going to beg me not to, isn't it. It's going to look at me with your eye and beg me to put it down. How do I tell the difference. How do I know when there's no more you to save and it's only the door wearing you."

"Because the begging means I am still there." He said it gently, the way you'd explain a hard thing to a child you loved. "While I beg, hold me. The begging is the part of me that still wants you to win fighting the part of me that wants to lose. As long as I am begging you, in either direction, I am still a fight you can win, and you must not use the Boltcaster, no matter how it sounds, no matter what I promise or threaten — you hold." A pause. The lights in his chest guttered, the dim one nearly out. "You will know it is time when I go quiet. Not the begging-quiet. The other one. When I stop arguing with you, when I stop arguing with myself, when I go still and turn to the wall and reach for it and have nothing left to say to you at all — that silence is not me resting, Tasha. That silence is the door swinging open. That is the only moment. You will have a breath, perhaps two, between the silence and the world ending, and in that breath you put Dorn's shot through the seam and you do not hesitate, because the hesitation is the exact size of the gap the thing needs."

I looked down at the Boltcaster in my hands. The whole stupid weight of it. The dead man in it. The friend who'd carried it unloaded and unfired across more road than I could hold in my head, because using it was unthinkable, handing it to me so that I could fire it into him.

"Why me," I said. "Roan would— no. Don't say it kindly. Say it true. Why is this mine."

<p align="center">&#42; &#42; &#42;</p>

"Because Roan would do it too soon," Third said. "He loves me as a boy loves the thing he's decided to be brave for, and that love would flinch at the begging and end me to spare me the struggle, and the struggle is the whole of the chance. And the cold man would do it too late, or not at all, because to him I am a mechanism and he would want to see if the mechanism could be made to work after all." The eye held mine. "You are the only one who will do it at exactly the right moment, which is to say the last possible one, which is to say only when there is truly nothing left — because you do not want to do it. Because you will hate every breath between the silence and the shot. I am not choosing the person most able to kill me, Tasha. I am choosing the person least willing. That unwillingness is the most precise instrument in the world. It is the only thing I trust to wait long enough, and still not wait too long."

He looked, for a moment, at the cold man coming, and then back at me, and he said the thing under the thing, the thing he had been walking toward saying since the slab field, since the geysers, since the inn:

"I am afraid I am going to want it. The opening. The price. I have been alone inside this wood and glass for three hundred thousand spins and it is going to offer me everyone, all at once, warm, and I am afraid — I, who am not built to be afraid of much — that I am going to want it more than I have ever wanted anything, more than I will want the world. So I am giving you the thing that means my wanting it will not be enough. I am putting the size of my own longing into your hands and I am asking you to be bigger than it. That is the whole of what I have ever asked anyone. I am sorry it is so large."

I did not say *I can't.* I had already used my no, and he had thanked me for it, and we both understood it had been the last one I was allowed.

I said, "Then you'd better beg loud, you old fool. Because I am going to need to hear you in there right up until the last breath, and if you go quiet on me one second before you have to, I will never forgive you, in this world or whatever's left of it."

"I will be as loud as I am able," Third said. "It is a promise. It may be the last one I am able to keep as myself."

And behind him, at the foot of the spine, where the pale grey stood up out of the ground against the pale dead stone, the cold man stopped walking.

We were out of breaths. He was here.
