# Six

"Soon," he'd said, which I took for a word chosen to avoid choosing a number. He kept his watch that night the same as every night, and if he learned anything more in the dark hours he didn't share it with me. The next morning came and went the way mornings did — shutters, stove, the regulars in for their bread — with nothing in any of it to suggest "soon" meant anything closer than it always had. It turned out to mean early afternoon, and it wasn't Third who set the date.

The traveller came in a little after midday, which was the wrong time for travellers. They arrived in the evening, mostly, when the dark was coming and a roof seemed worth paying for. A midday arrival meant someone who had been walking through the night, which meant someone who had not felt safe stopping, which meant news, and news that came in on foot at the wrong time of day was never the good kind.

He was Humin, middle-aged, with the particular grey-faced look of a man who has used up everything he had and is running on the memory of it. There was a wound on his forearm, bound with a strip of his own shirt, and the binding had gone stiff and dark. He came through the door and stopped just inside it, the way people do when they have been moving toward a destination for so long that arriving leaves them with nothing to do. And he brought with him the smell of someone who had been moving for a long time, but his breath was dry - any courage in him was entirely his own.

I got him into a chair before he fell into one. Klar brought water without being asked, which was Klar. Tondi, in her corner, set down her mending and watched, which was Tondi. And in the other corner, under his tablecloth again, Third did not move at all, which by now I had stopped finding remarkable.

The traveller drank. His hands shook. When he could speak, the first thing he said was a name I half-recognised — Chipwood — a settlement maybe two days spinward, on the same trail as ours, the kind of place that was to us what we were to Dunarth. Smaller. Further out. The next notch down the line.

"Gone," he said. "It's gone."

"Gone how," Tondi said, from her corner. Not unkind. Just direct, the way you are direct with someone in shock, because vagueness is a thing they cannot afford and neither can you.

"Ravagers." He said the word and then had to stop and breathe around it. "A whole — there were so many of them. They don't come in numbers like that. They don't. They come in twos and threes, you lose a goat, maybe a man if he's careless. This was—" He looked at his bound arm as though it belonged to someone else. "They came all at once. Like they'd been told to."

I felt the room go quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when several people have all had the same thought and none of them want to be the one to say it.

"Like they'd been told to," Tondi repeated.

"I got out because I was anti-spinward at the mill when it started. I ran. I didn't—" His voice did something then, and he didn't finish, and nobody made him. There are sentences you let a man leave on the floor.

I refilled his water. It seemed like the only useful thing available to me.

"There's more," he said, after a while. "I didn't see this part. I heard it from a woman on the trail, going the same way, faster than me." He wiped his mouth. "There was another town. Spinward of the one that — of mine. The Ravagers were going for it too. And a skybarge came down. The Porter."

I knew the Porter. Everyone on the trade did, even those of us who'd never once set foot up its ramp — it was the only inn in the world that came to you. A whole tavern slung under a skybarge's fans: Waj Hool's travelling house, food and drink and a dry bed for sale a hundred paces off the ground, here twice in three hundred spins if you were lucky and gone again on the wind. The whole town turned out to watch it settle, the time it came; I'd spent more than one shift at Tondi's after that wishing I worked a floor that moved. It was the last barge in the world I'd have looked to come down into trouble instead of sailing wide of it.

Tondi's mending had not moved in some time.

"Came down right over the square," the traveller said. "In the middle of it. Fans going, the whole — you could hear it to the next town, she said. And it just hung there. And the people were running for it, getting underneath it, and it wouldn't open. The barge wouldn't drop its ramp. Just hung there with the whole town screaming under it."

"Why wouldn't it open," Klar asked. He had not meant to ask. It had come out of him the way questions come out of the young, before they have learned that some answers cost more than the question is worth.

"She said there was someone arguing with Waj — with the master, down on the ground. Someone the master didn't want to listen to. She couldn't hear what was said. But the ramp came down in the end." He turned his cup in his hands, an echo of a gesture I recognised because it was mine. "Most of them got up and in before the Ravagers reached the square. Most of them. The woman said the thing that argued the ramp open was the last one up, and it walked up that ramp backward, facing the Ravagers, the whole way."

I did not look at the corner where Third sat. I want that on the record. I had every reason to and I did not, because some part of me had already done the arithmetic and did not want to confirm the sum.

But I heard it. Across the room, from under the tablecloth, the bellows in Third's chest drew breath — slow, the sound it made when something had landed with weight — and then nothing. No comment. No movement. Just the one long mechanical inhalation and the stillness after it, which was a different quality of stillness than the stillness before.

The traveller hadn't noticed. He was looking into his cup.

"There's one more thing," he said. "The part I keep — the part I can't—" He stopped. Started again. "When I went back. To my town. After. To see if anyone — I went back."

"You shouldn't have," Tondi said, gently, which was a register I had heard from her perhaps twice in my life.

"I know. I did anyway." He looked up, and his eyes found mine, and I wished they hadn't, because there was something in them that I did not have anywhere to put. "There was something there. Walking through it. No wait, more like floated through it. Through what was left. Not a Ravager — the Ravagers were gone, they'd moved on, they don't linger. This was after. Something floating through the dead, slow, like it was — looking for something. Going building to building. And the survivors, there were a few of us hiding, and it went right past us. Right past. Didn't look. Didn't care. Like we weren't worth the turning of its head."

The bellows again. Slow. Across the room. I kept my eyes on the traveller.

"What did it look like," I asked, and my voice came out steadier than I felt.

"I don't know. Cloaked. Hunched. There was — there were lights on its back, blue-white, like a bottled storm, like a whole rack of them, glowing through the cloak." He shuddered, and it was not from the wound. "It found whatever it was looking for in the headman's house. I heard it — there was a sound like a building being unmade. And then it left. Anti-spinward. Away from everything. And it never once looked at any of us."

He went quiet then, all the way quiet, the quiet of a man who has finally set down the thing he had been carrying and discovered that setting it down does not make it lighter, only shared.

I gave him the room. I gave everyone the room. The fire ticked. Klar stood in the kitchen doorway with a cloth in his hands, not using it. Tondi's mending lay in her lap.

And in the corner, Third stood up.

He did it slowly, the tablecloth sliding off his shoulders, and he did not bother to catch it, which was the first time I had seen him fail to account for the cloak in any way. He stood in the corner of the inn at midday with his one blue eye gone bright — brighter than I had seen it, the glow of it hard and steady — and he looked at the traveller, and the traveller looked up at the thing that had been sitting two paces away the whole time, and made a sound.

He was not the only one to make it. I had forgotten them the way you forget furniture — but the news had drawn a few of the regulars in off the lane and then kept them, the way bad news keeps people, and they had spent the last hour with their backs to the corner, listening to a town die, while the corner sat two paces behind their spines and did not breathe. Old Sem was there, and a carter I half-knew, and a woman who'd come for stew and stayed for the worse thing. And now the corner had stood up, and shrugged its cloth onto the floor, and become a wooden monster with a burning eye, and it had been there, that close, the entire time. The room came apart the way a startled flock does, all at once and in every direction. A bench went over backward. The carter was on his feet with a paring knife he'd lifted off the bar without deciding to, holding it the way you hold a thing you already understand will not help. Sem had the door half open. The traveller had shoved himself clear off his chair and fetched up against the wall, and the noise coming out of him had stopped being a noise a man chooses to make.

"Sit." Tondi did not stand. She didn't need to. She had a voice she kept for about once in three hundred spins, and she spent it now, and it landed on the room like a dropped lid. The knife stopped. The door stopped.

"That," she said, into the ringing after, and she did not so much as glance at the corner, "is the reason there's meat in your bowls and not a Shredder on your road. It came in off the dark and laid the head of the thing on my table — the Shredder that's been taking travellers off the anti-spinward trail, the trail half of you walk to market. I examined it myself. Klar's been feeding it to you since the pot got hot." She licked her thread and went looking for her needle.

"It has sat in that corner behind a cloth, troubling no one, doing you the courtesy of not frightening the very people who are now frightening themselves over a tipped bench. So. Sit down and finish your bowls like grown folk, or take your fright out the door and leave the rest of us to hear the man — but either way you'll do it without the knife, Bevin, before you cut yourself and I'm obliged to stitch you for nothing." It was not kindness. Tondi had no use for kindness; she found it sentimental. It was the thing she kept instead, which was worth more — the flat, bottomless certainty of a woman who has decided a thing is true and will not be argued off it by anybody's heartbeat. Bevin looked at the knife as though it had got into his hand by witchcraft, and set it on the bar. Sem let the door swing to. The woman sat, because Tondi had said sit and there are people in every town who have been waiting their whole lives for someone to tell them what to do in exactly that voice. Nobody else quite managed it. But nobody ran, either, and I was starting to learn that nobody-running was the whole of what you got to ask of people the first time they met him. Through all of it Third had not moved. He had not turned his head, had not marked the knife or the door or the going-over of the bench; he had stood with his one bright eye fixed on the traveller and the traveller alone, as untroubled by a room deciding whether to kill him as the Scar is by the things it passes over. He had been waiting, I understood, for the noise to finish — not out of patience, but because the noise had nothing to do with the single thing he had stood up to learn. "Anti-spinward," Third said. The voice filled the room the way it always did, but there was something underneath it now, something I had no name for and was not sure Third did either. "You are certain. Anti-spinward."

The traveller could only nod his frightened head. Third turned his head — the head, the whole head — toward me. The eye was very bright.

"You asked how soon," he said.

I had. The evening before, at that very table, I had asked exactly that, and he had not answered, and I had gone home thinking I'd have days to decide whether I'd meant it.

"Yes," I said. "I did."

"Now," Third said. "It is now."
