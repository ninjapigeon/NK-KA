# Twenty Six

We kept the watches in pairs, the way Mathen said, and I made myself Third's pair before anyone could think too hard about who'd draw the short straw of sitting up beside the one who didn't sleep. It wasn't bravery. I want that on the record. It was that I'd seen him come back off the flat slab unsteady, testing each hoof like a man on a deck, and I had decided — somewhere I hadn't asked permission of — that I was not going to let him do whatever he was about to do with nobody watching but the stones.

So Roan and Mathen took the first half, back to back on the fallen page with the fire between them and the field, and when the Scar swung its dark edge up over us and the cold came down, they woke us, and Mathen put a hand on my shoulder in passing and said nothing, which from Mathen was a whole speech, and then it was me and Third and the fire and the rearranging dark.

For a while we just kept it. The field did its work out there in the black — a crack like thunder, far off; a long grinding fall, further; the book turning its pages where no eye could hold them still. I'd stopped flinching. You'd be amazed what you stop flinching at.

"You're going to go across again," I said, eventually. It wasn't a question. None of mine were, anymore.

"I must." The coal of his eye was on the cliff, that black mass blacker than the dark around it. "He is coming. I do not know when. But the grey will know before the world does — it always knows the weather of a thing before the thing arrives — and I would rather meet him understanding something than meet him understanding nothing." A pause. "It is dangerous, here, to cross. You saw. It pulls. I told you it pulled."

"You told me you weren't sure you could've got back."

"I was not. I am still not." He turned the eye on me, and there was a request in it he plainly hated to make, because Third hated needing things, it was the most stubborn fact about him after the not-dying. "Which is why I am going to ask you for something I do not know how to ask for gracefully. I will not go all the way across tonight — going all the way across, out there, is how I nearly did not come back. I will hold at the edge of it, a foot in each, and reach. But the reaching is its own danger, and I need a thing to hold to on this side. A line back. The grey is quiet and it is patient and tonight it is *pulling,* and the danger is not that something in it hurts me. The danger is that it becomes easier to stay than to come back, and I stop wanting the coming back, and there is no edge to tell me I have waited too long." The fire ticked. "You are loud, Tasha. In the lived world. You have always been the loudest thing in any quiet I have stood in. I would like, if you are willing, to hold to that. To you. Talking. So that there is a thing on this side worth the difficulty of returning to."

I did not trust my voice for a second. Then I said, "You want me to nag you back from the dead."

"Essentially."

"Third, I have been told my whole life that's my worst quality. You're saying it's a rescue technique."

"I am saying," he said, with enormous gravity, "that tonight it may be the most useful thing in the world, and I would take it as a kindness if you did not stop, no matter how little I appear to be listening, and that if I tell you to stop, you must understand that the part of me asking you to stop is the part you are saving me from." The eye held me. "Will you?"

"Yeah," I said. "Talk you back. I can do that. I'm good at that."

"I know," he said. "Begin when I close the eye. Do not stop until I open it."

And then he set himself against it — not leaving, though leaving was the word that came; more the way a swimmer sets against a current that wants him, half his weight going out of the lived world and the other half digging in to stay — the eye dimming down not to the grieving coal but to almost-out, the last wire of it, the wooden shape of him gone rigid with the work of being in two places and surrendering to neither. And I started talking.

I don't remember the half of what I said. That's the truth of it. You don't, after; it isn't speech you're making, it's rope. I talked about the inn. I talked about Tondi and her water cup and the way she never touched a coin, and the stupid sign that said *Only*, and Klar's first attempt at Shredder stew that he'd been so proud of, and the way the militia drank their courage before patrol and Roan was the one who didn't, and the smell of the place in the morning before the stew went on. I talked about my father telling me never to let them see my fear. I talked about the road, every dull stretch of it, the bog and the blizzard and the field of stinging glass, all the things that had nearly killed us made small and ours and *survived,* because that was the point, I understood that even while I was doing it — I wasn't telling him true things, I was telling him *ongoing* things, things with more of them still to come, a world that was not finished and therefore worth the difficulty of returning to.

His eye stayed almost-out. The cold got worse. Out in the field the great book turned a page with a crack that I felt in the fallen slab under me, and I did not stop talking. A while into it — I don't know how long, time goes strange when you're being a rope — his eye began, very slightly, to *drift,* the way a moored boat drifts when the line goes slack, his whole still shape leaning a hair toward the cliff, toward the black mass and the pull behind it, and something in me that wasn't thinking said his name instead of all the other words, just his name, *Third, Third, you've got stew to complain about, Third, I'm not done telling you about the sign* — and the drift stopped. Held. Hung there at the end of the slack line that was apparently my idiot voice. And then, slowly, came back.

The eye came up. Not all the way. But up, and toward me, and the rest of him came back into one place, the hoof testing the slab, the deck-in-a-swell carefulness, and I shut up at last because he'd opened the eye and that was the deal, and I was shaking, and I hadn't noticed I'd taken hold of his wooden forearm with both hands at some point and was gripping it like the rail of a thing going down.

"You came back," I said.

"You were very loud," he said. "It was very difficult to stay." And then, lower, the nearest thing to *thank you* his pride would let out the door: "I heard the part about the sign. It is a good detail. The crafter illustrated the only building in town." A breath of the bellows, the one I'd allow him. "I held to that. The absurdity of it. A world that makes a sign like that is a world with more in it. You were right to choose it."

I let him sit a while before I asked. I'd earned the asking and he knew it, but I let him sit.

"What did you see."

The eye went to the cliff again, but it didn't drift this time; he had it on a short line now, the both of us did.

"It is worse than I said, and simpler. The grey is always empty, Tasha. In all the time I have known it — and that is the whole of my time — it has been a still grey nothing with the lived world showing through it like a drowned town through ice. Patient. Untenanted." The wire of his eye flickered. "It is not empty here. It is *draining* — all of it, the whole patient nothing, pouring slow toward that cliff the way a flood finds the one crack in a wall — and it is carrying things with it. Shapes. Far off, and dim, and being drawn the way I was being drawn, toward the wall, toward whatever is behind the wall that has begun, already, before he has even arrived, to pull the world apart at its last pin." He stopped.

"And," I said, because there was an *and,* I could hear the shape of the held-back thing.

"And one of them turned." Very quiet. "Out of all that dim drifting cargo of the grey, one shape, far off, in the churn — it stopped being carried, and it turned, against the current, the way a thing turns when it has noticed it is being looked at. Toward me. And I do not know what it was." The eye came to me, and the not-knowing in it was the worst thing I had seen in his face on the whole road, worse than the lowest coal. "I have spent tonight telling myself it was the man from the tor, come early through the grey, because that would at least be a thing I could name. But I am not certain. It was too far, and there was something in the *way* of it that was older than him, older than me, and it knew me, Tasha, the way the man on the tor knew me, that half-knowing, that hearing-a-childhood-tune — except this was not half. This one knew me entirely. And I have never in three hundred thousand spins been known entirely by anything, and I find I did not like how much I wanted to go to it." The bellows, again, ragged. "That is why I needed the rope. Not the pull of the grey. The pull of being known. I would have gone to it. You stopped me going to it."

I kept hold of his forearm. The fire had burned down and neither of us fed it.

"Then I'll keep the rope," I said. "However many nights it takes. You don't look into that thing without me on the other end of you. That's the deal now. You don't get to be known to death by something in the dark while I'm asleep ten steps away."

He looked at me for a long moment, the eye steadying toward something like its proper light.

"That is a great deal to take on," he said, "for a girl who followed me out of an inn because she was bored."

"Yeah, well." I looked out at the rearranging dark, the field that wouldn't hold still, the cliff that was quietly drinking the grey and everything in it. "I'm not bored now."

We kept the watch. Out past the firelight the book turned its pages where we couldn't see, and far behind the cliff the grey poured toward its ending, carrying its dim cargo of shapes, one of which had turned to look at a thing made of wood and known it entirely — and on the lived side of it all, a girl held a construct's forearm with both hands and talked, every time the eye began to drift, about a sign that said *Only*, and a stew nobody should have been proud of, and all the small unfinished things that are, when you are trying to keep someone in the world, the whole and only argument for staying in it.
