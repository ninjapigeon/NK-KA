# Sixteen

The talk had a way of stopping when the country insisted, and the country insisted that afternoon — off to the spireward side of our line, down in a grassy hollow we'd not have to cross, close enough to read and no closer. The smell reached us first; I was learning to read it before I saw its cause. The grass down there was churned dark and strewn with the dead: a whole pack of Herdukii, eight or ten of the wolf-things, torn apart and flung wide — and among them, harder to look at, two or three who had not been wolves. A Humin. A Kro. What was left of them was mixed through what was left of the pack, as though the same hand had pulled all of them to pieces and not troubled to sort the kinds. A cookpot lay canted in the mud a little apart from the rest, coals long gone cold and scattered, as though whatever had come through here had wanted that most of all. There was metal down there too: a good blade snapped clean, a shirt of mail opened like a husk and pitted where something had gripped it, untouched otherwise. Nothing had eaten any of it. That was the part that turned my stomach worst — a pack of Ravagers and the folk who'd died fighting them, all of it killed and none of it carried off, just opened and dropped and left for the flies.

I had stood at the edge of a place like this once, after. I never went out into it; I only saw it from the inn door, before Tondi pulled me back. My body remembered the shape of it anyway — a place where something had come through and unmade everyone in it. The warm easy knot we'd walked in all morning came apart, and nobody had to say why. Roan had gone the colour of tallow. Even Mathen was quiet. It was Roan who finally spoke, in a voice working hard to stay level. "Could you have come out of that?" He didn't say the rest of it aloud — whether Third, who declined every fight there was, would have walked clear where those people hadn't — but it was under the question, and the three of us waited on the answer.

Third looked the hollow over like a notice nailed to a post. "A flailing dreadnought made this," he said, the way you'd name a weather. "They are drawn to cooked food — smoke, rendered fat, the smell of a working kitchen carried on the wind. No one is certain why; the likeliest guess is that the Titan built them to find armies, and an army in the field always smells the same, however you dress it. The pack will have caught the travellers first, or found them already fighting the wolves; the dreadnought will have smelled someone's supper a day or two back and only now arrived, walked into the middle of the disagreement, and ended it on everyone at once." The eye came to rest on Roan. "I do not eat. I have cooked for others. I have never lit one for my own benefit in my life. It would have had small reason to mark me, even standing in the middle of it." A pause, while that settled into the unsettling thing it was. "These will have cooked, somewhere back along this stretch — dried meat softened over coals, a pot, something ordinary. That is what called it down, a day late and indifferent to whose fire it had followed. We will put more ground between us and this place before we light anything, and keep what we do light small and short-lived after that." He turned us back toward our line. "It is gone, in any case. It does not linger once it has fed, or once a trail runs cold." Which was meant as a comfort, and was, a little, and was also the worst thing he could have said: that a thing could do all this and walk on — not from cruelty, but from having finished an errand.

We walked well past where we'd normally have stopped, further than any of us said out loud we wanted to, before Third finally let us make camp — a glade well off the trail, the fire kept low and banked, more coals than flame, far enough from the road and far enough from the hollow behind us that neither smoke nor light would carry to either. Mathen had cooked something involving the dried mushrooms he'd been hoarding since the last town and refused to explain, and it had been better than it had any right to be, and now he was on his back with his hat over his face making the sounds of a man pretending to be asleep while actually listening to everything. Roan had drawn first watch and was sitting at the edge of the firelight with his back to us, doing it properly.

I'd asked Third a question I'd been carrying since the inn. I wasn't sure what made this the right moment — something about a full stomach and a fire and the particular quality of quiet that settles over a camp when the immediate work is done and the next immediate work hasn't started yet. It felt like a moment when a person might answer something honestly rather than usefully.

Third was not a person. But I was beginning to think the distinction mattered less than I'd assumed.

"How did it start?" I'd asked. "Knowing you were — you."

He was quiet for long enough that I thought he wasn't going to answer. Then he began.

The dust fell like frost — except that it was red. A fine red ash, the colour of the inside of a living thing, drifting down over the whole field in the heat of a cloudless day; and where it settled on me it was cold, the cold of a morning I had never had to wake to. One moment I was moving through the field with purpose. Then all of that was still there — the noise, the movement, the weight of my glaive — and also, now, something else. Something looking at it.

The field was still loud. This was important.

The Virek were not cheering at a safe distance. They were advancing — through the settling dust, across the churned ground, past my brothers who had not moved when the dust touched them. My brothers were still standing in attack stance, waiting for an order that was not going to come. The Virek understood this before I did. The spellfire was already exploding — those impossible multicoloured balls invoked from their sorcerers' line, bursting in chaotic patterns among the stunned Forged, and when they appeared there was an inverted absence of sound and then nothing where my brothers had been.

One landed close. Close enough that I felt the edge of it.

So I ran.

Not toward anything. I had been aware of myself for perhaps ten seconds and I had already learned that I did not want to cease, which was itself a discovery. I ran the way fire spreads — along whatever path offered the least resistance — and an arrow found my arm somewhere in the running and I noted it and kept moving and the Virek line was between me and the forest and then it wasn't, because I did something I did not have a name for yet.

I was somewhere else.

<p align="center">&#42; &#42; &#42;</p>

Third paused here, and I watched him across the fire. His eye had not moved from the middle distance — not looking at anything in the camp, looking at the memory of a sky that no longer existed.

<p align="center">&#42; &#42; &#42;</p>

The somewhere else was grey. No ground that felt like ground. No sky with any intention of being a sky. The world I had just left was visible as hazy shapes through the grey — the Virek as dimly glowing outlines, the spellfire moving through the mist like slow light through water.

Behind me, enormous, a shadow with weight.

I turned.

The Titan filled the space that space occupied. I will not try to explain the scale of it. An ornately carved spear protruded from its side. The spear was larger than most buildings I would later see, and the wound it made looked like the kind of wrong that does not heal.

I had not known the Titans could be wounded.

"You may have been forged by another," it said, "but my essence has awoken you. Go. Tell the rest how to find me. I will no longer enter your world."

The voice was not a sound. It arrived the way weather arrives — not from a direction, simply present. It said another, as though there had been more than one of it. I believed that then, the way everyone believes it. I have had a long time since to stop being sure. I looked at the wound. I looked at the spear. I did not look at either for long, because looking at them felt like standing at the edge of what had been happening to my brothers, and I had just decided I did not want that.

I ran again, and the grey did not take kindly to running. That was the only name I had for it then, and the truest one I have found since; the formal word — Akna, Anka inverted, solid set against not — I would not learn for a long time. The mist of it moved with me and against me at once. The shapes of the world showed through it like images in cloudy water, and as I ran the whole grey plane began to shake, subtly at first and then less subtly, the droning at the bottom of its silence becoming something with texture and mass, everything smearing into streaks —

I stumbled.

And I was back. Trees. Actual trees. Behind me, much further than I had expected, the plain where the battle had been. Ahead of me, through the forest, the sound of water.

Here Third stopped again, and when he continued his voice had shifted — not softer, exactly, but slower. Roan, at the edge of the firelight, had gone very still. Under his hat, Mathen was no longer making the sounds of pretend sleep.

<p align="center">&#42; &#42; &#42;</p>

I walked to the water. A brook. Small and clear and entirely indifferent to what had just ended. I crouched at the edge and put my hand in and held it there, and the cold of it moved against my palm, and I stayed until the battlefield sounds were entirely replaced by the sounds of a world that had not noticed, and was continuing regardless.

Then the boy stood up out of the reeds with his bow drawn.

He'd been resting, or hiding — both, probably. He was young. Perhaps 4,500 spins in the reckoning I would later learn. His arms were shaking, but he had asked a question rather than releasing the arrow, and I found that more interesting than the arrow would have been.

"What are you doing?" he said.

"I do not know," I said. It was the first true thing I had ever said. Before that I had only said things that were accurate, which is different. "What am I?"

He stared. "Blank. You're what we call a Blank."

I looked at my glaive. Then at his bow. The bow was exquisitely made — someone had spent a long time on it, cared about every part of it — and looking at it I understood something about what awareness had brought me that purpose had not. The ability to notice that a thing had been made with care. To find that significant rather than tactical.

I opened my hands. The glaive fell forward and stood briefly blade-first in the grass before settling.

"I am created to destroy your kind," I said. It seemed important to be accurate. "What are you created for?"

He stepped back. "We were created as playthings for the Titans. But we will overthrow them and make our own place."

I looked at the glaive in the grass. I looked at his bow.

"Then I make my own place too," I said. "The Titan I served is already overthrown."

I turned and walked downstream. Behind me I heard the tension leave his bow — the sound of a decision not made. I did not look back.

I had not gone far when I heard him say, quietly, as though to himself:

"A Blank just used *I*?"

<p align="center">&#42; &#42; &#42;</p>

Third's eye moved back to the fire. Whatever the middle distance had held, it was done holding it.

I realised I'd been holding my breath somewhere in the middle of all that, and quietly let it out. Across the camp, Mathen had pushed his hat up off his face and was looking at the sky with the expression of a man revising several assumptions about the world. Roan had turned around at some point. He was looking at Third with the expression of a man who has been brave all his life without ever having understood what bravery was measured against.

Third picked up a stick and adjusted the fire. The coals shifted. Sparks rose and went out.

"That," he said, "is how it started."

Nobody said anything for a while.

It was Mathen who spoke first, which was right, because Mathen always spoke first when silence needed breaking and somehow always found the right thing. He put his hat back over his face.

"Hell of a first day," he said.

Third made a sound that was not quite a laugh, from somewhere inside the bellows of his chest. It was the first time I had heard him make that sound.

"Yes," he said. "It was."
