# Seven

We got the traveller into one of the rooms upstairs, and he was asleep before Klar had finished setting down the second cup of water, the deep instant sleep of a body that has been refusing itself for too long. Tondi had looked at the wound on his arm and made the face she made when something was going to need more attention than it was going to get, and then she'd dressed it properly anyway, because that was the difference between Tondi and her reputation.

When she came back down, the inn had emptied of its few midday customers. It was just the four of us. Me. Tondi in her corner. Roan, who had come in off his rest and who had been standing by the door for the better part of an hour with the expression of a man who has not been invited to sit but does not want to leave. And Third, who had put the tablecloth back on without being asked, and who now sat at the corner table with his one blue eye on the middle distance, waiting, the way he waited for everything.

"All right," I said, and sat down across from him. "You said now. So tell us. All of it."

The bellows drew. Once. Then he began.

"I have been following this for some time," Third said. "Long enough to be certain it is not chance. Towns along the trails — small ones, mostly, the kind that do not appear on a trader's map and are not missed when they cease to appear anywhere at all. Each one struck. Each one to the same end result. Ravagers in numbers they do not naturally gather in, directed against the settlement all at once; rivers with no history of flooding, overflow their banks only on the village-side; giant creatures attacking a town when normally they wander the woods. And then, after, something walking, or floating, through the wreckage. Searching. Taking one thing and leaving everything else, including the survivors and wealth, untouched."

"The thing the traveller saw," Roan said, from the door.

"Yes."

"With the lights on its back."

"Yes." Third's eye moved to Roan — the glow sliding across the room, unhurried, the head following. "magefire. A great many bottles of it, more than I have ever seen one body carry. I do not know why it needs so much. I know that it does."

I thought about the traveller's words — a bottled storm, a whole rack of them — and I thought about how Third had gone still when he heard it, and I made myself ask the question I had been not-asking since midday.

"You were there," I said. "At the town with the Skybarge. You're the one who argued the ramp open."

The inn was very quiet. Roan looked at me, then at Third.

"Yes," Third said. No weight on it. No reluctance. He stated it the way he stated the hour. "The master of the barge — a Hin called Waj Hool — did not wish to descend into a Ravager assault. This was a reasonable position. I persuaded him that it was less reasonable than the alternative." A pause. "I was the last aboard. I prefer to keep a Ravager pack in front of me where I can account for it."

"You walked up backward," Roan said, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard from him before. Not quite awe. Closer to recognition.

"It is simply sound practice," Third said, which I was learning was the thing he said when the truer answer was something he did not have words for.

Tondi, in her corner, had not picked her mending back up. "You saved a town," she said. "And you did not stay. You came here instead."

"I saved the people of a town. The town itself is gone." The eye moved to her. "And I came here because the thing doing this moved anti-spinward, and anti-spinward of that town, on the trail it is following, is here. And then beyond here. I did not come to your town by accident, mender. I came because I believe it is in the path."

I felt that land in my stomach, cold and specific. "You think it's coming here."

"I think it is going somewhere, and that the line it is travelling passes near enough to here that I would not wish to be elsewhere when it does." He let that sit a moment. "But your town is not the destination. Of that I am reasonably certain. Your town has nothing it wants."

"And what does it want?" Tondi asked. "You keep saying a thing. The thing takes one object and leaves the rest. What is the object?"

This was the question. I could feel it was the question, the way you can feel the weight of a room turn toward a single point. Third was quiet for long enough that I thought, again, he might not answer. Then the bellows drew a second time — the second, I noted, the last one I would get from him tonight — and he set both his bifurcated hands flat on the table, which I had never seen him do, and which had the effect of making the wooden parody of a humin look, for a moment, like a man gathering himself to say a hard thing plainly.

"You know that the Titans made the world," he said. "This is told to children. It is also true, which is rarer than you would think for a thing told to children." The eye dimmed slightly — consideration, not grief. "What the children's telling leaves out is how. The Titans did not make the world by wishing it. They made it the way anything is made. With tools. With instruments. Things to cut with, and shape with, and bind one thing to another. Vast things, suited to vast hands."

"Tools," Roan repeated.

"When the Titans fell, the tools did not fall with them. They remained. They are scattered across all of Anka, and most of them are not recognised for what they are. A piece of titanic forge tongs, used as a tower shield; a town built around a bowl-shaped depression that nothing grows within, but the surrounding area is consistently warm; three gears that placed in proximity to each other begin rotating without external power. People walk past these instruments of creation every day and call them landmarks, or curiosities, or hauntings, and they are not wrong to, because the truth is not available to them and the landmark serves them better." The eye came back to me, bright again. "But someone has learned to recognise them. And someone is gathering them."

I had grown up in a world with a wound in its sky and most doors on the second floor and a spire on the horizon that everyone navigated by and no one questioned. I had never once thought of any of it as a thing somebody made. It had simply always been the shape of things. To have Third say it plainly — that the Spire was a tool, that the world was a workshop someone had walked away from — did something to the floor of my understanding that I did not enjoy.

"Why," I said. "Why gather them. What do you do with a — with the tools that made the world?"

"That," Third said, "is what I do not know. Not with certainty." And I believed him, because Third did not pretend to certainties he lacked, which was rarer and more valuable than I had words for at the time. "But I have a fear. The tools that made a thing are the tools that can make it whole again — not mend it, the way you'd mend a chair, but make it, entire, a second time, out of whatever is left of it. And the largest thing that was ever unmade—" "Was a Titan," Tondi said. Quiet. She had got there a breath before I did.

"They made the world," Third said. "They made me, too, in their way — I am one of their tools. I watched it come apart: not into a body you could bury, but into red dust, and into something else — something that went sideways out of the world, and did not entirely leave it… It scattered. And if someone is gathering the tools of creation, then the question I cannot put down is whether they mean to gather a fallen Titan back — to make it whole out of its dust and its absence, and call it down off the far side of the world into this one. To undo the one thing the Virek spent an entire people to do." Nobody said anything.

The fire ticked. Upstairs, faintly, the traveller turned over in his sleep and was quiet again.

"You don't know that's what it's doing," I said, finally. I don't know why I said it. Maybe because someone had to put a hand against the size of the thing. "You said yourself. You don't know."

"I do not," Third agreed. "I may be wrong. I hope to be wrong. I have been wrong before, about smaller things, and the relief was considerable." The eye found me. "But I have learned that when I cannot rule out the worst version of a thing, it is unwise to plan for the comfortable one. So I am going to find whoever is doing this. And I am going to learn what they want. And if it is what I fear, I am going to stop them, because the last time this was necessary it required an entire people, and that people is gone, and so it appears the task has fallen to whoever is still standing near it." A pause. "Which, at present, is me."

"And us," Roan said.

He said it from the door, and then looked faintly astonished that he had said it, the way you look when a thing you had been deciding turns out to have already been decided somewhere below the place where you do your deciding.

Third turned his head toward him. The whole head.

"That is not a small thing to offer," Third said. "I would have you understand what you are offering before you offer it."

"I heard you," Roan said. "Towns that don't make the maps. People walked past like they weren't worth turning a head for. I'm militia in a town on a trail. If that thing's path runs through here, then standing at the gate with a spear isn't going to be the brave choice. It's just going to be the choice where I die at the gate." He swallowed. "I'd rather go meet it as someone who chose to. With someone who knows what it is."

I looked at Roan, who did not drink for courage, and who had just talked himself into the end of the world with his back against the inn's door, and I understood that whatever happened next, I was not going to be able to watch it from here. That had stopped being available to me somewhere around the word now.

"How soon," I said, for the second time in two days. But this time it wasn't really a question about Third's schedule.

Third's eye came to me, and the glow did the thing I would come to know as close as he came to gentleness.

"That," he said, "is up to you. But I would leave at first light."
