# Thirty One

Grief in a party of three is a different animal from grief alone. Alone, you can let it have all of you; there's no one watching to be frightened by how much. With two others, you have to keep enough of yourself back to be a person they can stand near, and the keeping-back is its own exhaustion, on top of the grief, which does not take kindly to being kept back and finds other ways out.

We were very bad at it, the day after. We were bad at it because the person who would have been good at it, who would have known exactly the right thing to say and the right moment to say nothing and the right way to put a bowl down by your hand so that you ate without deciding to — that person was ash now, and his absence was loudest precisely in the places he used to fill. The silences he'd have broken sat there unbroken. The food I made was just food, fuel, grey in the mouth, and I understood for the first time that Mathen cooking well had never really been about the cooking; it had been about giving us a thing to look forward to three times a day on a road that otherwise offered nothing to look forward to at all. He had been feeding us hope in the shape of stew. I couldn't do it. I didn't have the recipe.

Roan came apart quietly, which is the way the strong ones do it. He did his watches and ate what was put in front of him and answered when spoken to, and somewhere behind his eyes a door had shut, and I watched him standing at the rim of the field that second evening looking out at nothing with the specific stillness of a young man doing arithmetic he doesn't want the answer to. I knew the arithmetic, because I was doing it too, and it wasn't hard to write out his version. *We're three. Mathen was worth any two of us in a fight and the thing took him in a breath, before he could lift the blade he was the best with. We cannot win this. Whatever Tasha and the Forged know that I don't, it did not save Mathen, and it will not save me.* And then the line his sums had that mine didn't: *I am young — too few spins behind me to have learned how to spend the rest of them well — and I do not want to be a pile of ash on a rock at the edge of the world.* So I went and stood beside him, and I gave him the thing nobody had given me, because I'd had no one to give it: the door, held open.

"You can go, Roan."

He didn't look at me. "Don't."

"I mean it. I'm not — this isn't a test, it's not me checking are-you-brave. You've nothing left to prove to me; I watched you stand still on that field with a Grinding Teeth cloud an arm's length off your face, I know what you've got." I kept my voice level, which took some doing. "But this is going to get worse, and it's not your fight. You came out because a Forged needed a head brought back and it turned into something nobody could have signed up for. Mathen would skin me if I let you die here without telling you, out loud, that you are allowed to walk away. You go home. You guard a gate. You get old. Nobody — not me, not Third, not him, wherever he is — nobody thinks one thing less of you. That's true and I need you to have it."

He was quiet a long while. The Scar's dark edge was coming up over the field. Out in it a far slab went over with a crash, and neither of us flinched, which tells you something about what half a tenday at the Graveyard does to a person.

"Is it worse than you're saying," he asked. "What's coming."

"Yes."

"Can you tell me how?"

I thought about it. About what Third had laid on me on the slab — the door, the offer, the dead held warm, the cruellest job in the world. It was not mine to hand to Roan, and it was too heavy to hand to anyone you were also telling to feel free to leave. "No," I said. "Not all of it. Some of it's Third's to tell or not. But the short of it is: there's a thing coming that wants something only Third can give it, and it's going to offer him the world to get it, and my job — the reason I can't take the door I'm offering you — is to make sure he doesn't give it. That's the whole of what I am here to do now. Stand between Third and the thing he's going to want more than anything. So no. I don't get to leave. But you do."

Roan turned and looked at me then, and the door behind his eyes was open again, just a crack, the arithmetic abandoned.

"Mathen didn't leave," he said. "He could've, loads of times. He stayed because — " Roan's jaw worked. "He stayed because of you two. Because you don't leave the people. He told me that once, not in those words, he never said anything in those words, but that's what it was. You tend the people. That's the — that's the whole job, he said. Everything else is just where you happen to be standing while you do it." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, rough, done with the coming-apart. "I'm not going. Not because I'm brave. I worked out on that field that I'm brave enough, which turns out to be a smaller and worse thing than I thought it'd be. I'm staying because you two are the people, now, and I'm not going to be the one who leaves them. That's it. That's the whole of my reason and it's not a grand one and I don't care."

And there it was — Mathen, sitting up in him, wearing his face, saying his things in not-his-words. I'd buried a man yesterday and here he was today, transplanted whole into a frightened brave boy who hadn't even noticed it happen. I didn't tell Roan. You don't tell someone they've started carrying your dead; they find it heavy enough without knowing what it is.

"Okay," I said. "Then you're staying. And Roan—" I made myself say it, because Mathen would have, because somebody had to be the one who said the warm thing now and the job had apparently fallen to me. "He'd be unbearable about this. He'd have a whole speech. He'd make you cry and then make you laugh and you'd hate him for it."

Roan laughed, once, wet and surprised, exactly the way Mathen had laughed at the end, and I had to look away at the field for a moment.

"Yeah," he said. "He would've."

Third I found where he always was now, at the very lip of the rim, as close to the cliff as he let himself get, watching the thing he would not walk into anymore.

He had not gone into the grey since that one time after Mathen. I knew what it was costing him — that the grey had been the one place he could set the weight down, visit the warm parts, and now he wouldn't, both because it would take him and because he could no longer trust what he found there to be his dead and not their wearer. He was carrying all of it awake now, all the time, with no relief, the way the rest of us carry our griefs, except he had three hundred thousand spins of them stacked up and no end coming to set them down at. I had started to understand that this — the not-going, the carrying-awake — was its own slow dying, and that he'd chosen it for us, and that the choosing was using him up.

I could see the using-up. When I came alongside him the seam down his chest caught the last of the light, and through it, faint, I could see the two small lights he kept in there, and the dim one was dimmer. Not gone. But lower than the geysers, lower than yesterday, a wick guttering toward a wick-end.

"It's lower," I said. "The light."

"Yes." He didn't pretend not to know which I meant. "The grey pulls harder the longer I stand near it, and standing near it is the whole of my purpose, so. It draws on me. I had perhaps twenty spins in the dim one when we reached the geysers. I have rather fewer now." The coal of his eye stayed on the cliff. "It does not matter, Tasha. Truly. Whatever happens at that cliff happens long before the light does. I mention it only because you asked, and because you have decided to know the true things now, and that is one of them. I am running down. We are all of us running down. Mine is merely visible."

"Don't," I said, and didn't have the rest of the sentence, so I just put my hand on his forearm, the place that had become mine, where Mathen's hand had been, where I would put it again when it mattered most.

"He is close," Third said. "The man. The hand of the thing. I cannot look into the grey to count the days, but I do not need to. It pours toward the cliff faster every watch. The field is changing more — you'll have noticed, the cracks come closer together now. Everything is being drawn in. He is coming to the last peg, and we are sitting on it, and it will be soon. A day. Perhaps two." The eye came round to me, and the fear was in it, and under the fear, the thing I had come to trust more than I trusted his strength: the decision, held. "You remember what you promised me."

"I remember."

"You will not believe the version of me that begs."

"I'll believe this one," I said. "The one telling me not to. I've got it written down in here—" I tapped my own skull "—where the begging can't get at it. I'm ready, Third. I've been ready since the slab. Being ready is most of what I do now."

"Yes," he said. "You are very good at it. Better than I am, and I have had longer to practise." Which was the closest Third came to a compliment, and I took it, and we stood the last of the light together looking at the cliff that was drinking the world.

That night the three of us sat the watch all together, no pairs, because what was the point of pairs now, and because none of us wanted to be the one not in the firelight when it came. Roan sharpened things that didn't need sharpening. I told them, badly, the story of the inn and the burglar Third scared off by warning it which way not to break in, and Third corrected three of my details and Roan almost smiled and it was nothing at all like Mathen doing it and it was the best we had and it was, I decided, enough. You do the thing the dead taught you, badly, in your own clumsy hands, because the alternative is to let the thing die with them, and we had buried enough.

Out past the fire the great book turned its pages faster than it used to. The grey poured toward the cliff, carrying its warm and terrible cargo, and somewhere out in the dark a man with six cold lights on his back walked the last of his straight line toward us, toward the last pin in the world and the door that was the only thing left that could open it.

We were three. We were tired. One of us was running down where you could see it and all of us were running down where you couldn't. We had a plan thinner than river ice and a job crueller than any of us had agreed to when we set out, and not one of us was leaving.

I had followed a monster out of an inn because I was bored, and I had found, at the cold edge of the world, the only thing I have ever been certain was worth the whole terrible trip: three of us at a fire, with the end coming, choosing to stay.

Let it come. We were ready. We were as ready as the unready ever get.
