# Twenty Eight

He came to me the next watch with the thing already decided in him. I could tell. After long enough beside a person you learn the difference between the silence of a creature keeping a thing back and the silence of one carrying a thing forward to set down in front of you, and this was the second kind, and it frightened me more than the first ever had, because Third did not set things down lightly. He'd been carrying whatever it was since the night the grey pulled at him, turning it, the way he turned everything, until it was the right shape to hand over.

Mathen and Roan were asleep at the far edge of the firelight. Third sat down beside me — sat, which he did not do, he stood his watches — and laid the Boltcaster across his knees, Dorn's Boltcaster, the way he had at the telling, and I understood I was about to be told something on the same shelf as that, or worse, and I put down the strip of dried fish I'd been pretending to eat.

"I have worked out what I am for," he said. "I would rather you heard it from me, sitting down, tonight, than worked it out yourself in the middle of the thing, when it will matter that you already understood. So I am going to tell you all of it. The part you have been letting lie. And the part I have only just been able to see." The coal of his eye was steady. "It will take a while. Do not say anything kind until I have finished. The kind things are the ones that make me stop."

"All right," I said.

"Her name was Liwon," Third said, and the grey-draining cliff and the rearranging field and the whole waiting world went very far away.

He told it plainly, which was the only way he had, and the plainness was the worst of it, because there is no flinching in a flat voice for you to flinch along with — you have to do all the flinching yourself.

He told me about a village his own kind had emptied, and a green-skinned infant on its back in the blood, too small to run, and the voice in his head that told him to finish it, and the thing he did instead, which was pick her up. He told me about the spins of learning to keep a small soft thing alive when he had been built to do the opposite — the food, the rivers, the chopping of whole glens to splinters when the voice got loud and the crying got louder and he could fix neither. He told me how she named herself, near enough, out of the only thing he ever called her; he had said *little one* a thousand times because it was the only true thing he had, and she had handed it back to him broken into *Liwon*, and he had liked the broken one better, and that was the first time, he said, that he had wanted a thing to be true over wanting it to be accurate, and he had her to thank for the whole of what he became after.

He told me about the Virek woman who took them in, and the spins of being a gate-warden to a village that had every reason to fear him and slowly, mostly, didn't; about Liwon growing into the people who were her own kind and away from the wooden thing that had carried her, which he said was right, which he said he had been glad of, and I believed about half of the gladness, which I think was all he believed of it himself.

And then he told me about the night the light flickered in the trees and the red veins came up over the world, and the Virek were simply *gone* — and in their place the two peoples you and I have walked among our whole lives, the pale ones and the green-toothed ones, the Kro and the Ork, made in a single instant out of the people who had been there a breath before. He told me it as a thing that had happened to the whole world — the sundering, the un-making of the first people into the two — and as a thing that had happened to one child on one floor, both at once, because they were the same thing, the great history and the small grief, one event wearing two sizes. He found her, he said. Or he found the two girls she had become, dying, one pale and one green, on the floor and beyond the window. And one of them, with the last of her, made a sound he knew. *Bada.* The thing she'd said when she was frightened, when she was small, when she wanted the wooden thing to come.

"And that," Third said, "is the someone. That is the her. You asked, on a different watch, and I gave you the one word, and you were kind enough not to take the rest. I am giving you the rest now, because you are going to need it. Not to grieve with me. I am not telling you so that you will grieve with me." The eye came round. "I am telling you so that you will know exactly what it is, when the time comes, that you are going to have to refuse to let me have."

I didn't understand yet. I almost did. I sat very still and let him bring it the rest of the way.

"The thing in the grey," he said. "The one that turned, on the watch. That knew me entirely. I told you I did not know what it was, and that was true when I said it. I know now." The Boltcaster sat under his stilled hands. "It is the one that made me. I have heard it twice before — once when the last of them fell and it looked at me and *smiled,* before it came apart into the dust that woke me; and once on the floor of that village, over Liwon, when it told me her dying was a seed, that it must go into the ground to become something better, that the loss was a kindness I did not yet have the wisdom to see. The same voice. Patient. Gentle. It has always been gentle with me, Tasha. I took it, all this time, for the carelessness of a vast thing toward a small one. I had it backwards. It was not careless of me. It was *courting* me. It has been courting me since before there were towns."

"Courting you to do what," I said, though the cold coming up my spine had already half-answered it.

"To come home. To stop." The eye dimmed, not to grief — to something more frightening, a kind of longing held at arm's length. "I am a made thing, woken by its own essence. I can walk the grey, which nothing else of this world can do unaided — nothing but the man on the tor, who needs a word for it and uses the word the way I use nothing, who has spent these spins gathering the pins that hold the world pegged to its place. I run on its blood. I am, Tasha — I have been trying to find a gentler word and there is not one — I am the *door.* The last thing it needs to come back through is a thing that belongs to both sides at once and will *open.* The pins are nearly all pulled. The grey is draining toward the last of them, behind that cliff. And the door is sitting on a fallen slab telling a girl who serves stew that he has finally understood what he is for, and what he is for is to *want to go.*"

The fire ticked. Out in the dark a page of the book turned over with a crack, and for once I'd have welcomed any noise that wasn't his voice.

"It cannot take me," he said. "That is the one mercy in the shape of it. It needs the door opened from the inside. It needs me *willing.* And so it will not seize me — it will *offer.* It will offer me the thing it has been saving the whole of my life to offer, the thing it showed me a corner of on the watch and I nearly walked into the grey to reach." His hand turned over, the old tell, closing and opening on nothing. "The grey keeps the warm part, Tasha. I have told you that. The warm part, before the end. It will offer me *all of them.* Not a visit. Not a corner I climb back out of lessened. It will offer me Liwon — and Dorn, and Adura, and the woman in the snow who told me to go on living — kept, whole, in the warm, forever, on the only condition that I stop holding the door shut. That I come home and be known and never be alone again and have every single one of them back." The coal guttered. "And in time, it will be able to offer me you."

I had thought, when I started down this road out of an inn because I was bored, that the worst thing that could happen to me was dying on it. I want to put on the record that I was a child when I thought that, whatever my age, and that I stopped being one somewhere in the next little while, sitting on a fallen page of stone listening to the kindest creature I have ever known explain to me that he was going to be offered his murdered daughter back and that I was the thing that had to say no.

"You can't refuse it," I said. My voice did something I wasn't proud of. "If it offers you that. Third. Nobody could refuse that. *I* couldn't refuse that, and I've known her for the length of one conversation."

"I know." He said it without any drama at all, which was how I knew it was the truest thing he'd said. "That is the difficulty. The woman in the snow — Lujian — gave me the only answer I have, long ago, for exactly this, though neither of us knew it was for this. She said the loss was *hers.* Liwon's. That it was not a seed and not a kindness and not for anything, that it was a person, and that the only way left to call it what it was, was to refuse to let it be made into a meaning. To go on. That stopping agreed with the liar." The eye came up, and there was something in it I had no name for, something older than the road. "If I take the offer, Liwon's death becomes the seed the Titan always said it was. The thing that softened me, and emptied me, and made me lonely enough to come home. Every death it ever arranged or allowed becomes a step on the path to opening the door. I would be agreeing, at the last, that the liar was right. I will not do that. I have decided. I am telling you on the night I have decided it, while I can still mean it cleanly."

"Then what do you need me for," I said, and I already knew, and I needed him to say it anyway.

"Because deciding it tonight is not the same as holding it then." For the first time the flat voice cracked, one hairline, and set again. "Because there will be a moment — at the cliff, in the thick of it, with the warm part open in front of me and her *in* it — when every part of me that is worth anything will want to go, and will believe, with total conviction, that going is right, that it is even good, that it is the loving thing, and I will turn to you, Tasha, and I will ask you with everything I have to let me go to her." The Boltcaster trembled, faintly, under his hand. "And you must not. That is the whole of it. That is what I am for and that is what *you* are for, the two of us, at the end of this road. I am telling you now, with the clean version of myself, so that you will know to distrust the other one. When I beg you — and I will beg you — you are to believe *this* me. Not that one. The me that is asking you, now, calmly, to refuse the me that will be on his knees. Can you —" "Yes," I said. I didn't let him finish it. I couldn't have stood the shape of the whole question in the air. "Yes. I'll hold the rope. I'll say no. I'll be the worst thing that ever happened to you, right at the end, and I'll do it on purpose, and I'll hate it more than anything I've ever done." I was crying by then, which I'd sworn at the inn I would never do in front of any of them, my father's voice in my head the whole time, *never let them see.* I let him see. "But you have to know what you're asking. You're asking me to look at you wanting her — *her,* Third, your little one — and tell you no. You're asking me to be the reason you don't get her back."

"Yes," Third said.

"That's the cruellest thing anyone has ever asked me."

"Yes," he said again. "I am sorry. There is no one else. You are the loudest thing in any quiet I have ever stood in, and you are the only one who has ever held me to the world hard enough to bring me back across it, and I would not ask it of you if the world did not, regrettably, depend on a girl who served stew being willing to break my heart at precisely the right moment." The eye found mine, steady now, the longing folded back away behind it. "I have great confidence in you. It is the only confidence I have left that is not also a fear."

We sat there a long time, on the fallen page, with the cliff drinking the grey and the dead held warm behind it and the whole impossible weight of what I now knew sitting in my lap like a stone, and I did not let go of his forearm, and he did not ask me to, and neither of us said anything else kind, because the kind things were the ones that made him stop, and I had finally, fully, terribly understood that stopping was the one thing in all the world I was now sworn to never, ever let him do.

That was the night I knew. All of it. The shape and the centre both.

I have not been bored since. I would give a great deal, now, to be bored again.
