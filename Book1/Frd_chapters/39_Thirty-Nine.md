# Thirty Nine

The world did not feel saved. I want to put that down plainly, because the stories will say we saved it and the word will do what words do, it will round everything off smooth, and there was nothing smooth about that rock in the grey light with the cold man gone and the three of us still on it, breathing, which is a different and much smaller thing than *saved.* The world felt the way a room feels after someone has been very ill in it and the fever has finally broken: not triumphant. Just quiet, and wrung out, and aware of how close the other thing had come.

I went to Roan first, because Roan was the one making noise, and noise meant alive.

He'd got himself half-propped on one elbow by the time I reached him, and he was swearing — a low steady creative stream of it, the kind you only manage when the hurt is real but not the worst kind, the kind that's furious to still be here rather than grateful — and when he saw my face he stopped and tried to rearrange his own into something braver, and failed, and I loved him for the failing.

"Don't move," I said, getting my hands on him. "Let me see."

"It threw me," he said, as if I hadn't watched. "It didn't even — Tasha, it didn't even *touch* me. It said a *word.*" His outrage at this was total and somehow the most healing thing in the world. A man does not waste breath being indignant about the rules of his injury unless he intends to live to complain about it properly later.

His leg was wrong below the knee — not bone-through-skin wrong, but wrong, already swelling, the kind of wrong that would make the walk out a long ugly business. Two of his ribs grated under my hands in a way that made us both hiss. There was blood drying at the corner of his mouth and a deeper greyish cast to his skin that I didn't like and didn't have a name for, the toll of having been flung so near a wall he was never meant to touch. But his eyes tracked. His swearing scanned. His hand, when I gripped it, gripped back hard enough to grind my knuckles.

"You're going to be unbearable about this," I told him, which was the truest comfort I had. "You threw yourself at a Forged and got cast off by a magic word and lived. You'll dine out on it for the rest of your life."

"Mathen," Roan said, and his voice cracked clean in half on it, "would've gotten a *tenday* of drinks."

"A hundred spins," I said. "Easy."

And we both looked away from each other for a moment, because that was as close as either of us could go to him just then, and it was enough, and it was the first time we'd been able to go even that close since the Grask.

Third had not moved.

He stood where he'd turned to face Sougad, a little apart, and he was looking at the place the grey had been — the ordinary rock, the ordinary low troughs, nothing standing up out of any of it — and I knew, the way I'd learned to know him, that he was not looking at the rock. He was looking at where she had been. At the empty place he had chosen to keep empty. The lights in his chest had gone back to their small steady burning, the dim one no worse than before, no better; the make of him was intact; he had taken no wound that he could repair or that anyone could see.

He had taken the other kind.

I left Roan propped and bound as well as I could manage and went and stood beside Third, not touching him, just there, the way he'd stood beside me a hundred times in the dark and let his being-there be the whole of what he offered. For a long while he said nothing. The Scar wheeled. Somewhere far off across the field a slab went over with a crash, because the place was still the place; the world had gone back to being only ordinarily terrible.

"I had her," he said at last. Not to me. To the empty rock. "For the length of a goodbye, I had her back, exactly as she was, and she was holding up her arms. And I gave her to the dark with my own hands so that he—" the eye moved, a fraction, toward Roan "—could keep being a boy who complains about magic words. I want you to understand that I do not feel I did a great thing, Tasha. I feel I did a murder. I murdered the chance to have my daughter, and I did it on purpose, and the only thing I can say for myself is that I would do it again, which is not the same as being glad, and I am so tired of people thinking those are the same."

"I don't think they're the same," I said.

"No," he said. "You don't. It is why I could bear for you to be the one who saw it." A pause. "Thank you for not telling me it was worth it. Everyone is going to tell me it was worth it."

"It wasn't worth it," I said. "It was just necessary. Those aren't the same either."

The single light of his eye came round to me, and held, and something in the make of him eased a fraction — not healed, nothing was healed, but eased, the way a weight shifts to a part of you better made to carry it.

"No," he said softly. "They are not. You have gotten very good at the true things, Tasha. I am sorry it was my company that taught you."

He saw the Boltcaster then. It was still in my hand. I had not been able to make myself put it away, and I realised, looking down at it, that my fingers had cramped around it, that I had been holding it that hard for that long, and that the safety of it — the loaded, ready, never-drawn weight of it — was the only thing my body had let itself do with the thing it could not do.

"You did not use it," Third said.

"No."

"When I went quiet. When I went to the wall and reached. That was the moment. That was the only moment, the one I drilled into you, and you had it, and you did not use it." He said it without accusation, the way you'd note the weather. "You could not. Could you."

"No." It came out of me in a rush, the confession I'd been dreading, the failure under the relief. "I couldn't. I had it aimed in my head a hundred times those two nights and when it was real my hand just — it wouldn't. It went tighter on your arm instead. I broke the plan, Third. The one thing you built the whole of it on, the failsafe, the precise instrument, *only when there's truly nothing left* — and when there was truly nothing left I screamed for a hurt boy instead of doing the one thing you asked me for. If Roan hadn't— if Sougad hadn't spent him in front of you— the world ends, because I couldn't open my hand."

Third was quiet a moment. Then he did a thing I had never seen him do and never saw him do again: he reached out, slow, with that wrong boneless ease, and he closed his own wooden hand around mine, around the Boltcaster, gently, over the cramp, and held it.

"I chose you," he said, "because you were the one least willing to do it. I told you that. I thought I was choosing the most precise blade — the hand that would wait until the last possible breath because it hated the cutting. I was wrong about what I was choosing. I was not choosing a blade at all. I built a failsafe because I needed to believe there was a bottom under me before I could step out over the drop — and the failsafe I actually built, without knowing it, was a person whose hand would not open on me no matter what. Who would scream for help instead of for an ending. Tasha. The reason you could not use it is the same reason it worked. You did not let go of me. That was always the only thing that was going to hold. Not the threat of the shot. The fact of the grip. I am only sorry I made you carry the shot at all, and I am taking it back now, and you will never have to decide that again."

He took Dorn's Boltcaster out of my cramped hand, as gently as he'd given it, and somewhere in the taking it became a memento again instead of a weapon, and he put it away at his back, and that was the end of the failsafe, which had never once been the thing that saved us.

We did not stay long. You don't, in that country; the place had spared us its worst but it had not become safe, and a slab the size of a barn settling half a field off is a poor lullaby. There was the matter of Roan, who could not walk, and the matter of how far we were from anywhere that wasn't trying to kill us, and the matter of the long impossible distance between a cliff at the cold edge of the world and any roof at all.

Third lifted Roan as easily as he'd once lifted an infant, one arm, settled, and Roan made a noise of protest that turned into a noise of pain that turned into the quiet of a man saving his strength, and we turned our backs on the spine.

I looked back once. The glaive was still standing where Third had leaned it, upright against the pale stone, the wide pale blade he'd carried since before he was anyone.

"You're leaving it," I said.

"It was never mine," said Third, with the boy on his arm. "Let it stand there. Let the next fool who climbs all this way looking for treasure find a door-warden's glaive and no door, and no warden, and let them make of it whatever story keeps them warm on the walk home. Mathen would like that, I think. A thing left behind on purpose, to be wondered at. He always said the wondering was the point."

And we walked out into the open book, the three of us, one of us carried and one of us carrying and one of us holding a rope that had no far end anymore and not yet knowing how to put it down — out across the crawling field toward a horizon that was a very long way away, with no notion in the world of how we were going to cross it, and behind us a glaive stood guard over nothing at all, exactly as it had been made to do, and was, at last, free of us.
