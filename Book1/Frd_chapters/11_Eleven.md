# Eleven

The carts had been on the road a long time.

There was a pile of them, hand-carts of the kind the Hin trader had, overturned and dragged half across the road and left — not recently, the wood gone grey and splitting, smell of it long gone, so that the mind doubted they were really there at all, one wheel still turning a slow finger's-width back and forth when the wind found it. Whatever had happened here had happened to people going the other way, spinward, toward the town and the trader and the ordinary world, and had happened all at once, the way the bad things on this road seemed to.

"Pulled across, deliberate," Roan said, crouching at the nearest. He'd got better at this — at reading a scene before walking into it, at not touching the first thing his hands wanted to touch. "Like a barricade. Someone tried to make a wall of them."

"It didn't work," I said.

"No." He stood. "It didn't."

There was no blood, which somehow was worse than blood would have been; blood at least tells you a story with an ending. This just told you people had been here, and had been afraid enough to throw their whole livelihood into a wall across the road, and then were not here anymore, and the carts had stayed to think about it for a few hundred spins.

The wall of them was too high and tangled to climb cleanly with packs, and the cleared margin on either side dropped off the edge of the Causeway into the held-back forest six men below. Roan and I stood there working out the puzzle of it — over, or down and around, or unstack the topmost cart and risk the whole rotten heap coming down — the ordinary arithmetic of an obstacle, the kind of problem I'd have welcomed an hour earlier for being a problem I could actually solve.

Third had gone quiet beside me. I noticed it the way you notice a sound stopping. He was looking at the wall of carts and not, I thought, really seeing it — the glow of his eye had gone soft and far-off, the way a person's eyes go when they're doing sums in their head or remembering a road, and he had begun, very slightly, to drift. Not toward the carts. Past them, somehow, at an angle I couldn't have drawn. He took a step like a man stepping off a kerb he'd walked a thousand times without looking.

"Third," I said, turning to ask him which way he thought, "do we—"

He wasn't there.

I want to be precise about this, because I have tried since to describe it to people who weren't on that road and have always failed. He had been standing at my shoulder. He did not walk away. He did not flicker or fade or do any of the things a story would give him to do. He simply stepped — sideways, at an angle that my eye refused to hold, the way you can't quite look at the place a reflection comes from — and the step took him out of the world, and the space where he had been was just air, with the dead carts beyond it.

It did not look like a decision. That was the thing I kept, after. It looked like a habit. It looked like the most natural thing in the world for him to do, and the most natural thing in the world is the one you do without remembering you have company.

Roan made a sound. I don't think he knew he made it.

Sideways was grey.

It was always grey, and it was always quiet in the particular way that is not the absence of sound but its opposite, a pressure, a held breath the size of a world. Third had stepped this way more times than he could have counted if counting had mattered here, and it never mattered here. The Causeway hung beside him as a smear of pale light, the carts a darker smudge in it, the held-back forest a green rumour far below. He had only to walk a little way along the grey and step back into the world past the obstruction. Seconds, on the other side. It was, as he would tell the Humin girl when he stepped back, faster.

But the grey did not only carry him forward. It had never only carried him forward. The grey was where the things he did not look at directly went to wait for him, and it was patient, and it was very old, and when he walked through it the old things rose up to walk beside him whether he had asked them to or not.

The carts were overturned. A wall of overturned things, thrown up by the frightened, that had not held.

And he was somewhere else, and it was long ago, and the things thrown down around him were not carts.

The village had been small. He understood that the way he understood everything in those first spins of his thinking — as information, arriving flat and complete, with no one yet home behind it to be moved by it. Small village. Virek. Perhaps forty dwellings, the woven-wall kind the green ones favoured, most of them open now, their walls broken outward.

His single eye moved across it and lit what it found. Blood on a wall, dark and dried at the edges, fresh and wet at the centre, pooled below the nearest body. The body was Virek and had been for a long time and would not be much longer; already the green was going grey. There were a great many bodies. He had counted them before he chose to, the count arriving the way the smallness of the village had arrived, unbidden and exact, and he made himself stop knowing the number because the number had begun to feel like a thing he should not be holding so lightly.

His hand held a glaive. The blade was wet.

He looked at the blade for a long time.

*You are Forged to eliminate the Virek,* the thoughts said. They were not cruel. They had never been cruel. Cruelty would have been easier to set down. They were only certain, the way the ground is certain, the way falling is. *This is the purpose. This is the whole of the purpose.*

There was an arrow in his garb, high on the shoulder, driven through the woven cords that served him for muscle and stopped by the wood beneath. It was a clean arrow, unfired-looking, absurd. And the blood on the cords around it was older than the arrow — he could see that, he could see that the blood had dried before the shaft ever found him, which meant the blood was not from this wound, which meant the blood was from the blade, which meant —

He did not finish the thought. It was the first thing he had ever declined to finish.

He could not tell. That was the truth of it, then and after and for every spin that would ever come after: he stood in the wreck of a village with a wet blade in his hand and blood on his armour that was not his own and he could not tell whether he had been one of the ones who added to the sea of bodies, or had only arrived in time to stand among it. The others like him had done this. He knew that. He had perhaps done this. He would never know, and the not-knowing would walk in the grey with him forever, and it was walking with him now.

Then the crying.

It was small and furious and entirely alive, and it came from a bundle near the broken wall of the nearest dwelling, and when his eye found it the crying did not stop, which was itself a kind of marvel — every other living thing he had come near in those early spins had stopped, had frozen or fled. This one only lay on its back and flailed its small green limbs and howled at the sky as if the sky had personally done this and owed it an answer.

*A Virek,* the thoughts said. *Eliminate it. The purpose is not complete while one remains.*

"It seems immobile," he said. His voice surprised him, in the quiet, among the dead. It always surprised him then. "It cannot have done anything. It cannot even turn over."

*That is not the measure. The measure is what it is, not what it has done.*

He looked at the small howling thing. He looked at the wet blade in his hand. And he watched his own arm — he remembered this exactly, the watching, as though the arm belonged to someone he was only distantly responsible for — reach down, open, and gather the bundle up by its wrappings, the blade forgotten, fallen, thumping once on the bloodied ground.

The wrappings began to tear.

"These will not hold for long," he told it, as though it had asked.

So he knelt among the people he might have killed, and he took the clothing from one of the dead — gently, which no one was there to see, which the thoughts informed him was wasted gentleness, which he did anyway — and he fashioned from it a harness, a sling, the kind he had seen the green ones carry their young in, before. He worked the knots with two fingers and a thumb of each bifurcated hand, slow, clumsy, learning it as he did it.

"This will hold for longer."

He put the child into the sling and drew it against the wooden cage of his chest, against the slow bellows that moved there, and the crying changed. It frayed, and softened, and came apart into long shuddering sobs, and then into something that was almost only breathing.

He went very still.

"That was unexpected," he said. "If I were to guess — you find this comfortable. The sound. The motion of the bellows, perhaps." The thing against his chest hitched once and was quiet. "I did not know I could be of use in that way. I have not, until now, been built for it."

*It is consuming time. It will consume more. Set it down. The purpose —*

"I have heard the purpose," he said, and it was the first time he had answered the thoughts aloud, and the sound of his own refusal in the dead village was the strangest thing that had happened to him since the dust. "I have heard it a great deal. I am choosing, instead, this."

And he turned away from the direction his own kind had gone — he could feel them out there, the unwoken ones, moving on to the next thing their thoughts told them to move on to — and he walked the other way, out of the village, into the trees, with a Virek child breathing against the place where his heart was not.

Behind him in the pale red dust that lay over everything in those spins, the dust that had woken him and had not stopped settling since, he left two cloven prints and the small dragged smudge of a sling, and nothing else, and no name yet for the thing he carried, because he did not yet know it would need one, or that he would be the one to give it, or what it would cost him, in the end, to have been the one who did.

The grey took it back. It always took it back. It gave him these things and then it took them back and left him holding only the walking, which was its single mercy and the reason, he suspected, that he had never stopped.

He stepped sideways, into the world, on the far side of the carts.

He was on the far side of the cart-wall.

Not appearing, exactly — just suddenly accounted for, standing on the swept stone beyond the wreck as though he'd been there the whole time and we'd merely failed to notice him. For a breath he stayed facing anti-spinward, away from us, already half a step into walking on, the way you carry on down a road you've walked alone for longer than there have been roads.

Then he stopped.

I watched it happen — watched the moment the habit let go of him and he remembered. His head came round, and the glow of his eye came back up from wherever it had gone, and he looked at the two of us standing on the wrong side of a wall of dead carts, and something in the set of him changed. He had left us. He had not meant to, and he had done it as easily as breathing, and now he saw that he had done it.

He came back — not sideways, and not over. He did not climb to our side and start the crossing again from where we stood. He stayed where his own habit had carried him, on the far side of the wreck, and he set his hands to the barricade from there, and began, without a word, to take it apart toward us.

It was not the effortless thing a story would have given him. He was stronger than a man — stronger than Roan and me together, I'd have guessed — but he was not a windlass. The carts were heavy, snarled, half-rotted into one another, and he worked them the way a patient person works a knot: a cart rocked up onto its edge and walked round on one corner, a wheel freed and rolled aside, a shaft lifted clear and laid down out of the path. What he had that we did not was not size. It was that he never strained against a thing and gave up, never stopped to breathe, never needed the small rests a body needs. He simply continued, at the same steady rate, until the stone showed through between us.

"That one next," he said, when he reached the rotten cart at the top, the one Roan and I had been afraid to touch. "Take the wheel side. I will take the weight." And he eased it down clear of the path, the two of them, as gently as if it might still have had someone in it.

So we opened the barricade between us, Third from his side and Roan and I from ours, meeting in the middle of it, and none of us spoke of the other thing, the sideways thing, because Roan plainly did not have words for it and I plainly did, and mine were all questions, and there is a time for questions and it is not while you are standing on a road that keeps itself otherwise clear for reasons of its own.

But I looked at him while we worked. I couldn't help it. The glow of his eye had gone down to its lowest ember, the way a banked fire goes, and once — easing a shaft down to the stone — his bellows drew long and slow, the sound it made only at the weights I had learned to watch for, though the shaft was no great burden and nothing we'd handled all day had drawn that sound from him before. He stood a moment when the path was clear, among the carts he'd moved, as though the few grey seconds he'd spent crossing had been a great deal longer than ours, and had asked something of him that the work never could.

"Third," I said. "Are you—"

"I am the same when I return as when I leave," he said. "It costs me nothing you need fear."

It was, I would understand much later — when I knew more than I wanted to and could not give the knowing back — the only thing Third ever told me that I am certain he believed and that was not true.

"We should keep moving," he said, before I could find the shape of any of it. "We are losing the light. And I find I would like to be off this road before dark, today, more than I usually do."

He didn't say why. I didn't ask. We walked on anti-spinward down the swept and watched-over road, the three of us together, and I did not learn for a long time what he had carried across it, or that he had carried it at all.
