# Thirty Three

There is a particular cruelty in knowing exactly how a thing is going to land before you do it, because Third had done it to me, on a slab two nights gone, and I had felt the floor of the world go out from under me one fact at a time, and now I was going to do the same to Roan with the cold man a thousand paces off and closing, and there was no kind way and no slow way and no time for either.

So I did it the way Tondi would have set a broken arm. Fast, and straight, and without asking him to hold still first, because asking only makes them tense.

"Roan. Listen to me and don't say anything till I'm done, because we don't have the breath. You know some of this. Here's the rest."

He looked at me. The spear had stopped its endless re-checking. That was how I knew he'd already understood that the talking part was over and some other part had started.

"The thing under the world wants back in. It can't come on its own — it's too big, it's in pieces, half of it's in the grey. It needs a door. Third is the door. Not because he's willing — because of what he's *made* of, what woke him, the way he walks in both places at once. It can't force the door; that's the one mercy in the whole rotten business. It can only get him to *open* it. From the inside. On purpose." I kept my voice flat, the way you keep a hand flat for a frightened animal. "So it's going to offer him a price. And the price is everyone he's ever lost. All of them. Whole, and warm, and back, forever — Liwon, the swordsman, the woman who was sick, the woman in the snow. Mathen."

That one I watched land. I'm sorry to say I had to. I needed to know he'd heard it.

"Mathen's in there now, Roan. In the warm. The thing has him. And it will hold him up at the end like the last coin in a purse and tell Third *all he has to do is open the door and he gets them all back, Mathen included,* and Third is going to want to. Not might. *Going to.* I've seen what it does to him just standing near it; I watched it nearly take him with my own voice the only thing holding the other end. He is going to stand at that wall and want, with everything three hundred thousand spins has piled up in him, to say yes. And if he says yes, the thing comes through, and there is no more world. Not our lives — the *world.* Everyone's. Everywhere. That's the size of it. That's what we walked out here to stand in front of."

I made myself stop. I let him have the breath I'd told him to save.

He did not bolt. I will love him for that as long as I have left to love anything. The size of it went through him — I watched it go through him, the way a swallow of something too strong goes through you, all the way down, and his face did several things and settled, at the end, on the one I'd hoped for and not dared expect: not the slack of a man who's quit, but the stillness of a man who's decided to be useful with whatever he's got left.

"What do I do," he said. Not *can we win.* Not *are you sure.* What do I do. Mathen, I thought. Mathen all the way down.

"You keep me on him." I turned him by the shoulder so he was facing Third, who stood a little apart, giving us this, watching the cold man come. "That's the whole of your job and it's the worst one and I'm sorry it's yours. Whatever happens, I am the rope. As long as my voice is on Third and my hand is on Third, there's a chance. The thing knows that now. So it's going to try to take me off him. Maybe through the cold man. Maybe—" I made myself say it "—maybe through Third. If it turns him, if it gets him moving, he is stronger than the three of us stacked up and he will come through me to get to that wall and he will not know he's doing it."

"And I stop him." Flat. Already accepting the impossible arithmetic of it.

"You don't *stop* him. You can't. None of us can. You're not meant to." Third's words, from the drill; I gave them to Roan now because they were the truest thing I had. "You're meant to cost it time. You put your body between me and whatever's coming and you make it spend seconds getting through you. Ten breaths. That's the job. Buy me ten breaths to finish refusing, and you'll have done more than any hero in any of Mathen's stories, and most of them only had to die for a *town.*"

He took that in. Then, the boy in him surfacing one last time before whatever he was about to become: "Will it hurt him? If I have to. Will I hurt Third."

And there it was — the thing that told me Mathen had built better than he knew. Roan, told he might have to throw his body in front of a three-hundred-thousand-spin war-construct to buy a tavern girl ten breaths to save the world, and the question he stopped on was whether *the construct* would be hurt by it.

"No," I said, which was a lie, and a small one, and the kind Mathen would have approved of. "You won't be able to hurt him. Just slow him. That's all."

Third came to us then, because the cold man was close enough now that there was no more *here* to be said in, only *now.* He looked at Roan for a long moment with the bright unblinking coal of his eye, and Roan looked back and did not flinch, which still, after everything, was rare.

"I brought you to this," Third said. "You were going to guard a gate and grow old. I am sorry. It is the truest apology I have, and it is not enough, and I am giving it to you anyway because you should have it before, and not after, in case there is no after for the giving."

"You didn't bring me," Roan said. "I came. There's a difference. Mathen taught me that one too."

Something moved in the make of Third — not the bellows, nothing so loud, just a settling, the sound a house makes accepting a weight. "Yes," he said. "There is a difference. Thank you for the difference."

And then he turned to me, and his whole attention came round and landed, and I knew from the quality of it that whatever the three of us had just been was finished, and that the next thing he had to say he had to say to me alone, and that I was not going to like it, and that I was going to have to carry it anyway, because that, in the end, was the difference I had chosen too.
