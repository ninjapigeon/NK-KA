# Nine

The gate was a different thing in the light. At night it had been a threat held off by spikes; now it was just a gap in a wall, with Pek leaning on the frame the way the militia always leaned on things, and the packed earth of the trail running out through it toward a horizon I had looked at my whole life and never once walked into.

There were not many to see us off. That suited everyone. A Forged leaving with two of the town's own was not the kind of thing you gathered to celebrate; it was the kind of thing you watched from a window with the shutter open just enough. I could feel the town doing exactly that around us, the way you can feel a room you've turned your back on.

Third stood a little apart, as he always did, his cloak doing its inadequate work and his one eye already turned toward the trail, toward the part of the morning that had not arrived yet. He had done this before. I understood that without being told — that for Third a gate was a gate, and a leaving was a leaving, and he had crossed more of both than there were names on every memorial pillar in the world. He was waiting for us to be finished being people about it.

Klar came running up just as Pek pushed the gate the rest of the way open.

"You almost left," he said, accusingly, out of breath, shoving a wrapped bundle into my arms. It was heavier than it looked and warm at the bottom. "Travel bread, the dense kind, it keeps. And the rest of the Shredder, smoked — don't make that face, it's good, I brined it. And there's a twist of the blue salt in the middle, don't lose it, that's the expensive one." He stopped, having run out of inventory, and then out of the thing under the inventory, which was the part he didn't have words for. "Just. Eat properly. He won't remind you." He jerked his head at Third. "He doesn't eat."

"I'll eat properly."

"You won't, but I tried." He stepped back, suddenly awkward, five thousand spins old again instead of the competent thing he became around a stove. "Bring the salt twist back if you don't use it. It's really expensive."

"I'll bring it back."

It was the second time in two mornings someone had handed me a thing I was meant to bring back, and I was beginning to understand that this was how the people who couldn't come with you said the thing they couldn't say: they gave you an errand. A reason shaped like an object. Come back, because the needles are mine. Come back, because the salt is expensive. Nobody said come back. Everybody said it.

Then we went through the gate.

I had expected it to feel like something. A line crossed, a door shut, some interior click of before and after. It didn't. It felt like walking, which is all it was — the spikes sliding past on either side, sharpened logs I'd seen ten thousand times and never from this angle, and then the open trail, and then the town was behind us and the morning was in front of us and my legs were just doing the ordinary thing legs do.

We hadn't gone a thousand paces before the reeds started up. I'd heard them my whole life without once being out in them at the right moment to hear them properly — the bellow reeds that grew in stands along the low ground beside the road, tall and hollow and brown, and when the small wind that comes with the Scar's turning moved through them they let out a sound I can only call mourning: a low, tuneless moan, dozens of hollow throats at once, rising as the grey came up and falling as it settled, and dredging up a smell of damp with the air. The morning bellow. Tondi had set the inn's whole day by it for as long as I'd known her — bread on at the first moan, shutters down at the evening one — and I'd thought of it, when I thought of it at all, as a thing the reeds did, the same as the Scar was a thing the sky did. Out here, with no walls to flatten it, it was enormous, and I understood for the first time that it was the nearest thing my whole country had to a clock. Roan walked into it without a flicker; he'd soldiered these roads and the sound was wallpaper to him. Third listened — the eye tracking the nearest stand as we passed, the small tilt that meant he was filing a thing away. "A loud stand, in a strong wind, will cover the sound of a man's feet entirely," he said, to no one in particular. "It is worth knowing which sounds in a country are the ones a person can hide inside." And he walked on, and left me to decide whether he'd handed me a piece of lore or a piece of warning — a distinction, I was learning, he didn't trouble to make.

The Spire was on the horizon where it always was. From the town you mostly saw it over the rooftops, a thin dark line you navigated by without thinking, the way you navigate by a thing you've decided is permanent. Out here, with nothing between us and it but a great deal of distance, I could see the gold catch at its tip where the Scar's grey light found it. Spireward was that way. Everything was measured from it. I had known that since before I could walk and had never once had a reason to care, and now I was walking across the actual world it stood in the middle of, and the knowing turned over in my chest into something that felt strange and large and not entirely comfortable.

We walked. Roan beside me, Third a few paces ahead at a pace he'd clearly chosen to be followable. The trail rose toward a low hill — the first hill, the one that from town was just the edge of the world you could see, the place the trail went to disappear.

I knew that when we crested it, the town would go behind us. The pillar, the inn, the gatehouse, all of it, dropping below the line of the land like a coin into a pocket.

I did not look back.

So I kept my eyes on Third's back and the gold on the horizon beside me, and the dense warm weight of Klar's bundle in my arms, and I walked over the hill and did not turn around, and I was, I think, a little proud of myself for it.

Roan looked back.

I saw him do it — at the crest, just before the town would have gone — a quick turn of the head, the way you check whether someone is watching you leave. I knew the look. I'd worn it. It was the look of a person who needs the leaving to have been witnessed, because a brave thing done where no one sees it doesn't yet feel, to a certain kind of young man, entirely real.

I didn't know yet whether anyone had been at the gate to see him. I didn't ask. It seemed like the kind of thing a person should be allowed to keep to himself, at least at the start, before the road taught him what I suspected it was going to teach him — that the looking back stops mattering somewhere out there, and that the not-needing-to is a thing you earn rather than decide.

Then the hill was behind us too, and the town with it, and there was only the trail and the grey morning and the gold pin of the Spire holding the whole flat world in place.

Some part of me had spent my whole life believing nothing would ever change in a quiet town. That part had been right, in its way. The town hadn't changed. The town was exactly where I'd left it, doing exactly what it had always done, and would go on doing it without me, with Kanot wiping my tables and Tondi mending its torn shoulders and Klar feeding whoever was hungry.

It was just that I was no longer in it.

"How far to the first stop?" I asked, because the silence had gone on long enough and because I had discovered that I did not want to walk inside my own head any further just then.

"Four days to the Causeway," Third said, without turning. "If the weather holds and you keep up." A pause. The glow of his eye angled back at me, just slightly. "You will keep up. You are already doing better than most."

"That's almost encouraging."

"It was meant to be entirely encouraging. I am told I lack the range."

Roan laughed — surprised out of him, the first real laugh since the gate — and Third's eye held on us a moment longer, bright in the grey light, before turning back to the trail.

We walked on toward the Causeway, and the Spire, and the thing at the end of it that none of us yet understood, and behind us the town did not change at all.
