# Thirty

He should not have gone in. He knew it the way he knew the weather of a blade coming — knew it before his hoof left the lived ground — and he went in anyway, because the alternative was to stand at the embers of his friend and not know whether the friend was anywhere at all, and that was a thing his three hundred thousand spins had not taught him how to bear.

Tasha had her hand on his forearm. He had told her he meant only to look, which was the first lie he had told her since the carts, and he suspected she knew it was one, because her grip was harder than looking warranted and her voice had already started — low, steady, the rope paying out before he'd even stepped — *I'm here, I've got you, you come back when I call, you hear me, you old fool, you come back.* He held the sound of her the way you hold a guide-rope in fog. Then he stepped beside the world, and the lived sound of her thinned to a thread, and the grey took him.

It was wrong, the way it had been wrong on the watch — churned, currented, the whole patient nothing sliding one way, toward the cliff, toward the drain behind it — but he had expected the wrongness and braced against it, leaning his will upcurrent like a man wading. What he had not braced against, what no bracing would have served against, was that the current was warm.

It had never been warm before. The grey was a cold neutral nothing; that was its whole nature. But the flow toward the cliff was warm, and the warmth had a grain to it he knew, and he understood with a lurch that the warmth was *company* — that the thing draining toward the cliff was not empty grey but grey full of the held warm parts of everything that had ever been lost here, all of it flowing home, and that if he let go his will and stopped wading he would flow with it, into the warm, toward the gladness he could feel waiting patient at the bottom of the pull, the thing that knew him, that had always known him, that was so very pleased he had finally come this far in.

And in the warm, a few steps downcurrent — close, so close — was Mathen.

He was sitting the way he sat at a fire, one knee up, easy, and he was *talking,* of course he was talking, telling some story to no one with his hands going, and when he saw Third his whole face did the thing it did, the lighting-up, the pleasure at company, undimmed.

"Found you," Mathen said — warm, glad, and the words went through Third like a blade because they were the other words, the wet-gravel words, *found you,* and here they were turned back into kindness in the mouth of the man the other mouth had killed. "Or you found me — I never could keep the score in that game. I wondered if you'd come. I hoped you wouldn't, and I hoped you would, which is the usual way of hoping, I find."

"You are here," Third said. The avalanche of his voice came strange in the grey, muffled, near.

"I'm somewhere. I'm here when you're here. I don't ask it too many questions." Mathen patted the nothing beside him, an invitation to sit, the way he'd patted a thousand fire-logs. "Sit a while. You look terrible, which I'll grant is your natural state, but more so. Sit. Tell me a story. You owe me one — you never did pay back the Dorn telling, and now look, I've gone and given you a second one, the whole dramatic exit, you could dine out on me for a tenday." The grin. "Make it a good one. Laugh at the start."

And Third wanted to sit. That was the thing he would carry out of the grey if he carried nothing else — the size of how much he wanted to sit down beside Mathen in the warm and tell him a story and have it be, for as long as forever lasted, exactly this easy. The grief he had carried up out of the lived world did not lessen in the warm; it was held there, but held the way a note is held, sustained, sweet, unbearable, and the only thing that promised to end the unbearable sweetness was to give in to it, to sit, to stay, to stop wading and let the warm current carry the two of them down to where all the others were, because they were there, he could feel them now, the whole downcurrent abundance of them — a green child, a swordsman with a Boltcaster, a woman who had been unwell, a woman from the snow — all of them, *kept,* all of them a few steps further into the warm, and the gladness at the bottom of it saying, without words, in the voice that had smiled at him as it fell: *yes. all of them. you have only to come the rest of the way. you have been alone so long. let me know you. let me give them back.*

He had been warned. He had warned himself, sitting on a fallen slab with Tasha, naming the trap aloud so that he would recognise it when it closed. He had thought, naming it, that knowing the shape of a trap was most of escaping it.

He had been wrong. Knowing the shape of it did nothing. The shape of it was *Mathen, glad to see him, asking for a story,* and there is no knowledge that armours a creature against being wanted by someone it loves.

So he reached, drowning, for the only thing that was not in the warm.

"Mathen," he said. "Are you Mathen?"

The grin flickered — not gone, but troubled, the way the living Mathen's face had gone troubled when a thing mattered. "Now that," he said slowly, "is a question I can't answer, and the fact that I can't is probably your answer, isn't it. Clever. You were always cleverer than you let the room think." He looked downcurrent, toward the warm gladness, then back, and something old and sad and entirely *himself* came up in him. "I'll tell you what I know. I know I feel like me. I know I'd tell you to stay, if staying were the kind thing — I never could stand to see you carry what you carry. And I know that *because* I'd want you to stay, you can't trust me to tell you to. If I'm really me, I'm compromised by loving you. And if I'm not really me—" the grin came back, rueful, terrible "— then I'm the prettiest hook that's ever been baited, and you should be halfway gone already." He spread his hands. "Either way, old friend, you can't take the word of anyone in here. That's the cruelty they built into the place. You'll never again be able to tell your dead from the thing wearing them. I'm sorry. I'd undo it for you if I could. Which is, of course, exactly what the hook would say."

And that — not the warning, not the will, not the wading — that was the thing that turned him. Because it was so precisely the move the living Mathen would have made, to look the trap in the face and name its teeth even at the cost of his own comfort, that Third could not tell if it proved the man real or proved the bait perfect, and in the not-being-able-to-tell he understood, finally and in his bones, what Tasha had understood for him with only a road's worth of watching: that the warm part was lost to him now. Not Mathen. The whole of it. Every grave he had ever visited in the grey, every warm note he had gone there to sustain — poisoned, every one, because he would never again know whether he was grieving or being fished.

The grey had been the one place his dead were safe. The Titan had made it the one place they were bait. It had taken from him the only comfort his unkillable life had ever found, and it had done it gently, and there had been, as ever, no cruelty in the doing — only the warm, and the gladness, and the patient certainty that he would, in the end, come home.

He would not give it that. He had decided this on a slab in the lived world and he decided it again now in the warm, with Mathen's face on it, which was harder by an order he had not imagined.

He turned upcurrent, into the wading, away from the warm and the abundance and the green child a few steps downcurrent that he did not let himself look at, that he would not look at, that he would spend the rest of whatever he had not-looking at, and he reached for the thread.

It was still there. Thin, lived, loud. *—come back, you hear me, you don't get to do this, you don't get to leave me here doing the watch alone, there's a whole stupid world out here with a sign that says Only and a boy who needs you and me, Third, me, I'm still telling you about the sign, come back and let me finish—* He took hold of Tasha's voice with everything that was left of his will, and he waded, and the warm pulled, and Mathen — or the thing that was kind enough or cruel enough to wear him — called after, the last of it, the living man's exact cadence:

"*Go on,*" Mathen said. "Whatever's coming. You go on. And Third—" almost lost now, under the rope, under the wading "—tell it well. Laugh at the start."

Third stepped back into the world.

He came back wrong, and he knew he came back wrong, because the first thing he was aware of was Tasha's face very close to his and both her hands fisted in the vines of his arm and tears on her and her voice gone hoarse from how long she had been throwing it after him into a place she could not see.

"I had you," she was saying. "I had you, I had you the whole time."

"You did," he managed. The lived world was very loud and very cold and very full of unfinished things, and he had never in three hundred thousand spins been so grateful for any of them. "You did. I heard the sign." The bellows dragged, ragged, the one he allowed himself. "Tasha. It was worse than I told you it would be. I told you it would be hard. I did not know — I could not have known until I was in it — that knowing would not help. That I would want it *with the knowing.* That the wanting does not care what I understand." The coal of his eye found hers, and there was naked fear in it, the certainty gone, the old sureness nowhere. "When it comes — the real offering, at the cliff, with all of them and not just the one — I am not certain my will is the thing that decides it. I think it may be yours. I think the rope may be the whole of what stands between that thing and the door. I am sorry. That is a terrible thing to hang on a person. I would carry it myself if there were any way at all to carry it myself."

"I know," Tasha said. She didn't let go of his arm. "I've got the rope. I'm not putting it down." A breath. "What did it offer."

He looked, for a moment, the way she had learned meant he was choosing the true thing over the easy one.

"Him," Third said. "And all of them behind him. Kept. Warm. Forever." The ember sank to almost nothing. "And the worst of it, Tasha — the worst of it is that I could not tell, at the end, whether the Mathen who told me to go on was Mathen being kind, or the thing being clever. I will never be able to tell. It has taken even that. I went in to grieve my friend and I came out unable to be sure I have ever grieved anyone, or only ever been fished by something patient wearing the faces of my own dead."

The fire had burned to coals. Out past it, unwatched, the great book turned a page. And the three of them sat in the cold lived world that was loud and unfinished and *true,* and none of them slept, and Third did not go into the grey again, though I think — I came to think, after — that the notgoing was its own slow kind of starving, and that he did it for us, and that it was one more thing he never once asked us to thank him for.
