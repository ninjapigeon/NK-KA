# Thirty Eight

I have been asked to describe what happened next more times than I have been asked anything else in my life, and I have never once done it well, because the truest answer is the one nobody wants: I don't know. I couldn't see it. The most important thing that has ever happened in the world happened a half-step sideways from me, in a place my eyes were not made to go, and all I had was the back of a wooden man and the things he said out loud and the slow terrible arithmetic of watching someone decide.

So this is not what happened. This is what it looked like, from the rock, from the wrong side of the wall, holding a rope I couldn't see the far end of.

It looked like a man saying goodbye to his daughter.

"Liwon," he said. The hoof was down. The other had not lifted again. But he had not turned from the grey either, and his voice when it came was not the avalanche and not the gravel — it was small, and careful, the voice you'd use on a child you were not going to be able to keep from crying. "Liwon. Look at me. No — keep your arms down a moment. I know. I know you're frightened. I know it's dark in there. Listen to me."

I could not hear an answer. There was no answer to hear; she was in the place I couldn't go. But I watched Third listen to one, watched his head tilt the exact degree it must have tilted over a sling three hundred thousand spins ago, and I have never in my life felt so much like an intruder on something holy and so unable to leave.

"I cannot come and get you," Third said. "I know I said I would always come. I know. I have thought about that promise every day you have been gone, and I am breaking it now, on purpose, with my eyes open, and I need you to hear me say that it is a promise I am choosing to break and not one I forgot." A pause. The lights in his chest had stopped guttering; they had gone very steady, very low, the way a flame goes steady right before. "Because the you in there is not the last true thing about you. The last true thing about you is that you were *mine,* and you were *real,* and you were taken, and it was wrong, and it has stayed wrong every day since, and that wrongness is the only honest grave you have. If I take the warm copy this thing is holding up, I have to agree with it that you were only ever a seed for it to grow me a flower from. And you were not a seed, Liwon. You were a girl. You were my girl. And I would rather keep the empty place where you were, true, forever, than fill it with something that calls your whole short real life a *down payment.*"

<p align="center">&#42; &#42; &#42;</p>

I felt it through the rope before I understood it. The arm under my hand — slack since the going-still took him — came back. Not pulling toward the wall. Not pulling anywhere. Just *his* again, weight in it, a man inhabiting his own limb.

"You taught me this," Third said, and I knew, this time, it was not to Liwon, and not to me; it was to a woman who had died old in a bed a long way from here, who had lost everything and gone on anyway. "You said going on was the only way to call the loss what it was. I did not understand you. I thought you meant *endure.* You meant *refuse.* You meant that to take the bargain is to let them tell you it was never really a loss at all — and that the loss is the last of the love you get to keep. I am keeping it. I am keeping all of it. Thank you. I am sorry it took me until the end of the world."

And then, to the grey, to the small reaching shape in it, the worst sentence I have ever heard a person say, said gently, said like tucking someone in:

"Goodbye, Liwon. I love you. Stay where it's warm. I am not coming."

<p align="center">&#42; &#42; &#42;</p>

I will tell you what I saw, and you can believe what you like about what it means.

I saw the standing wall of grey go *out* — not torn, not slammed, nothing violent. It went out the way a held breath goes out. It had stood up off the ground like something about to speak and now it simply sat back down into the rock and the troughs and the ordinary low places, thinning, paling, becoming again the faint wrong fog I'd watched all the way across the field, and then less than that, and then gone, and the bare honest rock of the world lay there under the Scar's grey light with nothing standing up out of it at all.

And I felt Third lose her.

I had braced for triumph. There wasn't any. What came through the rope, through the arm under my hand, was the thing I had no name for until I'd felt it — the second losing. He had buried her once, a child, three hundred thousand spins ago, with no choice in it. Now he had been handed her back, whole, warm, asking to be carried, and he had given her up *again,* with every choice in the world, and chosen the empty place on purpose. The first time the world took her from him. This time he handed her back to the dark with his own two-fingered hands, so that the world could keep being a world.

He made a sound then. Once. I won't try to spell it. It was not the bellows and it was not a word. It was the sound the make of him made around a grief it had chosen, and I hope I never hear anything like it again, and I will hear it for the rest of my life.

<p align="center">&#42; &#42; &#42;</p>

Sougad did not rage.

I think, if he had raged, I could have hated him cleanly, and it would have been easier. Instead the worn old face simply… settled, the way water settles when you stop disturbing it, back into the patience it had worn coming across the field — except the patience had nothing left to be patient *toward,* now, and so it was just a face, very old, looking at a wall that had closed and a door that had chosen, with the six dead lights guttering on his stooped back.

"You understand what you have done," he said. Not a threat. Barely a question. "I cannot be killed. Not by you, not by anything that lives on this side. I will go back to the Anvil, because there is nowhere else a thing like me can go, and I will be fixed to it as I have been fixed to it since before your makers had names, and I will pull, because that is the only thing a fixed thing can do. And one day, made thing — long after the girl beside you is dust, long after the boy on the rock is a name no one says — another door will wake. They always do. I am very patient. I have just been taught how patient I am going to have to be."

"Yes," Third said. He had turned, finally, away from the place she'd been. The single light of his eye found the cold man, and there was no triumph in it either, only a kind of terrible kinship. "You will wait. And I will spend whatever I have left making certain the next one who wakes is found by someone who loves them, before it is found by you. That is the whole of the war, I think. That is the only war there ever was. You have patience. I will leave behind people."

Sougad looked at him a long moment. Then he said the old word and stepped sideways, the half-step out of the world that I had watched Third make a dozen times, and the cold went with him, all at once, like a door closing on the cold — and the rock where he'd stood was only rock, and the Scar wheeled its slow light over a world that had, against everything, not ended.

I became aware that I was still holding Third's arm. That my other hand had, at some point I never felt, finally found the Boltcaster under my cloak, and was gripping it white-knuckled, and had never drawn it.

And a dozen paces off, on the cold stone, the heap that was Roan made a small, wet, outraged groan — the single most beautiful sound in the entire language of the living — and tried, and failed, and tried again, to sit up.
