# Thirty Five

Up close the cold of him was a real thing, a weather. It came off him the way heat comes off a stove, except backward, except wrong — a steady draught of cave-air that found the sweat on you and made you regret having a body at all. Roan, three paces to my left with his spear low, flinched at it before he caught himself. I didn't catch myself in time and shivered like a wet dog. Third did not feel it, or did not show it, which by then was the same thing.

I had braced, I think, for a monster. For something that looked like what it did. But the cold man just looked old — old past any use the word usually gets put to, old the way the cliff was old, a person who had been a person so long ago that the being-a-person had worn off him like paint off a fence. His face was all still there, eyes and mouth and the rest, and there was nothing behind any of it but patience. That was the worst part. Not menace. Patience. The patience of a thing that has already won and is only waiting for the rest of you to notice. And riding his stooped back, close enough now that I could see them clear, the six lights: bottled lightning long dead in their jars, the same blue-white as the two in Third's chest, except his were guttering and these six only sat there, steady and spent and somehow worse for it, like eyes that have stopped expecting anything.

He stopped a few paces from Third, and the two of them stood there, and I understood — watching the way they held themselves, both of them somehow a half-step out of the world, both leaning on a wind no one else could feel — that I was looking at two of a kind. Not the same. But kin, the way a key and a lock are kin: made to fit, made by the same hand for the same wound.

"You are awake," the cold man said.

His voice was nothing like Third's. Where Third was an avalanche, low and felt in the bones, this was the thin dry whisper of air pulled through a long-dead place, a sound with no warmth in it to lose. He said it the way you'd say a thing you had wanted for so long that getting it had stopped being a joy and become only a fact. There was no triumph in it. There was something far worse — something almost like tenderness.

"I felt them make you," he went on, when Third said nothing. "All of you. A long time ago, when the hand that made you struck you out — I felt each blow of it, here—" and he did not point to his chest, he pointed down, at the rock, at the cliff, at whatever was under it "—because I am fixed to the thing they struck you against. I have felt every one of your kind woken since, the way you feel a door open somewhere in a house you cannot leave. Most never woke. The ones that did were too far, or too broken, or died too soon." The patient eyes rested on Third. "I have waited a very long time for one of you to wake near enough, and whole enough, and last long enough to walk to me. And here you are. Do you know what you are?"

"I am Third," said Third. The avalanche held. "I know what I have made myself."

"That is not the question I asked." Not unkind. A teacher correcting a beloved pupil. "I asked if you know what you *are.* You do not. You have wondered — I can see that you have wondered, the whole long walk of your life. Why you can step beside the world when no other made thing can. Why the grey lets you pass and pulls at you both at once. Why, of all the things the Titans struck out, *you* woke." The cold deepened, or seemed to. "I will tell you, because I want you to understand what you are giving, when you give it. There is no joy in taking a thing from someone who does not know its worth."

I had the Boltcaster under my cloak, loaded, Dorn's whole grave of a weight against my ribs, and my hand on Third's forearm, and my voice already low in my throat saying the small steadying nothings I'd sworn to say — *here, I'm here, you're Third, I've got you* — and I felt Third lean, the smallest degree, toward the cold man's voice. Not toward the lure. There was no lure yet. Toward the *answer.* After three hundred thousand spins, somebody was finally going to tell him why.

"Under us," the cold man said, "is the Anvil. It is not a stone. It is the thing the Titans set everything else against when they were striking the world into shape — the surface they hammered creation on. It is in the grey, and it is larger than this cliff, larger than the country, and it cannot be moved; I have had an age to try, and I am fixed to it, and one does not move the thing one is nailed to." For the first time something that was not patience crossed him — a very old, very tired frustration, worn down to almost nothing. "Everything you have felt these last spins, the pulling, the grey running one way like water down a drain — that is the Anvil. That is me, drawing the world toward the place the world began, because that is the only thing a fixed thing can do. Pull. I have pulled for longer than your kind has had names." The eyes came up. "But pulling is not opening. And I need it *opened.*"

"Then open it," Third said. "You walk in the grey. I have watched you do it. Step across, here, where it is thin, and reach your Anvil, and leave us be."

"I can go to it." A sound came out of him that might once, very long ago, have been a laugh. "I have gone to it more times than there are days in your life. But I am a passenger, made thing. I cross the wall; I do not breach it. I can carry one body sideways into the grey — myself, a thing I collect, no more — the way a man swims a river. The river is not opened by his crossing; it closes behind him as he goes. What is wanted is not a swimmer. What is wanted is a *door* — the wall held wide, and held, long enough for what is on the far side to come *through,* into here, into Anka, in its own vastness and not one body at a time." The patient eyes, the terrible tenderness. "A wall is not opened from without. It is opened from within, by the part of it that is alive. You are the only living part of the wall, made thing. They forged you against the Anvil; the making wove you into the seam of the two worlds; and then the last of them, dying, woke you with its own essence — the very essence that now wants through. You are not a soldier who happens to walk in the grey. You are the grey, and you are Anka, in one skin, with one will. You are the only seam in all the world that can be asked to part. That is what you are. That is what I have waited for. I did not make you, and I do not know your name, and neither of those things has ever mattered. I only ever needed one of you to be *alive,* and *here,* and able to say yes."

The silence after that had weight, like the air before a stony.

I felt the whole of it land on Third — felt it through my hand on his arm, the way you feel a tree take an axe-blow through the ground. Everything he had ever wondered, answered, in a dead man's whisper, at the end of the world: not a fluke, not a mercy, not a question the Titans forgot to answer. A purpose. He had spent his entire long life believing he had *escaped* his making — refused the glaive, refused the killing, made himself into something his makers never intended — and here was a thing older than his makers telling him, gently, that the escape itself had been the design. That he had been woken to be a door, and everything he'd become since, every kindness, every refusal, every companion buried, was just the door learning to want things, so that one day it could be offered the one thing that would make it open.

I want to tell you he answered like a hero. He didn't. He went very still, and the lights in his chest stuttered, and for a moment — one moment, the worst of my life to that point — I thought I had already lost him, that the silence I'd been warned to watch for had come this early, before the cold man had even named a price.

"Third," I said. Out loud. Not a steadying nothing. His name, hard, the way you'd throw a rope. "You're Third. You made that name yourself; nobody struck it into you. Say it."

A long breath of bellows, ragged, the first he'd spent in days.

"I am Third," he said. To me. Only to me. "I made myself. Whatever I was struck for." The eye came back to the cold man, and the avalanche came back into the voice, and I could have wept. "You have told me what I am. You are mistaken about one thing only. A door can be a door, and still choose to stay shut. That is also a thing a living seam can do."

The cold man did not look troubled. He looked, if anything, fonder.

"Yes," he said. "It can. That is why I have not come to threaten you, or to break you, neither of which would serve. I have come to *offer* you something. You will want to hear it. Everyone, in the end, wants to hear it." He tilted his worn face, and the six dead lights shivered on his back, and the grey stood up off the ground behind him like a held breath. "Shall I tell you what is waiting, on the other side of the wall you are?"

And Scar help me, I felt Third want to say yes.
