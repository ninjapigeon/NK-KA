# Eighteen

We passed a marker that evening that I didn't understand, and Mathen did, and the explaining of it is what undid us.

It stood where two trails crossed: a waist-high cairn with a flat stone set into its face, and on the stone, scratched deep and then darkened with something to make them distinct, two shapes. A tall thin figure on one side. A hunched toothed one on the other. Between them, a line, scored so hard it had split the stone.

"Boundary stone," Mathen said.

"Kro that way, Ork the other. You see them all through this country — it's old border, older than the towns that sit on it. The line's the thing. Whoever cut it cut it to last." He ran a thumb along the split. "They've been hating each other since before anyone can say. That's the one fact every version agrees on. Every other part of the story, you'll get a different telling in every town — who started it, who betrayed who, which of them was the 'better race'. But the hate itself, nobody argues. It's just *there*. Like weather. Like the Scar."

"Why, though," Roan said. He was looking at the two scratched figures. "What did they actually do to each other, at the start of it?"

"Ah." Mathen settled back, the storyteller's pleasure coming over him. "Now, the one I like best — and I make no claim it's true, mind, I make a claim it's *good* — goes that they were one people once, and a great sorcerer offered to make them perfect, and they said yes, the fools, because everyone says yes to that, and…" He spread his hands. "Two peoples woke where one lay down. Each one looked at the other and saw everything it had just thrown away, walking around wearing its face. And you can forgive a stranger anything. But you can't forgive your own discarded half. That's the version I tell. It's almost certainly wrong." He glanced, the way he'd taken to glancing, across the trail. "Ser? You'd know better than the stone-cutters. Were they one people, once?"

Third did not answer.

I had learned his stillnesses by then, and I watched the first one arrive — the kettle kind, the held kind, the kind that had ended in a quiet correction the night Mathen told the war wrong. I waited for the correction. *That is not how it was.* It was practically a comfort by now, that flat patient voice setting the record straight.

It didn't come. Instead the second stillness came over him, the one that went *down* — the glow guttering low, the attention turning to a place that was not our place — and he rose, without a word, and stepped, at an angle my eye slid off of, and was gone from the fire before Roan had finished waiting for his answer.

"…did I say something?" Roan asked.

"No," I said. But I sat very still, and watched the empty dark where Third had been, and understood for the first time that there were stories Third would correct and stories Third would walk into the grey to get away from, and that we had just, without meaning to, asked for one of the second kind.

There had been, before, a stretch of spins that were nearly happy, and Third had let himself have them, which was the part he would never afterward forgive.

He was a warden of the wall by then. Not honorary any longer — the Virek had stopped saying the word, which was how he knew it had stopped being true, the way a scar stops being a wound when you forget to favour it. He walked the high logs at night and the green ones on watch with him no longer kept their distance, or kept less of it, and Liwon ran with the other children in the day and dreamed in their tongue at night, and that was right, and Third had decided to be content, and was, almost.

Wally was still with them. Wally did not grow, which nobody remarked on because nobody remarks on the Hin, but he had taken to walking the wall with Third on the long nights, the small person made of stories keeping company with the large one who had run out of them to tell. They did not always speak. It was enough, those nights, to stand the watch together and let Liwon belong to someone else in the daylight, and to mind, in the low private way Third had learned to mind things, like a temperature.

He saw the flicker from the wall.

It was far off, deep in the wood, and very bright — a white flash with a blue heart to it, there and gone, the kind of light you feel behind your eyes a moment after you stop seeing it. He marked it the way he marked everything, and was turning to call it down to the others, when the veins came.

They came up over everything at once. Red, branching, vast — across the ground and across the sky and across the walls and the wood and across his own outstretched arm, a webwork of red light that had the terrible quality of seeming to have been there always, beneath the skin of the world, and only now made to show. They held for a breath. Two. He had time to think *I have seen this colour before,* and to be right, and not to have time to finish being right —

— and then they were gone, and the village was full of growling.

He turned from the wall.

The Virek were not there.

Where the green ones had been — on the wall beside him, in the yards below, in the doorways of the woven houses — stood strangers. Pale ones, fine-boned, tall, with hair so fair it was nearly no colour at all, nor the green that the skin had been; they stood blinking in their new bodies, appalled, looking at their own pale hands. And among them, lower, broader, black-haired and already showing teeth, the others. The green had not left those ones. It had gone *into* them, concentrated, a deeper and angrier green, and they had teeth meant for meaning it, and they were the ones making the sound.

For one held instant, the whole village stood and looked at itself, pale beside green, and saw.

Then it began. Not a battle — there was no battle in it, no sides drawn, only the oldest recognition there is, every pale one seeing the green one that had been the rest of it and every green one seeing the pale, and the seeing being unbearable, and the unbearable becoming, in the space of a breath, hands and teeth and the wall-logs torn loose for clubs. It began everywhere at once. It began the way the veins had come, as if it had always been there under the skin of these people and the flash had only made it show.

"Liwon," Third said.

He had not decided to say it. He was already moving.

Zala's house was at the wall's foot, and the laminated reed of its door hung in tatters, and Third put his shoulder through what was left and stopped, and every gear in him stopped too.

There were two children on the floor.

One was pale. A small girl, fine-boned and new, her hair gone nearly clear, her eyes a blue he had never seen — Liwon's eyes had been Virek-dark, and these were not; these were a blue like the heart of the flicker. They were open. They found him. Her body was torn in a way the small new pale body could not mend and would not survive, and she was looking at him.

The other was green. Smaller-seeming, though they were the same, crouched over the pale one with blood on its fingers and its mouth, and it too looked at Third, and what came off it was rage and terror and a disgust so total it had no room left for anything that might have been love, and Third understood — in the flat fast way the old thoughts used to bring him things, except the thoughts were silent now, the thoughts had nothing — that these were not two children.

They were one child. They were the halves of one child, sundered the way the wood-stone said two peoples were sundered, and they had woken in the same room into the same instant of hating what they'd lost, and they had not even had names for it yet, they had only had each other, within reach.

The green one broke first. It scrambled up the wreck of Zala's furniture and went out the high window into the dark, fast, low, the way the thoughts had always told Third the green ones moved, and Third took one step after it —

— and outside there was a shout, a bright triumphant boy's shout, and then a wet sound, and the shout turned to laughter, and Third knew without going to the window that one of the pale ones had found the green child in the dark and done to it what pale and green were going to be doing to each other from this night until the end of the world.

He turned back to the one on the floor.

He crossed the room. Two paces. He had crossed worse rooms in two paces and not felt them; he felt this one. He knelt, and his hands, which could mend any part of himself, which would mend every part of himself at least once, hung over the torn pale body and could do nothing, because she was not made of wood and Titan-blood, she was made of the thing the green ones and pale ones were all made of, the soft mendable-only-by-time thing, and there was no time, there was the opposite of time.

"Bada?"

It was barely a breath. It came from the pale child's mouth, from lips that had never made the sound before because they had not existed before, in a body a quarter-hour old. But it was the sound. The exact sound. The lower lip, even now, even torn, going out the way it went out when she was small and frightened and wanted only to be lifted and held against the bellows until the world stopped being so large.

*Bada.* The word she had made for him before she had words. Another of her words he had decided not to correct toward accuracy, because accuracy had stopped being the only thing he wanted.

Something in Third broke that he did not have a part to replace.

"I am here," he said. "Liwon. I am here."

The blue eyes — the wrong eyes, her eyes — stayed on him a moment, and were uncertain, the way they were uncertain in strange surroundings, and these were the strangest surroundings, this new pale unasked-for self. And then they were not uncertain about anything anymore.

There was a small sound behind him.

Wally stood in the broken doorway. Wally, who had not changed — who could not change, who was not Virek, who was a story and stories do not sunder — Wally stood unchanged in a village where every single soul had been split in two, and he was looking at the pale child on the floor with an expression Third had never seen a Hin wear, because Hin do not, as a rule, have anything in their lives that a true thing can hurt.

"That's her," Wally said. Not a question. Plain Hin-honesty even now. "Both of them were her. The one outside too." He came in, and knelt on the child's other side, small and solid and entirely real, and he reached out and closed the blue eyes with two fingers, gently, the way you'd do for someone, because Third's hands would not stop hanging uselessly in the air and someone had to, and Wally was the only one left who could.

And as he did it — Third saw it and would carry the seeing forever — Wally's hand, where it touched the child's face, was not quite as solid as it had been. You could not have said how. You could only have said that a thing made of telling had just lost the one it had been told to, and that the world had noticed, and had begun, very gently, the way the tide goes out, to ask for him back.

"Wally," Third said.

"I know," said Wally. "It's all right. I got to be in the good part." He looked up, and he was still smiling, a little, the smile of the stories he was made of. "You tell it. The good part. You keep telling it, and maybe I—" He stopped. He looked at his own hand. "Huh," he said, softly, the way you'd say it at a mildly interesting thing, and that was the last whole word Wally said in front of him, though it was not, Third would learn, the last of Wally.

He found himself in the grey without stepping there.

It took him without his choosing it, the way it had the first time, on the field, under the dust — one breath in the torn room with the closed-eyed child and the fading friend, and the next breath the room was gone and the colour was gone and he was kneeling in the swirling nothing with the wisps coming off his own arms, and where she had been, was empty grey floor. Not even the silhouette of her. The grey did not keep her. The grey, which kept so much, would not keep the one thing he would have given the rest of it to hold.

Behind him, the voice.

"Loss is profound," it said, "when the relationship has grown for some time."

It was barely above a whisper, and it had the weight of a mountain choosing to lie down. He did not turn. He could not have borne to see it, the vast horned dark of it, the thing whose dust had woken him and whose blood ran in the geysers and whose teeth lay in the fields; the thing that had made the world as a child makes a thing, with great power and no wisdom and no notion at all of what it is to be small inside the made thing and to love another small thing inside it.

"How do I fix this," Third said. He was not sure it was a question. It came out of the broken place, which was still broken, which he could already tell was going to stay broken in a way no wood and no blood would close.

"You do not," the voice said. There was no cruelty in it. That was the part Third would spend the next three hundred thousand or so spins failing to get out from under: there was no cruelty in it at all. "Like a seed, it must die and be buried before it blossoms into something beautiful. You are watching a beginning. You have mistaken it for an ending, because you are small, and the small feel the burial and never see the bloom." A vast and patient nothing. "Two peoples now, where one was. More to play. More to watch. It is better. In time you will see that it is better."

The broken place in Third warmed, faintly, the way a coal warms — and he understood, with horror, that some made part of him, some old obedient architecture, *agreed*, was built to agree, found the logic clean. He wanted the warmth. He wanted, for one unforgivable instant, to believe that Liwon was a seed and her death a planting and that somewhere ahead was a bloom that would make the torn pale body on the floor into a thing that had been *worth it.*

He wanted the beauty. He did not want the pain.

And then he chose the pain, because the pain was hers and the beauty was the Titan's, and he would not give the Titan one more thing that had been Liwon's, not even the meaning of her dying.

He stood up, and stepped back.

The room was still there, just. The fighting outside had thinned to the after-sounds of it, the single cries and the running feet and the long silences between. The child lay where she lay, eyes closed by a friend who was no longer in the doorway, who was not anywhere Third could see, though Third would feel him for a long time yet, thin and far, the way you feel a story you are forgetting.

And the former part of Liwon lay at his feet, and Third, who could mend anything he was made of, stood in the wreck of the only stillness he had ever managed to give her, and could not mend this, and did not try, and stayed.

He came back to the fire near the bottom of the night.

He didn't step out of the dark so much as resolve from it, suddenly accounted for at the edge of the light. The glow of his eye was down past the lowest ember I'd seen on him — down to almost nothing, a coal under ash — and it took a long moment finding me, and longer still coming up to anything I'd have called present.

Mathen had waited up. Roan had fallen asleep mid-wait, the way the young do. Mathen looked at Third across the coals, and whatever he saw there made him set aside, very quietly, the storyteller's hunger I'd watched him carry toward Third for a tenday.

"You don't have to," Mathen said. "I withdraw the question. It was a pub question. I'm sorry I asked it by a real fire."

Third was quiet a while. The coals ticked.

"They were one people once," he said, at last. The voice came from somewhere deeper than usual, the bellows dragging under it. "They chose to become perfect, and were offered the means, and took it. Two woke where one had lain down. Each kept what it could not bear to keep whole, and could not forgive the other for carrying the rest." A pause. "Your version is not wrong, master Mathen. It is only missing the part where it happened to specific people, in specific rooms, on a specific night, and someone watched, who had loved one of them." The ember held on the fire. "Tell yours. It is kinder. Kind is a fair trade for their coin."

And that was all. Mathen didn't push, and neither did I. We banked the fire and slept. In the morning Third was Third again, watchful and dry and exact, walking us anti-spinward under a sky that had a wound in it but no veins, and I thought — Scar help me, I actually thought — that it had only been a hard memory of a long war, the kind a soldier carries.

I did not know there had been a child. I did not know there had been a friend made of stories. I did not know that the thing every town agreed on, the hate older than weather, had a night attached to it, and a name, and that the name was one Third had decided, long ago, not to correct toward accuracy.

I know now. I am telling it. That is the only thing I can do with it — the thing Mathen said, that I didn't understand until I was the one left carrying it.

You make more than one person carry the thing.

So that it doesn't all rest on him.
