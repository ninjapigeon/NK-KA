# Thirteen

There was a shard tree at the edge of our camp that first night past the Snake Forest, and the pull of it told me I still hadn't entirely learned my lesson. I knew them by now. We had come up through a whole forest of them on the Causeway road, and that was where I'd learned what the prettiest thing on that road costs you — I still wore the thin white line across the back of my hand where one had taught me. But the forest had been a thousand of them, a roof of glass twenty men overhead, beauty and slaughter in one breath. This was a single tree, standing a little apart from the honest ones, slimmer, paler, its leaves long and narrow and the colour of dark honey; and when the night breeze moved them — gently, only gently — they made a sound I have never heard anything else make: a thin bright tinkling, like a shelf of glass cups touched all at once, delicate and lovely and entirely out of place in a wood at night. I got up and took a step toward it before the back of my hand reminded me, and I sat down again. One of the few beautiful things on that road that would also open your throat — and I had no intention of finding out whether the wind meant to stay soft. So I let it sing from where I was. I stayed by the banked fire, where Roan was a long breathing shape under his blanket, and I listened to the glass leaves sing, and across the camp Third sat his watch with his eye on the dark, and after a while I noticed that he had stopped watching the dark.

He was watching the tree.

I had begun, by then, to learn the difference between Third's stillnesses. There was the watchful one, which was most of them, the eye tracking, the body a held readiness. And there was the other one — the one I'd first really seen on the Causeway, when he'd come back from the carts diminished — where the stillness went *down* into him somehow, where the glow of the eye softened and turned its attention to a place that wasn't the place the rest of us were in. He was doing the second one now. Watching the singing tree, the eye gone soft and far — and then, between one breath of mine and the next, he was not there. I want to be careful about this, because I was three-quarters asleep and the fire was low. But I do not think I dreamed it. One moment his still shape sat against the dark with the glass leaves singing over it; the next, the place by the fire was simply empty, in a way my eye slid off and would not hold; and then — no more than a breath later, before the leaves had finished their phrase — he was back, sitting exactly as he'd sat, as though he had never moved. In the only time that counted to us, he never had: whatever while he spent away, he spent where time runs faster than it has any business to, and stepped home into the gap of a single breath. I didn't understand it then. I thought I'd nodded and missed him shifting. I know now that he had stepped — gone bodily out of the world to where they were, because the glass leaves were singing and the glass leaves had sung that day too, and after three hundred thousand spins he would step a little way beside the world and be with them again, and that it cost him almost no time at all, and that this was the cruellest mercy he had ever been given.

The child would not eat meat, and this was a problem, because Third had nothing else to offer and no understanding of what else there might be.

He had carried it three spins by then, the small green thing in its stolen sling, and it had made its needs known in the only language it had, which was volume, and Third had learned to read the volume the way he had learned everything in those days — by trial, and by failure, and by the slow accretion of a competence no one had built him to have. Raw meat, refused. Water, accepted, with suspicion. A paste he made of river-tubers mashed between two flat stones, accepted, and then worn, much of it, and then — this was the part the unbidden voice found most contemptible and the part Third found, privately, the most interesting — produced again at the far end some hours later, without warning or apology, down the length of his arm.

*It is consuming your time and your resources,* the voice observed, as it observed most things, flatly, certainly, without malice and without end. *It would be more efficient to set it down.*

"I have heard you," Third told it, scraping his arm with a leaf. "You are very consistent."

The child watched him clean himself, and made a sound that was not quite a word and not quite a laugh, and Third went still, because it was the first sound it had made that was aimed *at* him rather than at the indifferent sky, and he did not yet have anywhere to put what that did.

Her name came out of his, in a manner of speaking — out of the only thing he had ever called her. He had no name for her and felt no need of one; she was the child, which was accurate, and accuracy was the whole of what he asked of words in those days. But on the long walks he had taken to talking to her, low, to keep the volume down, and the thing he said most, when he lifted her or settled her or warned her back from an edge, was *little one.* Little one. It was not a name. It was only true.

And one day, reaching up at him from the sling with both green hands, pleased with herself, she gave it back to him in the shape her mouth could manage: "Li-wan."

"Little one," he said.

"*Li-wan,*" she insisted, as though correcting *him,* and would not be moved, and that was that. He had said a true thing to her a thousand times and she had handed it back to him broken into a name, and he found — turning the discovery over, the way he turned over every new thing the child made him feel — that he liked the broken version better than the true one. That accuracy had stopped, somewhere on those walks, being the only thing he wanted from a word.

Wally came out of the stories.

Third should have expected it, having memory of the Hin from before, having known in some flat archived way that a tale told often enough and meant hard enough will, on Anka, sometimes get up and walk. But knowing a thing as fact is not the same as turning from the fire one evening to find that the fact has acquired a face.

And a beard. It had started because Liwon would not sleep, and Third had no songs — songs were not in him — so he told her instead the only stories he had, which were the things he knew: the trees, the weather, the shapes of the dangerous world. He told them as stories because she would not take them as warnings. He gave the Shard trees a voice and the Squid trees a temper and the sleep-breathing shrubs a sly cowardice, and he told her the same ones over and over because she demanded the same ones over and over, and somewhere in the thirtieth-plus spin of this a small person was sitting at the edge of the firelight listening too.

He was about Liwon's height, which is to say small, and he had the look of someone who had always been there, and he was already laughing at the part of the Squid-tree story that Liwon always laughed at, on cue, as though he had heard it as many times as she had, which — Third came to understand — in every way that mattered, he had.

"You tell it wrong," the small person said, comfortably, to a being made to end his kind. "The Squid tree wouldn't say *that.* It'd say it grumbling. Like this." And he said it grumbling, and Liwon shrieked with delight, and Third sat with the particular stillness of a construct revising its model of the world in real time.

"You are Hin," Third said.

"I'm Wally," said Wally, as if these were the same thing, which for him they were. He had arrived complete — no infancy, no arrival, simply a yesterday in which he had not been and a today in which he had, and a perfect untroubled certainty that this was the normal way for a person to begin. He remembered the stories as his own childhood because the stories were, precisely, his childhood. He had been made of them. He found Third's careful questions about *when* and *how* very funny, and answered them the way you'd answer a child asking where the wind keeps its things.

Third did not tell either of them what he privately concluded, which was that he had talked a person into being out of grief and golden firelight and the need to keep one child asleep, and that this was either the best thing he had ever done or a thing he had no right to have done, and that he could not tell which, and that the not-telling had begun to feel familiar.

She learned the trees the way other children learn rhymes.

"Da goad ones," Liwon recited, marching beside him on the stone road with Wally marching behind doing an exaggerated version of her march, "have leefs dat hurt in wind. Or when dey drop." A solemn pause. "Da ones wif green smoke can stop my breaving. An' da ones wif da eye at da bottom is *safe.*" Triumphant. "'Cept when hurt."

"Good," Third said. "Yes. And when are they hurt?"

"When sumfin *bites* dem," she said, with relish, biting the air.

"When something bites them. Yes."

It had taken him, with the voice helping, the better part of their first spins together to learn to read the dangers of the wood. It took her a handful of tellings, and Wally fewer than that, and Third walked the Titan road behind the two small persons he had — there was no other honest word for it, and he had stopped fighting the word — *made,* one carried out of a massacre and one talked out of the dark, and he listened to them get the rhymes right, and the voice in his head said *this is not the purpose,* and Third said, internally, where only the voice could hear, *no. It is better than the purpose,* and the voice, for once, had nothing to add.

The village took her in the end, as he had always known a village would have to.

He found it by following the road to where the road found people — a Virek place, walled the way they walled, the tree-trunk spikes that always looked to Third like a porcupine's failed idea — and he came to it because Liwon was growing, and growing needed things he was not built to give: others of her kind, hands that knew what her body would do next, a place that did not move every dawn. He had not been able to give her stillness. In all the spins since his waking — and they were not so many, then; he was still new enough to be counting them — it was the one thing he had never once managed to be.

A woman came out to him. Zala. She was unarmed and unafraid in a way that the men on the wall, with their bows, very much were not, and she looked at the green child on the back of the thing that had been built to murder green children, and she did the arithmetic faster than her own people could, and what she landed on was not *monster.* What she landed on was a question she asked gently, already half-knowing: "You've been caring for her?"

"As best I was able," Third said. "My limits are becoming apparent as she ages. That is the only reason I have come."

She let them in. It took the men longer — an arrow came, badly aimed, and Liwon cried at the shouting, and Wally went and stood very deliberately between the child and the wall with his small bearded chin up, which did nothing and meant everything — but Zala's voice carried more than her rank, and in the end the gate held open and the three of them went through it, and that was where Liwon began, by slow degrees, to belong to someone other than him.

He watched it happen over the spins that followed. He had asked for it. He had walked her here precisely so that it would happen. And still there was a thing in him — he located it eventually in the place the bellows lived, low in the wooden cage of his chest — that registered each step of her moving toward them and away from him, the way you'd register a slow tide going out: not as a wound, exactly. As a temperature. As the world getting cooler by a degree you only noticed in sum.

She ran to the Virek children now, not to him. She learned their words faster than the ones he'd given her, and then better than them, and then she dreamed in them, which he knew because Wally told him, Wally who did not sleep much either and who sat with Third on the long nights and reported the small treasons of growing up with no cruelty and no comfort, just the plain Hin-honesty of a person who could not imagine why a true thing would hurt.

"She still asks for the tree story," Wally offered once, into one of Third's silences. "The one you tell wrong."

"Does she."

"I tell it now. I tell it right." A pause. "She says mine isn't as good." Wally seemed to find this satisfactory in a way Third was slow to follow. "She means yours. She means she wants *yours.* She just doesn't know that's what she means yet."

Third sat with that a while, on the wall he was permitted to walk now, an honorary warden of a village of the people he'd been forged to end, watching the dark for the things that came out of it.

"It is as it should be," he said, at last. He had decided to believe it. He had given her a place to be still in, which was the one gift he had never been able to give her himself, and a people whose hands knew her, and a future that did not move every dawn. That it cost him the degree of warmth he had only just learned he could hold — that was a fair price, and he was, he told himself, content to have paid it.

He believed it almost entirely.

The almost is the part the grey keeps. The almost is what was waiting for him under the singing of the glass leaves, all those spins later, on a road with a Humin girl asleep beside a fire and a whole catastrophe still ahead of them both that he had not yet let himself walk back into.

He stayed in the warm part. Just this once, he let himself stay in the warm part, and watch her get the rhyme right, and not go forward to the night the sky filled with red veins.

He was permitted that much. The grey is patient. It would still be there when he stepped back.

I woke fully near the end of his watch and he was Third again — the ordinary watchful stillness back in him, the eye on the dark, the night unremarkable. But the glow had gone down to its lowest ember, the banked-fire dimness I had seen on the Causeway, and when he turned his head to mark me waking, it took a moment to come back up, the way a person surfaces from a long way down.

"You went somewhere," I said. I didn't mean it as a question and it didn't come out as one.

The eye held on me.

"Yes," he said.

I waited to see if there was more, the way I'd learned to wait. The glass leaves sang. Roan turned over and muttered and slept on.

"Was it somewhere good?" I asked. I don't know why I asked it that way. It seemed, suddenly, the only thing worth knowing.

Third was quiet for a long moment, the ember of his eye steady on the singing tree.

"It was good," he said, "for a while. The good ones are. That is what makes them the ones worth walking back into." And then, before I could find the next question: "Sleep, Tasha. You have the dawn watch, and we walk hard tomorrow. We are close to the geysers now."

I slept. I did not know what geysers meant yet, or who we'd find there, or that the warm part of whatever Third had gone into had an end he hadn't shown me. I only knew that he had gone somewhere good and come back lessened, and that the two facts sitting together bothered me more than either would have alone, and I carried that small wrongness down into sleep and did not solve it.

I wouldn't solve it for a long time.
