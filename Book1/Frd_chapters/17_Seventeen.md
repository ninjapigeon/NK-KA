# Seventeen

We had come through the bog that morning, and the bog had nearly kept Roan, so the smell, when it found us in the afternoon, landed on people already shaken.

It had looked, the bog, like the kindest thing the country had shown us in days — we came out of scrub onto a wide flat meadow of soft green reeds, knee-high, level as a swept floor, with berry-bushes scattered across it and the Scar far off and the day's warmth coming down mild, and every part of me that was tired wanted to walk straight out into it and lie down. Third put his Falka across my chest before I'd taken three steps. "It is water," he said. "All of it. The reeds grow on the surface. They will hold a bird. They will not hold you." Roan was already six steps out, past where Third's arm could reach, having seen the berries and not the warning — and the green floor opened under him without a sound. No splash, just a swallowing, the reeds closing over the place he'd been like a mouth. Mathen and I had him by an arm and a fistful of cloak before his head went under. We hauled; the bog hauled back. For a long ugly moment the country was very calmly trying to drink a man in front of us while looking like a place you'd picnic. We got him out black to the waist and shaking. We went round the long way after that, along the firm edge, and the meadow lay there behind us soft and green and patient, beautiful as anything, waiting for the next tired thing that trusted its eyes.

So when the smell hit — in the afternoon, which meant the wind was coming from anti-spinward, which meant we were downwind of whatever was left of the town, which meant we'd been smelling it for the last hour without knowing what it was — I'd thought, hoped, it was just the bog again, turned rank in the heat behind us.

It wasn't the bog.

"Titans," Mathen said, pulling his collar up over his nose. "And here I'd just decided I was hungry."

"You are always hungry," Third said, not breaking stride. "We are downwind. You smell the dead."

"I'd worked that out, thank you." But the joke had gone out of his voice by the end of it. Mathen could keep a mood afloat across most things — I'd watched him do it for three days of rain and a washed-out ford and Roan's snoring — but there's a particular silence that comes off a place where a lot of people died at once, and even Mathen went quiet under it.

Roan didn't say anything at all. He'd gone the colour of old porridge and was looking at the gate ahead of us with the fixed attention of a man who has realised that the stories and the thing the stories are about are not the same size.

"If anyone lived," Third said, "they've moved on. The only things moving in there now are scavengers." He tilted his head — not toward the town, but at the angle I'd learned meant he was running several thoughts at once. "This was within the last spin. Two at most."

As if to make his point, an Axe Beak strode out from between the spikes with something in its mouth that I chose not to identify — a squat armour-plated thing about a humin and a half of bulk, most of it beak and teeth, the little wing-stumps twitching at its sides as it moved. It wove between the sharpened logs like they were saplings, unhurried, fed and unbothered, and vanished around the side of the nearest building without sparing us a glance.

"Those spikes were never going to stop anything, were they," I said.

"They would stop a massed charge," Third said. "Which this town would never face — nothing has charged a wall in ordered ranks since the Titans fell. What does come for a place like this walks between them, as that one did, or simply goes around." "Optimistic," Mathen agreed, in the tone of a man filing the word away for a song he had no intention of writing.

We went in through the gap where the gate had been — the gate itself reduced to kindling, scattered too small to be useful. The ground inside was wrong in the way ground gets when too many things happen on it in too little time. I kept my eyes moving and my hand near my blade and tried not to look too long at the things that weren't moving.

The Herdukii came from behind the first building's corner without warning.

Wolf-headed, bear-clawed, a dead man's cloak knotted round its middle and a string of fingers at its throat. It had been feeding, and it came up out of its crouch and launched itself at Third with the confidence of something that had not yet learned what Third was.

Several things happened in the space of about a heartbeat.

Roan made a sound I'd never heard him make and went backward off his own feet, scrambling, his spear coming up far too late to matter — the honest reflex of a man whose body had decided to be somewhere else before his training could vote on it. Mathen's swords were out before I'd finished flinching, both of them, low and wide and already moving to flank the thing, fast enough that I understood for the first time that the campfire jokes had been hiding something. And I was reaching for my own blade with the awful certainty that I was going to be too slow to be useful.

None of us needed to be anything.

Third's Falka cleared its sheath and returned to it in what felt like one motion. The Herdukii landed in two pieces that had been one piece, twitched, and stopped. Third had not broken stride.

There was a silence.

Mathen looked at his two drawn swords. He looked at the Herdukii. He looked at his swords again, and then sheathed them, both, with the unhurried dignity of a man pretending the whole manoeuvre had been a stretch.

"Well," he said. "Glad I could help."

"You were prepared to," Third said, which from Third was close to a compliment, and which Mathen accepted with a small bow he managed to make only slightly mocking.

Roan was getting up off the ground, scarlet now where he'd been pale, brushing at himself with the furious focus of someone who would rather be doing literally anything than meeting another person's eyes.

"It came out of nowhere," he said.

"It came from behind a building," Mathen said, kindly. "Which, from where you were standing, is much the same thing. You got your spear up. First time I saw one of those I got my spear into my own foot." He clapped Roan on the shoulder on the way past. "You'll meet the next one standing. That's the whole trick — there's always a next one, and you're always a little readier."

I didn't think Mathen had ever put a spear through his own foot in his life. But I watched the colour come back to Roan's face at a survivable rate instead of all at once, and I understood that this was the thing Mathen actually did, the thing that was worth more than the swords: he made the unbearable into something a person could stand inside.

"They were not scavengers here," Third said. He was looking down at the body with no disgust, the way you'd look at a knot you'd just untied. "They were directed. Held in numbers, set on the town all at once, the way the traveller described. Ravagers do not wait. Something made this one wait."

"Something worse than a Ravager," Roan said. His voice only shook a little.

"Yes."

We split up to search — Mathen and Roan taking the outbuildings along the wall, Third and I taking the large structure near the centre that Third had been looking at since we came through the gate. I think he sent the others wide on purpose. I think he already knew what we were going to find, and didn't want an audience for it.

The memorial pillar stood near the middle of the settlement, knee-high, three-sided, perhaps a dozen names across it. The founding plaque at its base had been there long enough to wear smooth at the edges but not long enough to rise very far out of the ground.

I stopped at it for a moment. A generation. Maybe less. Some of these names had likely been children when the town was founded; they'd grown up and built something, and the something was gone in a spin or two along with everyone who'd made it. I thought about a knee-high pillar in a town a great deal like this one, with a great deal fewer names on it, and Tondi in her corner, and Klar's stew, and I made myself stop thinking about it.

Third pointed at the central building. His arm extended with the directness of someone who had been pointing at things for so long he had long since stopped making it a social gesture.

"There. That is what they came for."

The wall had been breached from the inside out — not smashed, breached, the debris fallen outward in a way that said whatever did it had known exactly where it was going and gone there without hesitation. No matching hole in the far wall. Whatever came out had left through the opening it made.

"You think a caged Tusker did that?" I said.

Third paused, considering it with apparent sincerity. "Was that a joke?"

"Was it a good enough one that you can't tell?"

A shorter pause. Something in the set of his shoulders I'd learned to read as the Forged equivalent of dry amusement; the glow of his eye dimmed a fraction, the way it did when he was, in his own fashion, almost enjoying himself. "It was not. After you."

Inside had been an official's residence — six or seven strides to a raised platform with a seat on it, ramps up each side to the rooms above. The sort of room built to make a visitor feel the right size. It was still managing it, even without the official.

The lockbox sat in the centre of the floor. It had been below the centre of the floor, in the proper fashion, and was now above it, and the floor around it was in several places it should not have been. The hexwood box was big enough to hold a reclining child. Its gear-lock hadn't been picked or broken by someone who understood it — it had been torn apart by something that had decided the mechanism was beneath its notice. The lid was peeled back the way you'd husk a grain, if you were enormous and the husk were reinforced hexwood. And the whole of it smelled faintly of cinnamon. I never understood why hexwood smelled like cinnamon when it was damaged. Not that I had many chances to learn, it was a rare thing to damage hexwood. 

I looked at it. Then I looked at Third.

He bent, lifted the lid, and held it out to me flat in both hands, like an offering.

"I did not do this," he said.

I hadn't asked. That was the thing I noticed, and didn't understand, and filed away with the other small wrong-shaped pieces of Third: that he had answered a question no one had put to him, in the flat careful voice he used for the things that were true and cost him to say. As if somewhere, once, there had been a wrecked place he had not been able to say it about, and he had been saying it ever since to make up the difference.

I took it. Heavier than it looked, and it looked heavy. The hinges still hung off it, wrenched bodily out of the box. The wood hadn't split or cracked where it bent back. It had simply been made to go where something had decided it should go.

"You told us what was being collected," I said. "Back at the inn. Tools. I don't think I believed you. Not in the stomach. I believed you the way you believe a story." I set the lid down, carefully, for no reason. "I believe you in the stomach now."

"Good," Third said, and did not say it unkindly. "Belief in the stomach is the only kind that keeps a person alive out here." He looked at the ruined box. "But this tells me something I did not know before, which is worth the smell. Whatever did this came for one thing. It did not search. It did not ransack the town for valuables. It went to this room, opened this box, took what was inside, and left through the wall. It knew the object was here before it arrived."

"The traveller said it went building to building."

"In his town. Not here. Here it walked a straight line." The glow sharpened. "Which means between his town and this one, it learned to find the thing without looking. Or it always could, and chose not to, in his town, for reasons I do not yet have."

I found the slat-book in the upper room, in a drawer that had survived, probably because a drawer held nothing worth the unmaking of a wall. Most of it was the ordinary business of a small place — supply tallies, complaints, notes on the Scar and the weather it dragged behind it. Then, near the end, a hand that pressed harder on the wood:

"He was nervous," I said. "Listen. He's writing about keeping the 'Titan's stone' in town. 'Even hidden as it was.' He doesn't say what it is. Only that he'd been afraid of it for a while, and afraid of not having it, both at once."

Third had gone still. Not the watchful stillness. The other one — the one that meant something was being assembled behind the eye rather than observed in front of it.

"Titan's stone," he said, turning the frightened man's phrase over. "His name for it, not a true one. It will be a tool — one of the lesser ones, a thing the Titan made and set down and forgot, the way you or I might leave a chisel in a field. Not the great anchor the stories mean when they say such words. A small thing. But small things were how the Titan held the world together, and a man who is gathering them is not gathering them to build a fence." The eye came to me, the glow steady and hard. "And a frightened headman who did not know what he held — yet it walked straight to the box, in a town it had never seen, and opened the one that mattered and no other."

"So how did it know?" I asked.

"To come, or which wall?" The eye stayed on the emptied box.

"To come is the simple part. A town that hides a thing it fears cannot keep the secret — the fear leaks, into a tally-book, into a tavern, into the wrong ear, until it is a rumour, and a rumour walks farther than feet. It will have heard there was a town frightened of something it could not name, and come to learn whether the something was one of these, and to take it if it was." A beat.

"Which wall is the part worth fearing. No one showed it. It can feel these the way I can — they have a pull to them, faint and steady, a sound just below hearing — and it walked toward what it wanted the way water walks downhill. Nothing is directing it. Nothing has to."

"That's worse than someone telling it," I said.

"Yes," Third said.

"It usually is." Outside, the Scar had begun its crawl across the sky — that five-minute fade from full light toward the flat Anka dark. We had a walk to make before it finished, and the Herdukii by the gate to step over again, and I was going to be smelling this place for days whichever way the wind turned.

Mathen and Roan were waiting by the gap in the wall when we came out, Roan steadier now, Mathen with the particular lightness he put on when he'd decided the rest of us needed it.

"Anything?" Mathen asked.

"A direction," Third said.

"My favourite kind of nothing." Mathen fell into step. "Lead on, then. Away from the smell, ideally."

I took one last look back at the lockbox lid through the broken wall, lying where I'd set it. Hexwood, unmarked but for the torn hinges. Made to last generations. Opened in seconds, and left behind because it was not interesting.

"Let's go," I said.

Third was already at the gate.
