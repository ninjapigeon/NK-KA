# Four

I'd got as far as selecting the vegetables for the day's stew when Tondi came down, and that was when the morning stopped going the way mornings usually went.

I should explain about Tondi. She was the town's mender, which meant she stitched clothes and stitched people and was, by a margin nobody disputed, the most capable person in the settlement at both. She had grey hair that she wore loose and falling across her face, which made eye contact difficult, which I had eventually understood was the entire point. She did not touch money — she had a bucket, and you put your Black or your Teeth in the bucket, and that was the transaction. Nobody knew why. Nobody asked. You did not ask Tondi things. Tondi was a curmudgeon of such reliable and total commitment to the craft that the town had simply organised itself around the assumption that she would be unpleasant and correct, in roughly that order, about everything.

She had raised me, more or less, since I was old enough to carry things. I would not have said she was warm. I would have said she was the most dependable fixed point in my life, which is a different thing, and arguably better.

She came down the stairs from her room with the particular shuffle of someone who is awake but has not yet decided to be, took three steps into the main room, and saw Third.

The rocks were just starting to bounce off the roof, a loud plinking off the wood shingles. I have heard Tondi raise her voice perhaps four times in my life. This was one of them.

"Gah! What in the name of the Titans—"

And then she stopped.

The plinking continued, but abated slightly, as if waiting for our mender to gather herself. That was the thing I would think about afterward, when I had time to think about things. The scream was real — she was genuinely, fully startled, a grey-haired woman finding a Titanspawn sitting in the corner of her inn at first light. But the stopping was just as real, and faster than it had any right to be. One breath of pure shock, and then something behind her eyes simply went to work, sorting the impossible thing in the corner into categories, weighing it, and arriving — visibly, in the space of about two heartbeats — at a conclusion.

The conclusion was: this requires information, not panic.

"Tasha," she said, in a voice that had already climbed all the way back down to its normal register. "Did you let that in here."

It was not really a question. I answered it anyway, from the kitchen doorway, with a vegetable still in my hand.

"Morning, Tondi. Yes. It scared off a burglar last night, and it—" I gestured with the vegetable, which I realised partway through was not helping. "It's got the Shredder's head. The one Roan was supposed to ask about. So I figured I'd trust it with a chair."

Tondi looked at me. Then she looked at Third. Then she looked back at me with an expression that communicated, with great economy, that we would be discussing the limits of my judgement at a later date.

Third, to his credit, did the thing he always did when he became the subject of a room's attention, which was nothing. He simply turned his head toward Tondi, and waited.

Then he produced the Shredder's head.

I had seen him do this the night before and it had unsettled me then. In the morning light, with Tondi watching, it was somehow worse — the arm going around to the back without the body turning, the angles wrong, the whole motion carrying the casual competence of something that had been retrieving objects from impossible places for longer than the town had existed. The head came out and was held forth at arm's length, a gruesome offering between two intelligent beings, neither of whom was entirely sure what the correct etiquette was.

"This was what you sought," Third said. The voice did the thing it did — arrived in the chest before the ears. "As I understood it. If it is not sufficient, I will leave."

There was a silence then that I would not wish on anyone. Tondi standing very still. Third holding a decapitated Shredder head at arm's length. Me in the doorway with a vegetable. The fire not yet lit, the room cold, the light coming grey through the just-opened shutters.

The stony had passed. Tondi closed her eyes for a breath. I watched her hands, which had balled into fists at her sides, slowly uncurl. When she opened her eyes again, she had decided something, and Tondi deciding something was a force roughly equivalent to weather.

"Thank you," she said, "for ridding us of that beast." A pause, during which she was clearly choosing her next words with the care of someone disarming a trap. "I can see you're a reasonable… being."

The word being cost her something. I could hear it. But she said it, which was Tondi all over — she would give you the truth even when the truth was inconvenient to her own prejudices, because the alternative was being wrong, and Tondi would rather do almost anything than be wrong.

"That said," she went on, "some of the townsfolk—" She stopped, reconsidered, and corrected herself with the flat honesty that was the closest thing she had to a personality trait. "Most of the townsfolk will not understand. Would you mind setting yourself somewhere more out of the centre of the room. And—" she looked at the cloak, which was doing its usual poor job. "—covering yourself a bit more thoroughly. Scar's sake."

She did not wait for an answer. She walked to the corner she always sat in, the one with the best light for mending, and she picked up the cup of water I put out for her every morning, and she drank from it, and she almost visibly relaxed. Third looked down at its cloak, considering the problem of how to cover himself better. This required the use of an arm, so it set the Shredder's head down on the nearest table — without any apparent concern for the fact that it was a decapitated head on a table where people would later eat — and began trying to arrange the inadequate cloak into something more adequate.

I put the vegetable down and went over to Tondi, not for conversation, which one did not seek with Tondi, but because there was a tablecloth on the pile of her current projects that was past saving, stained and fraying, not worth the Black it would take to mend.

"That old cloth," I said. "It's done, isn't it. Not worth fixing."

She didn't look up. She made a sound that I had learned to interpret, over many spins, as assent delivered grudgingly enough to preserve her dignity.

I took the cloth.

"Here," I said to Third, and draped it over his back and shoulders, on top of the failing cloak. With the tablecloth added, and Third positioned away from the door and out of the direct sightline of anyone walking in, you would have to get fairly close to notice the hooves, or the wrongness of the arms, or the single blue glow where a face should have been.

Klar arrived for his shift just then, which was its own small mercy, because Klar arriving meant the morning had to resume being a morning. I took the Shredder's head off the table before he could see where it had been sitting and handed it to him, the smell still lingering to the old wood.

"Customer brought us a trophy," I said. "Think you can do something with it?"

Klar's whole face changed. He had not yet looked up enough to register the Titanspawn Forged sitting in the corner under a tablecloth, because he was only 5,100 spins and a Shredder head was the most interesting thing that had happened to him in a tenday.

"A Shredder head? You don't even—" He was already turning toward the kitchen, already planning. "Let me get a skewer."

And he was gone.

I looked at Third, in his corner, under his tablecloth, with his one blue eye, watching the room with the patience of something that had decided this was an acceptable place to be and had no particular feelings about how long it took. I looked at Tondi, mending, her water at her elbow, behaving as though there were not a Titanspawn in her inn.

Then I went and lit the stove, because the stew was not going to make itself, and because I had apparently decided, somewhere in the last few hours, that this was simply how the morning was going to go.

I did not examine that decision very closely either.
