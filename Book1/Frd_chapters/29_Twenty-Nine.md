# Twenty Nine

The last ordinary thing Mathen ever did was complain about the cold.

We had three nights of waiting in us by then, and the third had brought a chill up off the bare rock that even the fire couldn't argue with, and Mathen sat working feeling back into his hands and delivering, to no one, a long and structured grievance against the entire concept of weather that stands still. "A storm that *moves,*" he was saying, "I can respect. A storm that moves has somewhere to be. This—" he gestured at the cold, the dark, the cliff, all of it — "this is just a thing that has decided to sit on us out of spite, and I have decided, in return, to take it personally." Roan was almost smiling. That was the gift of Mathen, even then, even there: he could get a boy who'd been afraid for three nights to almost smile, by being theatrically furious at the temperature. He looked over at me to see if I'd liked the bit. I had. I'd liked all his bits. I gave him the small nod that meant *that one landed,* and he looked pleased, and that is the last face I have of him that is whole.

Because that was when the whispering started, and we all made the same mistake at the same time.

We thought it was the grey.

It came up under the wind, under the fire-crackle — a thin braided murmur with no source, words in no language at the edge of hearing, and every one of us turned the same way: toward the cliff. Of course toward the cliff. We had spent three nights learning that the cliff was where the wrong came from, the grey draining into it, the thing that knew Third, the whole vast danger we had walked across the world to stand in front of. So when the world began to whisper, we faced the danger we knew, all four of us, and we braced for the thing we were expecting.

And the ground came up behind us.

I have tried to set the next part down a dozen ways and they are all too slow. It was not slow. It was the fastest thing I have ever lived through and it is the one I can least bear to make quick.

The rock under the fallen slab heaved and split, and the thing came up out of it the way a fist comes up through paper — long, pale, wet, a body like an enormous worm with hands, actual hands, too many of them, sprouting from it at wrong intervals on long clawed arms, and it came up *between* us, into the middle of our braced and facing-the-wrong-way little circle.

And the whispering, which I had taken for the grey, came down on us all at once and *held* us.

I have to tell this part with care, because the way it is always told — in the joke, in the story, in every fireside Grask anyone ever laughed at — leaves out the thing that mattered, and the leaving-out is the whole reason we were no use. In Dorn's joke a Grask was a punchline. It burrowed and it waited and it ate the clever people while they argued and it said its line. A thing you could laugh at, a little, around a fire. The joke never once mentioned the whispering. The joke never said that when a Grask decides on you it puts a sound inside your skull that unstitches you from your own body — that you stand there with every muscle you own handed over to some other keeping, your mind shrieking the orders your training carved into your spine, and your spine simply does not answer. The joke left that out. I have since worked out why the joke leaves it out, and it is the worst thing I know about stories: the people who learn that part do not come back to put it in.

Mathen was the best blade among us. I want that on the record beside everything else. On any other ground, with half a breath of warning, Mathen would have taken that thing apart and made it look easy and told us about it for a tenday. He had no breath of warning and, in the end, he had no body to do it with, because the whispering had him the way it had all of us — frozen, aware, screaming inside a statue. I know he was screaming inside his, because I was screaming inside mine, and our eyes could still move, and his found mine, and I watched a master swordsman understand, in perfect stillness, that the one thing he was for was not going to be permitted to happen.

All of us but Third.

I don't know why the sound didn't take him. I have wondered since whether there is simply nothing in him for it to unstitch — that whatever a Grask reaches for in a mind, Third's is built of other stuff, the Titan's stuff, wrong for the hook, so that the whisper went through him and found no seam to catch. He was not frozen. He was already moving, the instant the rock broke, the way he was always already moving.

He was one step too far away.

That is the whole of it. That is the sentence I have had to live inside since. One step — the thing came up *between* us and Mathen was on its side of the gap and Third was on ours, and a Grask does not spend the breath a step would cost. Its front split open, four jaws peeling back like the petals of something obscene, and in the wet centre of them a pair of eyes, and they found Mathen, frozen, and the whispering drew itself into one clear sentence, almost gentle, in a voice like wet gravel:

*"Found you."*

Not the line. Even then — even frozen, even with the world ending a step away — my idiot filing mind caught it and would not let it go: *that is not the line.* The joke said *there you are,* light, almost a greeting, the sort of thing you'd say glad to a friend across a room. This said *found you,* flat and final, the way you'd say it of a thing that had always been yours and had only briefly been mislaid. The joke had got even that wrong. The joke had made it almost warm. There was nothing warm in it, and I understood in that frozen breath that every Grask anyone had ever laughed at had said this colder truer thing to someone who never lived to correct the joke, and that we had walked into this one armoured in a story that had lied to us about its teeth.

Then Third reached them, one step too late, and the world started again all at once.

The arms had Mathen — round the chest, dragging him toward the jaws and beginning to pull him *down,* toward the broken ground, toward the burrow — and the freezing broke as the thing's attention bent wholly to its prize, and Roan got Mathen's name out in a tearing shout, and I got my own body back and it was no use to anyone, and Mathen — with a Grask's claws in him and the dark coming up to take him — *laughed.* A short, wet, astonished laugh, the laugh of a man who has just watched a joke he told come true in the worst possible way, and he got it out, around the blood, looking not at the thing that was killing him but at us, at me—

"—*told you* it was a good one—"

And then Third was on it.

He did not fight it. Fighting is what you do to a thing that might win. Third arrived at the Grask the way the Scar arrives at the day — completely, without hurry, without doubt — and the Falkas came out and the wrong-jointed arms came off, one, two, the hands still clutching as they fell, and the thing made a sound I will not try to spell, and tried to do the one clever thing it knew, tried to drop back into its hole and take what it had, and Third put himself between it and the ground. He would not let it burrow. He would not let it take Mathen down into the dark where even he could not follow. He cut it off from the earth and then he cut it apart, methodically, the way he cut back the flora at the geysers — the same way he did every necessary terrible thing — until the four jaws stopped working and the eyes in the wet of them went flat and the long pale body slumped down half in and half out of its own ruined hole, and the whispering stopped, and the only sound on that whole rearranging plain was Roan saying *no, no, no,* very fast and very quietly, over Mathen.

Third turned from the dead thing to the dying man and I watched something happen in him that I have no good word for. He knelt. He, who knelt to nothing. And he gathered Mathen up out of the bloodied moss the way I had seen him, in my mind's eye only, gather up a green-skinned infant from another field of the wrongly dead, and he held him, and the rest of us came down around them in the cold.

"Mathen," Third said.

"Don't," Mathen got out. He was going. We all knew the look by now; the road had taught us that look. "Don't do the — flat thing. The stating-facts thing. Not for this." A breath that didn't go all the way in. "Make it a *story.* Promise me you'll — you'll make it a story, not a — not a list of what the thing did to me. Tell it so they laugh at the start." His hand found Third's wooden forearm, the one that held him. "A Mender, a Crafter, and a *Grask.* Eh? You've got the — you've got the ending now. Free of charge."

"I have it," Third said. The avalanche of his voice had gone to almost nothing. "I will tell it well. I will learn to tell it well, the way you would have."

"Spread the weight," Mathen said. It was nearly the last of it. His eyes went, with effort, from Third to me, and stopped on me, and there was something in them he was trying to hand over, the way you hand over a watch. "She knows how. Taught her m'self." The ghost of the grin. "Stronger in the — back. Told you." And then, to Third, fading, the thing I did not understand was a gift until much later, until the cliff, until everything: "Don't you stop, you old — you keep *going.* Whatever's coming. You keep — you go *on.* That's the whole — that's the whole trick of it. You just—"

He didn't finish the sentence. I have spent a long time deciding how it ended, and I have settled, for my own sake, on *you just go on,* because it was the thing he'd have said, and because it was, though he never knew it, the exact armour a dead woman in the snow had once handed Third for exactly this, given again now by a dying man who only meant be brave, be kind, don't let the road harden you — and who could not have known he was loading the one weapon his friend was going to need against the offer of getting him, and all the rest of them, back.

We burned him at the rim, on a fallen page of the great book, the way Mathen had once told me he'd want if it came to it — "somewhere with a view, and don't any of you dare be solemn about it, I'll know." We tried not to be solemn. We were terrible at it. Roan said three words and couldn't do the rest. I did a little better and hated every steady syllable. Third spoke last, and he did not state facts; he told a small story, badly, about a man who cooked well and complained beautifully and could make a frightened boy almost smile on the worst night of his life, and it was the first story Third ever told without being asked, and Mathen would have given him notes, and that is the kindest thing I can think to say about it.

And when it was done, and the others had gone to the fire, I stood a moment longer by the embers with Third, and I felt the new thing settle on me, the thing I had not had room to feel until the burning was over — that Mathen was in the grey now. That the warm part had him. That somewhere behind that drinking cliff the thing that wanted Third to come home had just been handed one more reason, one more face, one more voice that could be offered back, *whole, in the warm, forever, if you only stop holding the door.* The world had not taken Mathen from Third. It had taken Mathen and *given him to the enemy,* to hold up at the end like bait.

I did not say it. Third knew it. I could see he knew it, the coal of his eye on the embers, doing the arithmetic of his own grief and finding, at the bottom of it, the trap.

"Three of us now," he said at last.

"Three of us now," I said. And I took hold of his forearm, where Mathen's hand had been, and I did not let go, because I had a job now, the cruellest job in the world, and it had just gotten one face harder to do.
