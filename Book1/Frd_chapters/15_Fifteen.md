# Fifteen

A party of three has a shape, and the shape is a line. We'd walked it for a tenday without my ever thinking about it: Third in front because Third was always in front, me behind him because his back was the safest place in the world to put your eyes, and Roan behind me with his spear and his checking. A line. You don't talk much in a line. You talk to the back of a head, or you don't, and mostly we hadn't.

Four made a different shape, and Mathen made it on purpose.

He'd worked out by the second morning that the line was a thing to be dismantled, and he went about it the way I'd later learn he went about everything — sideways, cheerfully, and without once announcing what he was doing. He'd fall back and walk with Roan a while and get him talking about the militia, about the town, about the girl Roan never quite named; then he'd drift up and walk with me and ask the kind of question that has no defensible reason not to answer, so that I'd answer it before I'd decided to; then he'd lengthen his stride and pull even with Third, which nobody had done, and just *walk there*, companionable, as if walking beside the oldest thing in the world were a thing a person did.

By the fourth day the line was gone. We walked in a loose knot that rearranged itself like weather, and there was talk in it, the ordinary unimportant talk that I hadn't known a group could make until I heard ours start making it, and I understood that this was a thing Mathen *did* — that he was, in some way I didn't have a trade-word for, a mender too. Tondi fixed seams. Mathen fixed the spaces between people. He'd just walked into the middle of three strangers and begun quietly stitching them into a company.

He cooked, too, and he cooked *well*, which after a tenday of my road-fat-and-resentment was its own small revolution. The first night he took over the fire was the night we camped under the wall.

I call it a wall. It was a wall the way the causeway was a bridge — a thing that wore the word loosely and was really something else. It rose out of the grassland a half-day spinward of us, sheer and seamless and grey, as high as a hill and longer than sight, and it went *nowhere.* It did not ring a thing, or hold back a thing, or join a thing. It started out of flat ground at one end and stopped at flat ground at the other and in between it was simply a wall, a colossal straight nothing the Titan had begun and never finished, or finished and never explained, and the wind came round its end with a low constant note like someone blowing across a jug the size of the world. We camped in the lee of it because the lee was out of the wind. That was the only use it had ever been to anyone, and it had not been built for it, and a hundred generations of travellers had warmed themselves against the side of a thing whose entire purpose had died with the child that wanted it.

And there, in the windbreak of an abandoned god-sized whim, the first night he took over the fire, Mathen produced from the same dried fish and tubers I'd been ruining something a person would willingly eat twice, and he did it while talking the entire time, and he served Third a portion.

"I don't eat," Third said. It was not a complaint. Third stated facts about himself the way he stated facts about the weather.

"I know," Mathen said, setting the bowl down by Third's hoof anyway. "But a man cooks for his company, and you're my company, and if I leave you out it'll show on the count. Let it go cold. Let the smell do the work. My gran used to set a bowl for her brother forty spins after he passed, and nobody ever told her it was strange, because it wasn't." He sat. "You can give it to the guard when I'm not looking. He's a dozen kinds of hungry."

"I'm seven thousand spins old," Roan said, around a mouthful, with the dignity of a grown man who has been a guardsman for some spins and would like that noticed.

"You're a growing boy," said Mathen serenely, "and you've a growing boy's appetite, and I respect it," and Roan, who'd spent four spins trying to be taken seriously by an actual swordsman, found himself grinning at being called a boy, which is a difficult trick to do to a grown man and Mathen did it without seeming to try.

The trouble started, as it would keep starting, with a story.

Mathen told them most nights — he couldn't not, it was the same compulsion in him that made Third correct a wrong fact, the exact inverse engine — and the third or fourth night he told the one about the breaking of the sky. The Titans' war. He told it well. He told it with the storm-Titan and the magic-Titan and the glutton each given a voice, with the Virek — may they never be forgotten — host shining and doomed and noble, with a last stand on a field of glass and a hero with a carved bow who put the final arrow through the final Titan's heart while the world held its breath.

It was a good story. Roan was rapt. I was halfway rapt myself, and I'd stopped believing in heroes around the time I started closing up the inn alone.

Across the fire, Third had gone very still. Not the stillness that went down into him. The other kind — a held, mounting stillness, the stillness of a kettle deciding.

"That is not how it was," Third said.

"Course it isn't," Mathen said comfortably, not stopping. "It's how it's *told.*"

"The storm and the magic and the glutton were not three. There was—" Third stopped himself, and I watched him put something back behind a door, something too large for the firelight, and choose a smaller true thing to say instead. "There were not separate Titans warring as rivals. That is a tidiness the telling adds. And the Virek were not shining. They were frightened, and they were clever, and they were dying, and there was nothing noble in any of it, there was only people who did not want to be playthings doing a terrible thing to stop being played with. I was on that field. There was no breath held. There was screaming, and then there was a great deal of dust, and the screaming went on under the dust for some time."

The fire popped. Roan had stopped chewing.

"And the hero with the bow," Third said, quieter. "There was a boy with a bow. He was not a hero. He was terrified and he was perhaps fourteen spins past being a child, and the bow was beautiful, and after it was over he sat up in the grass and said 'oh, I thought I was dead,' and then he saw me and pointed it at me, because that is what you do, when you are a frightened boy and a monster is standing over you." The eye dimmed. "I would prefer he were remembered as he was. I find I am the only one left who could correct the record, and I have just done it, and it has changed nothing, because his is not the version that gets told."

There was a silence after that with a great deal in it.

I'd have let it sit. I am good at letting things sit. But Mathen set down his bowl, and leaned forward, and his face had lost the performing ease and found something underneath it I hadn't seen yet — the real man, who took stories more seriously than anyone I'd ever met precisely because he knew exactly what they were for.

"Then tell it," Mathen said. "The true one. Not to argue mine down — mine's for strangers in a pub who want a Titan and a hero and a clean ending, and they're owed that, it's a fair trade for their coin. But the true one's owed to *somebody.* That boy's owed it. You're the last who carries him, and when you go down — three hundred thousand spins or no, everything goes down eventually — he goes with you, unless somebody else is carrying him too." He sat back. "That's all a story is, ser. It's a way of making more than one person carry the thing. So that it doesn't all rest on you."

Third looked at him across the fire for a long moment.

"You are arguing that I should make inaccurate things," he said slowly, "in order to preserve a true one."

"I'm arguing," said Mathen, "that *accurate* and *carried* aren't the same, and that you've spent a long time keeping things accurate by keeping them to yourself, and that it hasn't kept them safe, it's just kept them *yours.*" He shrugged, the ease coming back over him like a cloak he'd taken off for exactly one sentence. "Anyway. You don't have to tonight. I just think you've got the oldest true story walking around in you that anybody's ever met, and it'd be a crime against my whole profession to let it walk off uncopied." He grinned. "Think of me as greedy. It's more comfortable for everyone."

Third didn't answer. But he didn't close the door all the way either — I could tell, by then, the difference between Third deciding *no* and Third deciding *not yet.* This was the second one.

Later, when Roan was asleep and Third had stepped off to walk the dark the way he did, Mathen and I sat with the coals.

"You do it to everyone," I said. "Get them talking. I noticed on the road."

"I do." He didn't pretend otherwise. That was a thing about Mathen — he'd lie all night in a story and not at all out of one. "It's a habit. Habits come from somewhere." He turned a stick in the fire. "I had a family of the talking kind. Loud table, everybody at once. I got good at making space in it for the quiet ones, because there was one of us who was quiet, and somebody had to." A beat, the smallest catch in the easy voice, a door I heard close before I'd properly seen it open. "Anyway. Old habit. Cheaper than the alternative."

I noticed he'd used Third's phrase. *Cheaper than the alternative.* I wondered if he'd caught it from Third or if Third had caught it from someone like Mathen, a long time ago, and it had been rattling around in him for three hundred thousand spins waiting to come back out.

"And me?" I said. "What are you making space for, with me?"

He looked at me then, the storyteller's look, the one that reads you. I felt the question I hadn't asked get asked anyway, the *what are you running from* of it, sitting there in the firelight as plain as if he'd said it.

And then, kindly, he let it go.

"Nothing," he said. "You don't need the space made. You make your own, and you keep it swept, and you don't let anybody track mud in. I respect it. I'll just sit near it, if that's all right, and you can use it or not." He poked the fire. "That's the other half of the trade. Knowing which ones want the door opened and which ones are holding it shut for a reason."

I didn't say anything. But I didn't get up either, and we sat near my swept and guarded space together, and it was — I'll use the word, because there was so little of it coming and I want it on the record — it was companionable. It was a good night. I had them more often, that stretch of the road, than I'd had in a long while, and I didn't know to be afraid of how good they were.

Across the dark, at the edge of where the firelight failed, Third stood his watch.

He'd taken to standing a little apart from us, those nights — not far, never far, but apart, the way you stand back from a fire that's burning well, because you've learned what fires do and you've no wish to be in one again when it goes out. I didn't understand it then. I thought it was only his nature, the old solitude reasserting itself between jobs.

I know now he was doing arithmetic. He'd said as much, at the geysers — *I am only counting; it is a habit.* He had acquired three of us, and he had not expected to acquire any, and somewhere in the part of him the gears turned and didn't make sense, he was already counting the cost of each of us, the way a man who has been burned counts the exits before he sits down. Not because he didn't want us. Because he did, and he knew exactly what wanting cost, and he had paid it before, in a currency I hadn't yet learned he'd ever held.

He watched Mathen make us a company.

And he let him.

That was the thing, looking back — that was the bravest thing I watched Third do on that whole road, and I didn't even see it for what it was at the time. He let Mathen stitch us together, knowing what stitching costs when it tears. He stood a little apart, and counted, and let it happen anyway.
