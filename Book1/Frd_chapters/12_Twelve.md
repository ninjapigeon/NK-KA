# Twelve

We came off the causeway after travelling it for two days, by one of the corkscrew ramps. The last of the high country had been more of the world insisting on itself — a stretch where the trail crossed a field of low sterile rings, perfect circles of bare grey dirt a few paces across with a sickly green tree dead-centre of each, no grass, no weed, nothing growing under them but the next identical sapling pushing up at the ring's edge to start its own circle of starvation. These I knew — occluding trees, the farmers near home called them, and hated them — and we walked between the rings the way you'd walk between graves, and nobody spoke, and the trees drank the life out of the dirt in their tidy obsessive circles and asked us for nothing. So when the ramp let us down into ordinary country that felt like a held breath let out, and for half a day the trail was just a trail and the trees were just trees, I believed in the world again — not because the world had earned it, but because I wanted it so badly that half a day of plain green was enough to buy me.

Then the trees changed.

It wasn't sudden. That was the first wrong thing about it — there was no edge to the place, no line like the held-back margin of the causeway, no moment you could point to and say *here, it starts here.* The ordinary forest simply thinned and was replaced, tree by tree, the way one word in a sentence gets replaced until you look up and the sentence means something else, and by the time I understood what I was looking at we had been walking in it for some while already, which I did not like, because it meant the place had got in before I noticed the door.

Every tree was bent the same way.

Not bent like wind-bent, all leaning from a prevailing weather. Not bent like reaching, the way trees crowd toward light. Each trunk came up out of the ground and then turned — a long, smooth, deliberate-looking curve, low down, near the root — and then straightened again and went up true, so that every tree in that forest stood vertical at the top and yet carried, down near the earth, the identical memory of having once leaned hard in a single direction. The same direction. Every one. As far into the green as I could see, a whole forest of trunks all making the same turn at the same height, like a field of people caught in the instant of flinching from the same blow.

"Snake Forest," Roan said. His voice came out wrong, too flat, the voice of someone keeping a lid on. "My gran used to — there's a rhyme. About not counting the trees." He stopped. "I don't remember how it goes."

"Then do not try to remember it," Third said.

There is nothing to tell about the Snake Forest, which is exactly the thing that is hard to tell. Nothing happened. Nothing moved. Nothing came out of the green at us with teeth. If you asked me to list the events of the worst stretch of country we crossed in all that journey, I would have nothing to give you but a list of things that did not occur, and the slow unbearable accumulation of them.

The trees did not move. I want that on the record, because the whole long day was a war against the conviction that they had. You would look at one — a particular trunk, its curve, the way it almost made a shape if you held it wrong in your eye — and you would walk, and you would look away to mind your feet, and when you looked back the conviction would be on you cold and total that it had shifted while your eyes were elsewhere, that the curve was at a different angle now, that it had been facing the path and was now facing *you.* And you could not prove it hadn't. That was the engine of the place. You could never prove it hadn't, because you had looked away, and looking away was the one thing a body cannot stop itself doing.

It got into your head through that crack and then it widened the crack.

The thing I have never been able to explain to anyone is how a place with no enemy in it can exhaust you more thoroughly than a fight. By midday — if it was midday; the Scar's light came down grey and even through the canopy and gave us nothing to steer by — Roan had stopped talking entirely and walked with his hand wrapped white-knuckled around the haft of his spear, turning, every few paces, to check the trees behind us. There was nothing behind us. He checked anyway. He could not stop checking, and the checking was eating him, and there was nothing I could say that the place did not immediately turn sour in my mouth before I could say it.

Because it was getting into me too, and it had found the door it wanted.

I am good at not thinking about things. I have said this already and it is the truest thing about me and it is, I understand now, the reason the Snake Forest nearly had me — because a place whose whole craft is making you think about things had, in me, found a person who had spent her whole life building a door against exactly that, and there is nothing a pressure loves like a door. It got my father first. It got him up out of wherever I keep him and stood him in front of me on the trail — not a memory, nothing so kind as a memory, just the fact of him, the *elsewhere* of him, the question I never let myself ask about why elsewhere had always been so easy for him to choose. Then it got the inn, and Tondi, and the needles over my heart, and the cold arithmetic of how unlikely it was that I would ever use them anywhere she would hear how it went. Then it got the simplest, worst one, the one I had been not-thinking since the gate: *what are you doing out here. You serve stew. You are going to die in the green for a wooden man's quarrel and no one will ever find the pieces.*

"He's not even one of us," Roan said.

I don't know how long he'd been walking with that in him before it came out. It came out quiet and reasonable and absolutely poisonous, and it was aimed at Third's back, ten paces ahead.

"He doesn't feel it," Roan said. "Look at him. Walks through here like it's a meadow. He doesn't feel any of it, and he's the reason we're in it, and we're going to leave our minds in these trees so he can chase a — a thing he isn't even sure is real—"

"Roan." It came out of me harder than I meant, and meaner, and the meanness felt *good*, which was how I knew. "Shut your mouth."

"You shut yours. You've wanted to say it too. I've watched you—"

"I have known you four days and every one of them you've needed someone to tell you you're brave—"

I heard it land. I watched it land on him, the exact cruel place I'd aimed it without deciding to aim anywhere, and the small hot satisfaction of having hurt him curdled even as it arrived, and that was the thing, that was the whole horror of the place laid bare in a sentence: it did not invent anything. It had nothing to put in us that we had not brought in ourselves. It only reached in, and found the unkindest true thing, and helped us say it.

"Stop walking," Third said.

He had turned around. The glow of his eye moved over the two of us — Roan with his spear half come up off his shoulder, me with my hands in fists I didn't remember making — and whatever he read there, he set himself down on the path between us and the green ahead, facing back the way we'd look, and he said:

"Listen to me, because the trees would prefer that you did not. They do not move. I have been watching them since we entered, every one within reach of my eye, without once looking away — I do not need to look away; it is among the few advantages of being made the way I was made. They have not moved. Not one of them, not the width of a hair. The thing in your minds telling you otherwise is the only danger in this place, and it is lying, and I am the proof of it. You do not have to watch the trees. I will watch the trees. That is mine. Yours is only to walk, and to know that what you have just said to each other was said by the forest, in your voices, because it has no voice of its own."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"I'm sorry," Roan said, to the ground.

"Me too," I said, and meant it, and felt the meaning come back to me from a long way off, like something thawing.

"You will both be sorry several more times before we are clear," Third said, and stood, and something in the flatness of it was almost — not a joke. Third did not make jokes. But the shape where a joke would go, offered to us deliberately, like a hand. "It is the nature of the place. Apologise as often as you need to. It is cheaper than the alternative, and I will not keep count."

So we walked, and Third walked ahead of us facing backward as often as forward, his eye on the trees, taking the watching off us and onto himself, and it helped. It genuinely helped, to be relieved of the trees, to have one fixed certain thing — *he is watching; if they move he will say* — to lean the mind against.

It was perhaps an hour after that, in the worst of the grey afternoon, that I heard Third say, low, not to me:

"No."

I looked up. He was facing forward, the eye on the path, and there was no one he could have been answering.

"No," he said again, quieter, the way you'd answer a thing that had asked the same question many times before and would ask it many more. And then a word I didn't catch, and then nothing, and his hand had closed and opened once at his side, the way Roan's kept closing on his spear.

"Third?" I said. "Who are you talking to?"

The eye came to me. For a moment — only a moment — the glow had a hardness in it I hadn't seen since the night the wounded traveller spoke of the thing that walked through the dead, a held thing, a door of his own with something leaning on it from the other side.

"Myself," he said. "It is an old conversation. The forest has merely turned up its volume." The glow eased. "Do not be concerned. I have been refusing this particular argument for a very long time. The trees have nothing to teach me about it that I have not already declined to learn."

He didn't explain, and I had no idea then what the argument was, or who the other voice in it belonged to, or that it had been speaking to him without rest since before my grandmother's grandmother served her first stew. I only knew that whatever it was, the Snake Forest had found his door the same as it had found mine and Roan's — and that the difference between us was not that the place could not reach him.

It was that he had had three hundred thousand spins of practice keeping the door shut.

We came out of it the way we'd gone in, with no edge to mark the leaving, the bent trunks giving way tree by tree to honest crooked ordinary ones, until I looked up and the forest behind us was just a forest and the thing in my head had let go its grip so gently I couldn't say when.

We made camp early, well clear of the last of the bent trees, and none of us suggested going further though there was light left. Roan built the fire too big. I let him. We didn't talk about the things we'd said, but Roan took first watch without being asked, and when I woke in the dark he was sitting close to the coals with his spear across his knees, and he looked over at me, and in the firelight he looked like a child — younger than his spins, young enough that guarding anything seemed a cruel joke to have handed him.

"It wasn't true," he said. "What you said. I mean — it was. But it wasn't the why of it. I don't need someone to tell me I'm brave." He poked the fire. "I need to find out if I am. They turn out to be different things, when you're actually out here. I didn't know that before."

"It wasn't true what I said either," I told him. "You're the least cowardly person I've ever watched be frightened. That's a better thing than not being frightened. Mathen would have a line for it." I stopped. I didn't know who Mathen was. I had never heard the name. It had come up out of me from nowhere, the way the bent trees came up out of the ground, and I put it down to the forest, to a day of having my mind turned over, and I let it go.

Across the fire, Third sat with his eye on the dark, watching nothing, watching everything, keeping a door shut that I would not understand for a long time yet.

In the morning the trail was clear and the trees were honest and we walked on toward the geysers, where everything would change again, though we didn't know that yet either.
