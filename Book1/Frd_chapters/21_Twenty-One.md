# Twenty One

The blizzard was waiting for us on the far side of a warm afternoon, and it was not coming, and it was not going. It simply stood.

We came down a long gold slope into grazing country, the kind of soft easy ground that lets you forget for a while, and there across the bottom of it was a wall of white. Not cloud. Snow — falling snow, a curtain of it from the ground to as high as I could see, churning, the wind inside it loud enough to hear from where we stood in the warm — and on our side of the line the grass was green and the air was mild and a few paces beyond it the world was a screaming whiteout, and the line between the two was as straight and as hard as the edge of a table.

I had stopped walking without deciding to. "That's—"

"Weather," Mathen said, in the tone of a man who has seen enough of Anka not to argue with it. "It does that, out here. Stands. Or walks at you in a wall, which is worse — you can outrun a storm that knows where it's going. This kind just sits and waits to see if you're fool enough to need the ground it's sitting on." He squinted at it. "We need the ground it's sitting on."

"It'll end," Third said. "They do. But not soon, and not for our convenience. We go through. Keep close, keep the rope, and do not stop inside it — inside it is where it wants you to stop."

We tied on, and we went through the table-edge of it, and the warm afternoon was simply gone, cut off behind us like a closed door, and we were in the white.

I have been cold since. I have not been in anything like that. It was not the cold that took you, it was the *sameness* — no up, no down, no forward that looked different from back, no sound but the one enormous sound, the world reduced to a roaring white nothing with four roped figures struggling through the middle of it. Roan vanished to a shape at rope's-length. The wind ate our voices. And in the middle of it, where you could see nothing and hear nothing and the world had stopped being a world and become only a white waiting, I felt the rope go slack ahead of me, and I knew, the way I knew things about Third now, that he had gone — not fallen, not lost. Stepped. Beside. Into the only quiet there was.

I held the slack rope and I waited, in the screaming white, for him to come back from wherever the stillness had sent him.

After Liwon, Third stopped.

He did not decide to. There was no moment he could afterward point to and say *there, that is where I chose to stop* — there was only the carrying of her, the small weight of what had been her, to a place with a tree he thought she would have liked, and the putting of her into the ground, and the sitting down beside the place to make certain nothing disturbed it, and then the not getting up.

He told himself, at first, that he was keeping watch. That was a thing he knew how to do; he could do it for a long time. But the watch had no end written into it, because the thing he was guarding was past being harmed, and a watch with no end is only a way of describing a thing that has stopped.

The Scar swung overhead, dark and light and dark, faster than it should have, or his sense of it loosened — he could not afterward say which. Greenery came up around him. A vine found his still arm and climbed it and he did not brush it off, because brushing it off was a thing a person did, and he had stopped being sure he was one. The weather passed over him the way water passes over a stone in a stream: he felt it, distantly, as pressure and temperature, and it wore at him, and he let it. He stepped to the grey more and more — a breath gone from the hillside, a long while on the far side of it, the warm part, the part where she still got the rhyme right — and he stayed longer and longer inside each breath, and minded less and less the coming back, until the coming back was the only part he still did out of habit, and even the habit was wearing thin.

He was, he understood without alarm, dying. Not the way the soft ones died. The way a thing winds down. The lights in his chest he could always have kept — he had kept them three hundred thousand spins, a bottle scrounged here, bought there, pried from some dead place, none of the small ones lasting much past a hundred spins before they went to ash, so that staying lit had never been the hard part, only the bothering. And it was the bothering that was failing now: the will of him, the part the Titan had not built and had no name for, going out like a banked fire nobody fed.

He watched the dim light gutter, and did not get up, and did not go looking for the next. He had been there long enough to be more shrub than statue when the woman sat down in front of him.

She did it without ceremony, the way you'd sit on any convenient rock, and she looked at him — at the eye, which still lit, the one thing the vines had not taken — and she said, "You're not dead. I've buried dead. You're something else."

Third did not answer. Answering was a thing a person did.

"That's all right," she said. "I'll talk. I'm good at it, and you appear to have the time."

Her name, he would learn over the slow spins that followed, was Lujian, and she was a wanderer of the particular kind that the loss of everything sometimes makes — not aimless, but unanchored, moving because the places she stopped all had holes in them shaped like people who weren't there. She had been a scholar of something once; she still had the habits of it, the naming, the patience, the need to understand a thing before she would consent to fear it. She had lost — she told him this plainly, early, the way you'd hand a stranger your name — a husband and both her children, in one of the small endless wars the sundered peoples had been making of the world since the night the veins came, and she had walked away from the graves of them and not stopped walking, and she was not, she said, looking for anything, except possibly a reason to be looking for something.

She found the stopped Forged, and she did not loot it, and she did not fear it, and she did not try to wake it. She simply began, each day, to sit in front of it and talk, and to clear, idly, a little of the growth from its joints while she did, the way you'd worry a loose thread.

It took a long time. She talked about the war, and the towns, and the two peoples who could not stop killing each other, and the grey philosophies of a poet she'd loved whose name Third would hear again much later from another mouth on another road. And one day — he did not know how many spins in, the count had stopped meaning anything — she said the thing that woke him, and she did not say it to wake him, she said it because it was true and she was tired and she had stopped, a little, performing patience.

"I don't know what you lost," Lujian said, to the silent overgrown thing. "Something. You've the shape of a thing that lost something. I know the shape; I wear it." She pulled a vine off his wrist. "Here's what I've worked out, in all my walking. There's a thing the world wants you to believe, when it takes someone. It wants you to believe it was *for* something. That it'll make sense later. That they're a — seed, or a lesson, or a step on the way to some better thing, and if you just hold still and trust the design, the grief'll turn into a meaning and you'll be glad." Her voice had gone hard, not at him. "It's a lie. It's the kindest-sounding lie there is and it's still a lie. They weren't *for* anything. They were *theirs.* And the only thing your stopping does — this, the vines, the winding down — the only thing it does is finish the job. It turns them into exactly what the liar wanted. A thing that happened on the way to you sitting down."

The eye, which had not moved in a thousand spins, moved.

"There it is," Lujian said softly, not surprised, only tired and a little glad. "No. Don't — you don't have to say anything. I'm only telling you. If you want them to have been a person and not a *reason*, you don't get to stop. Stopping agrees with the liar. Going on is the only way left to call it what it was." She sat back. "It's not a comfort. I know it's not. It's the opposite of a comfort. It's a debt. But it's theirs, and it's yours, and I'd think a thing that's carried a glaive since before there were towns to carry it past could carry one more thing that heavy."

Third's hand, when he lifted it — the first thing he had moved on purpose in longer than he could hold — closed around the vine she had been worrying, and pulled it, slow, from the wood of his arm.

"They were a child," he said. The voice came out of him cracked and disused, a landslide that had forgotten the shape of words. "I called her little one. She made it into a name."

Lujian nodded, the way you nod at a thing set finally down between you. "Then she had a name," she said. "That's more than most of mine got, at the end. Come on. You've a great deal of going on to do, and the first step's the worst of it; after that it's only walking."

He stood. The growth tore away from him in sheets. He had been a long time stopped, and the standing was its own kind of agony, every wound he had let go unmended announcing itself at once — and under the agony, faint, unwanted, the terrible relief of a debt accepted.

He did not stay stopped again. Whatever else the spins did to him after — and they did the usual things, they gave him Lujian's company on the road for a good span and then, in the way of all of it, they took her too, quieter than Liwon, an old woman's ordinary end with Third sitting the watch he actually knew how to sit this time, the kind with an end in it — he did not, ever again, sit down to wind himself out. She had made that impossible. She had turned his stopping into a thing he would have been doing *to* Liwon, and he would not do one more thing to Liwon, not even the mercy of an ending.

It was, he understood much later, the same refusal twice. The Titan had said *seed.* Lujian had said *hers.* And every spin Third kept walking was a spin he spent on Lujian's side of it.

He stepped back into the white.

The rope went taut. He was there, ahead of me, a dark shape resolving in the white, leaning back into the line of it, and we struggled the last of the way through the blizzard's far wall and out the table-edge of it into — of course — a mild grey ordinary evening, dripping, the screaming cold sealed behind us like it had never been, already shrinking at our backs to a white wall standing patient over grass.

We were all of us soaked and shaking, except Third, who did not shake, but whose eye had gone to the coal again, the lowest ember, the look I had learned meant he had been somewhere and paid for it.

I got the rope off and went to him while Mathen got a fire fighting the damp and Roan stamped feeling back into his feet.

"You went," I said. Not a question; we were past those.

"I did."

"Her again?" I had earned that much, I thought — the one word he'd given me, *her*, and the right to use it.

"Not only her, this time." The ember considered the shrinking white wall. "Someone who came after. Who found me when I had — stopped. And made me start." A pause. "She has been gone a long time also. They are all gone a long time, Tasha. That is the part the grey does not change. I can have the warm parts back, as often as I like, for as long as I can stand them. I cannot have any of them *back.*"

I didn't say anything for a moment. The pieces were on the table, and I had learned to look at them now, and a new one had just been set down, and I did not like the shape it made with the others.

"How many," I said. "Have there been. People who — found you. Started you."

The eye came round to me, and held, and I understood he knew exactly why I was asking, and that he was going to answer honestly because he had decided, somewhere back there in the white, that he was done managing me.

"A few," Third said. "Across a long time. Not many. The child. The one in the snow just now. A swordsman who told a joke about a Grask. A woman who was unwell." The ember was steady. "And a girl who served stew, who followed me out of an inn because she was bored, and who has lately begun to look at me as though she has worked out that she is on a list."

I had. That was the thing. I had just worked out exactly that.

"It's not a curse," he said, gently, which from Third was an enormous gentleness. "I want to be clear. You are not doomed by standing near me. Most of them died of the ordinary things, in the ordinary times, that the soft ones die of, and would have died of them whether they had met me or not. I am not the thing that kills them." The ember dimmed. "I am only the thing that remembers all of them. That is a different curse, and it is mine, and it is not catching."

"That's not better," I said. My voice came out smaller than I meant it.

"No," Third agreed. "It is not better. But it is true, and you have lately preferred true, even when it is not better. I thought you should have this one accurately."

Behind us the blizzard stood its patient ground over the grass, going nowhere, waiting for the next fools who needed the dirt beneath it. Ahead the trail ran on alongside the rim, toward the fields where the glutton's teeth lay scattered, toward the place all the lines pointed. And I walked it knowing, now, that I was the latest entry in a list a very old creature had been keeping for three hundred thousand spins, and that the list had only ever ended one way, and that I was going to keep walking it anyway — which was, I understood with a lurch, exactly the choice a woman named Lujian had once talked him into, in a different ruin, a long time ago.

Going on was the only way to call it what it was.
