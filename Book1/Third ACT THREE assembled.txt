

We knew it was the day because the field stopped pretending.

For a tenday the open book had played its patient game — moved only when we looked away, settled its
great canting pages a little nearer or a little further each time our backs were turned, so that you
could almost believe you'd misremembered, almost believe stone behaved. That morning it stopped
bothering. We woke to a field rearranged in plain sight, not while we slept but while we watched: a
slab the size of a barn sliding its base across bare rock with a sound like a slow tooth, grinding
itself a hand's breadth spireward and stopping, as if it had remembered it no longer needed to be
shy. The grey was running so hard toward the cliff by then that even I could feel it, and I have no
gift for the grey at all — a tug, low in the chest, the exact place you'd press if you were trying
not to be sick, except the pull was outward, toward the pale wall, as though the morning itself were
draining into it.

Third stood at the rim with his back to our dead fire and looked at the cliff the way you look at a
debt you have always known you would have to pay.

"He is on the field," he said. "He came in the dark, while we kept the watch facing the
wrong way again." A dry sound, not quite the bellows. "We are very bad at facing the right way. It
is, I think, the most human thing about all of us." The eye came round. "It is today. Do you still
mean to come, or have you the sense the night might have given you?"

"Don't," I said. "You know I'm coming. Roan?"

"Coming," Roan said, and checked the lashings on his spear for the fourth time that hour, which was
how Roan said *I am terrified and I am coming anyway,* and which I had learned not to comment on.

"Then we go to it," Third said, "while we can still choose the ground. He will make for the spine.
The spine is the last of it — the last pin, the place the whole shape is hung from. If he reaches it
unopposed he has everything he needs and we have a long walk home with nothing to show for it but
our lives, which he will not let us keep regardless." He took up his Glaive, the bolter, and his falcatas, and
he did not put on the cloak. He had no more use for looking like a man.

---

I have crossed bad country. I grew up a walk from a black swamp; I had been over the Gnawing Fields
with their floating teeth and their pale plinths; I had stood a tenday at the rim of this
place watching it crawl. None of it taught me what it was to walk *into* the open book, out among the
pages, with the rule of the place — *do not look away* — turned from a curiosity into the only law
that mattered.

Because you cannot watch everything. That is the whole cruelty of it. You have a front and the field
has no manners about which direction it uses. We crossed in a tight knot, the three of us, each facing
a third of the world, turning our heads in a slow shared sweep we fell into without discussing, like
oarsmen finding a stroke — and still, every time the sweep came round, something was *different.* A
slab that had leaned left now leaned right. A gap we'd meant to walk through had closed to a seam. A
flat page we'd marked as our path had tilted in the breath our eyes were elsewhere and become a wall,
and we had to find the path again from nothing, in a place that rearranged its own evidence the
instant we trusted it.

Roan said, once, very low, "It's like being watched by something that stops the moment you turn." And
none of us answered, because he was exactly right and there was nothing to add to it except more fear.

The pages got bigger as we went in. At the rim they had been barn-sized; here they were the size of
town walls, of hillsides, vast flat sheets of pale stone canted at angles that hurt to stand under,
each one a leaf of a book the size of a country laid open and reading itself in the dark. And between
them, in the low places, the grey had come up out of the ground. I had never seen it from the lived
side — Third's country, not mine — but there it was, pooled in the troughs between the great stones
like ground-fog that had forgotten to be white, a faint moving greyness with the wrongest light in it,
all of it sliding, all of it pouring, the same direction we were walking. Toward the wall.

---

The wall took its toll on Third by lengths.

I'd promised to watch him and I watched him, and what I watched was a slow leaning. Not of his body —
his body crossed that field like it crossed everything, sure-hooved and exact — but of *him,* the
him inside the make of him, tilting toward the cliff the way the slabs tilted, the way the grey poured.
By the time the pages were hillside-sized he was walking at a slight cant against nothing, leaning
upcurrent into a wind only he could feel, and the seam down his chest showed both lights working hard,
the dim one guttering with the effort, and his answers had gone short.

"Third."

"I am here."

"Say the thing. The whole thing. Out loud, for me."

He knew what I was asking. We had made it a drill on the slab two nights back — that if the pull got
into him I was to make him *talk,* because the part of him that talked to me was the part that was
still walking to the cliff to stop a thing, and not the part that wanted to lie down in the warm and
be known.

"I am Third," he said, and the avalanche of it steadied as he said it, the way a man's hands steady on
a rope he has remembered he is holding. "I am going to the spine to refuse him. I will not be made
willing. You will not believe the version of me that begs. There is a girl named Tasha walking on my
left who is going to be the whole of my will if my own gives out, and a guardsman on my right who has
chosen the people over the sense, and they are mine, and they are *here,* and that is more true than
anything that wall is going to offer me." A pause. The cant of him eased, a degree. "Thank you. Ask
me again when I go quiet."

"I will. I'll ask you the whole way."

"I know you will. It is why I let you come."

---

We saw him when the field gave us a long straight aisle between two leaning pages — a corridor of bare
rock running spireward, and at the far end of it, small with distance, a figure walking.

He walked the way the grey-faced traveller had described back at the inn, and worse for the seeing of
it: dead straight,
unhurried, putting one foot exactly in front of the other along a line only he could see, as though
the field's crawling did not apply to him, as though the slabs declined to rearrange in front of *that.*
And riding his back, even at that distance, even in the day, six small cold lights — bottled lightning
gone to dying embers in their jars — six of them where Third had two, and one of Third's already
failing. He did not look at us. He had not, I understood, looked at anything in a very long time that
was not the thing he was walking toward. He went building to building through a dead town and never
turned his head; he would cross a field that ate the unwary and never break his line; and he was going
to reach the spine at the same time we did, from the other arm of the open book, the two of us converging
on the hinge of the world like the covers of the book closing.

"There," Third said, unnecessarily. The eye had gone very bright and very steady. "The hand of the thing
under everything."

And then he went still — stiller than the moment asked for, a stillness with a different grain to it,
the same stillness that had come over him in the inn the day the grey-faced traveller spoke, as though
the shape of that far walker had reached across all the bare rock between us and pressed on something
in him he had no intention of letting me see. Whatever it was, he didn't say it. His head stayed level
on the distant figure, and his answer, when I asked with a look, was only the bright steady eye and
nothing behind it I was allowed to read.

I filed it. Of course I filed it; it is the curse Mathen used to tease me for, and I had no Mathen now
to tease me, so I filed it alone and said nothing, and we walked the last of the
aisle toward the place the whole shape was hung from, where the grey stood up out of the ground in a
pale drowning wall against the pale dead wall of stone, and where a thing was waiting that had a place
in it shaped exactly like Third, and a girl from an inn was going to have to be a rope.

The spine rose in front of us, and resolved, the way the cliff always resolved on approach — gradually,
grudgingly, a little more true with every step until you could not unsee how big it was. And at its foot,
in the last of the bare rock before the stone and the grey, there was room enough to stand and say the
things that had to be said before the other thing arrived to say his.

"Here," Third said. "We say what we must here. Quickly. He is close, and once he is closer there will
be no more saying, only doing."

I looked at Roan, who did not know yet all of what he had walked into.

It was, I realised, my turn to hold a door open for someone — and this time, the cruel kind, the kind
that only opens inward.

---

There is a particular cruelty in knowing exactly how a thing is going to land before you do it,
because Third had done it to me, on a slab two nights gone, and I had felt the floor of the world go
out from under me one fact at a time, and now I was going to do the same to Roan with the cold man a
thousand paces off and closing, and there was no kind way and no slow way and no time for either.

So I did it the way Tondi would have set a broken arm. Fast, and straight, and without asking him to
hold still first, because asking only makes them tense.

"Roan. Listen to me and don't say anything till I'm done, because we don't have the breath. You know
some of this. Here's the rest."

He looked at me. The spear had stopped its endless re-checking. That was how I knew he'd already
understood that the talking part was over and some other part had started.

"The thing under the world wants back in. It can't come on its own — it's too big, it's in pieces,
half of it's in the grey. It needs a door. Third is the door. Not because he's willing — because of
what he's *made* of, what woke him, the way he walks in both places at once. It can't force the door;
that's the one mercy in the whole rotten business. It can only get him to *open* it. From the inside.
On purpose." I kept my voice flat, the way you keep a hand flat for a frightened animal. "So it's
going to offer him a price. And the price is everyone he's ever lost. All of them. Whole, and warm,
and back, forever — Liwon, the swordsman, the woman who was sick, the woman in the snow. Mathen."

That one I watched land. I'm sorry to say I had to. I needed to know he'd heard it.

"Mathen's in there now, Roan. In the warm. The thing has him. And it will hold him up at the end like
the last coin in a purse and tell Third *all he has to do is open the door and he gets them all back,
Mathen included,* and Third is going to want to. Not might. *Going to.* I've seen what it does to him
just standing near it; I watched it nearly take him with my own voice the only thing holding the other
end. He is going to stand at that wall and want, with everything three hundred thousand spins has piled
up in him, to say yes. And if he says yes, the thing comes through, and there is no more world. Not our
lives — the *world.* Everyone's. Everywhere. That's the size of it. That's what we walked out here to
stand in front of."

I made myself stop. I let him have the breath I'd told him to save.

---

He did not bolt. I will love him for that as long as I have left to love anything. The size of it went
through him — I watched it go through him, the way a swallow of something too strong goes through you,
all the way down, and his face did several things and settled, at the end, on the one I'd hoped for
and not dared expect: not the slack of a man who's quit, but the stillness of a man who's decided to
be useful with whatever he's got left.

"What do I do," he said. Not *can we win.* Not *are you sure.* What do I do. Mathen, I thought. Mathen
all the way down.

"You keep me on him." I turned him by the shoulder so he was facing Third, who stood a little apart,
giving us this, watching the cold man come. "That's the whole of your job and it's the worst one and
I'm sorry it's yours. Whatever happens, I am the rope. As long as my voice is on Third and my hand is
on Third, there's a chance. The thing knows that now. So it's going to try to take me off him. Maybe
through the cold man. Maybe—" I made myself say it "—maybe through Third. If it turns him, if it gets
him moving, he is stronger than the three of us stacked up and he will come through me to get to that
wall and he will not know he's doing it."

"And I stop him." Flat. Already accepting the impossible arithmetic of it.

"You don't *stop* him. You can't. None of us can. You're not meant to." Third's words, from the drill;
I gave them to Roan now because they were the truest thing I had. "You're meant to cost it time. You
put your body between me and whatever's coming and you make it spend seconds getting through you.
Ten breaths. That's the job. Buy me ten breaths to finish refusing, and you'll have done more than any
hero in any of Mathen's stories, and most of them only had to die for a *town.*"

He took that in. Then, the boy in him surfacing one last time before whatever he was about to become:
"Will it hurt him? If I have to. Will I hurt Third."

And there it was — the thing that told me Mathen had built better than he knew. Roan, told he might
have to throw his body in front of a three-hundred-thousand-spin war-construct to buy a tavern girl
ten breaths to save the world, and the question he stopped on was whether *the construct* would be
hurt by it.

"No," I said, which was a lie, and a small one, and the kind Mathen would have approved of. "You won't
be able to hurt him. Just slow him. That's all."

---

Third came to us then, because the cold man was close enough now that there was no more *here* to be
said in, only *now.* He looked at Roan for a long moment with the bright unblinking coal of his eye,
and Roan looked back and did not flinch, which still, after everything, was rare.

"I brought you to this," Third said. "You were going to guard a gate and grow old. I am sorry. It is
the truest apology I have, and it is not enough, and I am giving it to you anyway because you should
have it before, and not after, in case there is no after for the giving."

"You didn't bring me," Roan said. "I came. There's a difference. Mathen taught me that one too."

Something moved in the make of Third — not the bellows, nothing so loud, just a settling, the sound a
house makes accepting a weight. "Yes," he said. "There is a difference. Thank you for the difference."

And then he turned to me, and his whole attention came round and landed, and I knew from the quality
of it that whatever the three of us had just been was finished, and that the next thing he had to say
he had to say to me alone, and that I was not going to like it, and that I was going to have to carry
it anyway, because that, in the end, was the difference I had chosen too.

---

The first thing he did was set down the Glaive.

He did it carefully, the way you set down something that has a history of being mishandled. He leaned
it against the foot of the spine, upright, the wide wooden blade he had carried since the day he was
made — since before he was anyone, when he was only the thing the Titan had wrapped a purpose around
and called finished — and he stepped back from it and left it standing there against the pale stone
like a tool returned to a shed. He kept the falcatas. The two curved blades he'd spent thirty spins of
the Scar learning to dance with, the weapons that were his choosing and not his making, those he
checked at his hips with the small unconscious touch of a man making sure of his hands.

I understood it without being told, which is a thing that happens between people who have walked far
enough together. He was not going to meet the end of the world as the instrument someone had built. He
was going to meet it as the thing that instrument had decided, against every line of its making, to
become. The Glaive could stand there and watch. It had never been his anyway.

"Tasha," he said. "The last of it. The part I have not told you."

"I know. I saw it on your face. Get it said; he's nearly here."

He was. Down the converging aisle the cold man had grown from a far mark to a near one, close enough
now that I could see the lights riding his back had a faint and constant tremor, like something
breathing. We had a hundred breaths, perhaps. Perhaps fewer.

---

"You are the rope," Third said. "That is true and it remains true and it is the first thing. As long as
you hold me, I have a chance to hold myself. But you are going to need to know the second thing, and the
second thing is worse, and I have not let myself say it until now because saying it makes it real and I
wanted, selfishly, a few more spins of it not being real." The eye was very steady on me. "If the rope
fails — if it gets so far into me that I begin to open, and you cannot pull me back — then holding me is
no longer the job. Ending me is the job."

I had known, somewhere, that this was coming. Knowing did not help. It never does; he'd taught me that
himself, coming back out of the grey with Mathen's face still on him.

"No," I said.

"Tasha—"

"I heard you. I said no. Tell me the rest of it anyway, because I'm going to need it, but you should know
my answer first so you understand what it costs me to change it."

Something in the make of him that was not quite the bellows let go, a small mechanical sigh of a thing
that had been braced for a fight and been handed honesty instead. "Thank you," he said. "That is exactly
why it is you."

And then he reached around to his back, with that wrong boneless ease that still put a flutter in my
chest after all this time, and he brought out the Bolter.

Dorn's Bolter. I knew it the moment I saw it because he'd shown it to me once, early, at a fire, and
told me whose it had been and how he had died — a tendril through the chest from below, before Third
had finished standing up — and told me he had never once loaded it, having carried it a very long
time and never fired it, because it was not a weapon to him. It was a person. It was the last weight of a
man he had failed to save by the width of a single breath.

He held it a moment longer before he gave it over — and then, with the same wrong boneless economy, he
brought a single magefire shot up from somewhere on him, a little vial of caged lightning, and he
loaded it. I watched him do it and felt the floor go out from under me a second time, because at the
fire, the night he told us about Dorn, he had said it plain to the whole table: *I do not carry it to
use.* He had carried it unloaded across more road than I could imagine, kept it the way you keep a
grave tidy, and now, here, he was thumbing a shot into it with his own two-fingered hands.

"I have never loaded it," he said, as if he'd heard the thought. "Not once. Loading it would have made
it a weapon again, and it stopped being a weapon the day it became Dorn. I am loading it now, because
this is the one thing left in the world he would forgive me for aiming it at." Then he put it in my
hands. It was heavier than it looked, and warm where his grip had been.

"There is a seam down the centre of my chest. You have seen the lights through it. Those lights are the
whole of what keeps the door a door — they are what the wall wants, the same as the cold man carries,
the same make, the same dying glow. If you put one of Dorn's shots into that seam, into the lights, the
door stops being a door. Permanently. There is no repairing it; I have repaired everything I am, a
hundred times over, and I could not repair that. It would be the one wound I have ever taken that I
could not stand up from."

"You're asking me to kill you."

"I am asking you to unmake a door. That the door is also me is the cruelty of it, and I will not pretend
it is anything else, because you have earned better than pretending."

---

"How do I know," I said. My voice was doing something I did not give it permission to do. "How do I know
when. You already told me you'll beg. You already made me promise not to believe the begging. Now you're
telling me to — and the begging version is going to beg me not to, isn't it. It's going to look at me
with your eye and beg me to put it down. How do I tell the difference. How do I know when there's no more
you to save and it's only the door wearing you."

"Because the begging means I am still there." He said it gently, the way you'd explain a hard thing to a
child you loved. "While I beg, hold me. The begging is the part of me that still wants you to win
fighting the part of me that wants to lose. As long as I am begging you, in either direction, I am still
a fight you can win, and you must not use the Bolter, no matter how it sounds, no matter what I promise
or threaten — you hold." A pause. The lights in his chest guttered, the dim one nearly out. "You will
know it is time when I go quiet. Not the begging-quiet. The other one. When I stop arguing with you,
when I stop arguing with myself, when I go still and turn to the wall and reach for it and have nothing
left to say to you at all — that silence is not me resting, Tasha. That silence is the door swinging
open. That is the only moment. You will have a breath, perhaps two, between the silence and the world
ending, and in that breath you put Dorn's shot through the seam and you do not hesitate, because the
hesitation is the exact size of the gap the thing needs."

I looked down at the Bolter in my hands. The whole stupid weight of it. The dead man in it. The friend
who'd carried it unloaded and unfired across more road than I could hold in my head, because using it
was unthinkable, handing it to me so that I could fire it into him.

"Why me," I said. "Roan would— no. Don't say it kindly. Say it true. Why is this mine."

---

"Because Roan would do it too soon," Third said. "He loves me as a boy loves the thing he's decided to
be brave for, and that love would flinch at the begging and end me to spare me the struggle, and the
struggle is the whole of the chance. And the cold man would do it too late, or not at all, because to
him I am a mechanism and he would want to see if the mechanism could be made to work after all." The eye
held mine. "You are the only one who will do it at exactly the right moment, which is to say the last
possible one, which is to say only when there is truly nothing left — because you do not want to do it.
Because you will hate every breath between the silence and the shot. I am not choosing the person most
able to kill me, Tasha. I am choosing the person least willing. That unwillingness is the most precise
instrument in the world. It is the only thing I trust to wait long enough, and still not wait too long."

He looked, for a moment, at the cold man coming, and then back at me, and he said the thing under the
thing, the thing he had been walking toward saying since the slab field, since the geysers, since the
inn:

"I am afraid I am going to want it. The opening. The price. I have been alone inside this make of mine
for three hundred thousand spins and it is going to offer me everyone, all at once, warm, and I am
afraid — I, who am not built to be afraid of much — that I am going to want it more than I have ever
wanted anything, more than I will want the world. So I am giving you the thing that means my wanting it
will not be enough. I am putting the size of my own longing into your hands and I am asking you to be
bigger than it. That is the whole of what I have ever asked anyone. I am sorry it is so large."

I did not say *I can't.* I had already used my no, and he had thanked me for it, and we both understood
it had been the last one I was allowed.

I said, "Then you'd better beg loud, you old fool. Because I am going to need to hear you in there right
up until the last breath, and if you go quiet on me one second before you have to, I will never forgive
you, in this world or whatever's left of it."

"I will be as loud as I am able," Third said. "It is a promise. It may be the last one I am able to keep
as myself."

And behind him, at the foot of the spine, where the pale grey stood up out of the ground against the
pale dead stone, the cold man stopped walking.

We were out of breaths. He was here.

---

Up close the cold of him was a real thing, a weather. It came off him the way heat comes off a stove,
except backwards, except wrong — a steady draught of cave-air that found the sweat on you and made you
regret having a body at all. Roan, three paces to my left with his spear low, flinched at it before he
caught himself. I didn't catch myself in time and shivered like a wet dog. Third did not feel it, or
did not show it, which by then was the same thing.

I had braced, I think, for a monster. For something that looked like what it did. But the cold man just
looked old — old past any use the word usually gets put to, old the way the cliff was old, a person who
had been a person so long ago that the being-a-person had worn off him like paint off a fence. His face
was all still there, eyes and mouth and the rest, and there was nothing behind any of it but patience.
That was the worst part. Not menace. Patience. The patience of a thing that has already won and is only
waiting for the rest of you to notice. And riding his stooped back, close enough now that I could see
them clear, the six lights: bottled lightning long dead in their jars, the same blue-white as the two
in Third's chest, except his were guttering and these six only sat there, steady and spent and somehow
worse for it, like eyes that have stopped expecting anything.

He stopped a few paces from Third, and the two of them stood there, and I understood — watching the way
they held themselves, both of them somehow a half-step out of the world, both leaning on a wind no one
else could feel — that I was looking at two of a kind. Not the same. But kin, the way a key and a lock
are kin: made to fit, made by the same hand for the same wound.

---

"You are awake," the cold man said.

His voice was nothing like Third's. Where Third was an avalanche, low and felt in the bones, this was
the thin dry whisper of air pulled through a long-dead place, a sound with no warmth in it to lose. He
said it the way you'd say a thing you had wanted for so long that getting it had stopped being a joy and
become only a fact. There was no triumph in it. There was something far worse — something almost like
tenderness.

"I felt them make you," he went on, when Third said nothing. "All of you. A long time ago, when the
hand that made you struck you out — I felt each blow of it, here—" and he did not point to his chest,
he pointed down, at the rock, at the cliff, at whatever was under it "—because I am fixed to the thing
they struck you against. I have felt every one of your kind woken since, the way you feel a door open
somewhere in a house you cannot leave. Most never woke. The ones that did were too far, or too broken,
or died too soon." The patient eyes rested on Third. "I have waited a very long time for one of you to
wake near enough, and whole enough, and last long enough to walk to me. And here you are. Do you know
what you are?"

"I am Third," said Third. The avalanche held. "I know what I have made myself."

"That is not the question I asked." Not unkind. A teacher correcting a beloved pupil. "I asked if you
know what you *are.* You do not. You have wondered — I can see that you have wondered, the whole long
walk of your life. Why you can step beside the world when no other made thing can. Why the grey lets
you pass and pulls at you both at once. Why, of all the things the Titans struck out, *you* woke." The
cold deepened, or seemed to. "I will tell you, because I want you to understand what you are giving,
when you give it. There is no joy in taking a thing from someone who does not know its worth."

I had the Bolter under my cloak, loaded, Dorn's whole grave of a weight against my ribs, and my hand
on Third's forearm, and my voice already low in my throat saying the small steadying nothings I'd
sworn to say — *here, I'm here, you're Third, I've got you* — and I felt Third lean, the smallest
degree, toward the cold man's voice. Not toward the lure. There was no lure yet. Toward the *answer.*
After three hundred thousand spins, somebody was finally going to tell him why.

---

"Under us," the cold man said, "is the Anvil. It is not a stone. It is the thing the Titans set
everything else against when they were striking the world into shape — the surface they beat creation
on. It is in the grey, and it is larger than this cliff, larger than the country, and it cannot be
moved; I have had an age to try, and I am fixed to it, and one does not move the thing one is nailed
to." For the first time something that was not patience crossed him — a very old, very tired
frustration, worn down to almost nothing. "Everything you have felt these last spins, the pulling, the
grey running one way like water down a drain — that is the Anvil. That is me, drawing the world toward
the place the world began, because that is the only thing a fixed thing can do. Pull. I have pulled for
longer than your kind has had names." The eyes came up. "But pulling is not opening. And I need it
*opened.*"

"Then open it," Third said. "You walk in the grey. I have watched you do it. Step across, here, where
it is thin, and reach your Anvil, and leave us be."

"I can go to it." A sound came out of him that might once, very long ago, have been a laugh. "I have
gone to it more times than there are days in your life. But I am a passenger, made thing. I cross the
wall; I do not breach it. I can carry one body sideways into the grey — myself, a thing I collect, no
more — the way a man swims a river. The river is not opened by his crossing; it closes behind him as
he goes. What is wanted is not a swimmer. What is wanted is a *door* — the wall held wide, and held,
long enough for what is on the far side to come *through,* into here, into Anka, in its own vastness
and not one body at a time." The patient eyes, the terrible tenderness. "A wall is not opened from
without. It is opened from within, by the part of it that is alive. You are the only living part of
the wall, made thing. They forged you against the Anvil; the making wove you into the seam of the two
worlds; and then the last of them, dying, woke you with its own essence — the very essence that now
wants through. You are not a soldier who happens to walk in the grey. You are the grey, and you are
Anka, in one skin, with one will. You are the only seam in all the world that can be asked to part.
That is what you are. That is what I have waited for. I did not make you, and I do not know your name,
and neither of those things has ever mattered. I only ever needed one of you to be *alive,* and *here,*
and able to say yes."

---

The silence after that had weight, like the air before a stony.

I felt the whole of it land on Third — felt it through my hand on his arm, the way you feel a tree take
an axe-blow through the ground. Everything he had ever wondered, answered, in a dead man's whisper, at
the end of the world: not a fluke, not a mercy, not a question the Titans forgot to answer. A purpose.
He had spent his entire long life believing he had *escaped* his making — refused the glaive, refused
the killing, made himself into something his makers never intended — and here was a thing older than
his makers telling him, gently, that the escape itself had been the design. That he had been woken to
be a door, and everything he'd become since, every kindness, every refusal, every companion buried,
was just the door learning to want things, so that one day it could be offered the one thing that would
make it open.

I want to tell you he answered like a hero. He didn't. He went very still, and the lights in his chest
stuttered, and for a moment — one moment, the worst of my life to that point — I thought I had already
lost him, that the silence I'd been warned to watch for had come this early, before the cold man had
even named a price.

"Third," I said. Out loud. Not a steadying nothing. His name, hard, the way you'd throw a rope. "You're
Third. You made that name yourself; nobody struck it into you. Say it."

A long breath of bellows, ragged, the first he'd spent in days.

"I am Third," he said. To me. Only to me. "I made myself. Whatever I was struck for." The eye came back
to the cold man, and the avalanche came back into the voice, and I could have wept. "You have told me
what I am. You are mistaken about one thing only. A door can be a door, and still choose to stay shut.
That is also a thing a living seam can do."

The cold man did not look troubled. He looked, if anything, fonder.

"Yes," he said. "It can. That is why I have not come to threaten you, or to break you, neither of which
would serve. I have come to *offer* you something. You will want to hear it. Everyone, in the end, wants
to hear it." He tilted his worn face, and the six dead lights shivered on his back, and the grey stood
up off the ground behind him like a held breath. "Shall I tell you what is waiting, on the other side
of the wall you are?"

And Scar help me, I felt Third want to say yes.

---

He did not describe it. I think that is what I had braced for — a speech, a temptation laid out in
words I could hear and hate and argue with. Instead the cold man simply opened his hand, palm up,
toward the wall of standing grey behind him, and the grey thinned where he gestured the way frost
thins on a window when you put your thumb to it, and Third made a sound.

I have heard Third make a great many sounds. The bellows-wheeze, the avalanche, the dry not-laugh.
I had never heard him make this one. It was small, and it came from somewhere the bellows didn't
reach, and it was the sound a person makes when they see something they had decided, for the sake of
going on living, to stop letting themselves want.

I couldn't see it. That is the thing I have to keep telling people, the thing that makes them look at
me like I failed him: I could not see into the grey. Whatever opened in that thinning, whatever stood
in it warm and reaching, it was shown to Third and not to me. I fought the whole rest of it blind. I
had a hand on his arm and his name in my mouth and a dead man's Bolter against my ribs, and I was
holding him against a thing I could not see, by the one thread I had, which was that he had asked me
to.

"There," said the cold man, soft as falling dust. "You know her."

---

"Liwon," Third said. Not to me. Not to the cold man. To the thinning in the air, to whatever stood in
it. The avalanche had gone all to gravel. "Liwon."

"As she was," the cold man said. "Before the sundering took her. Whole. Small, and afraid of
the dark, and certain, the way the very young are certain, that you would never let anything happen to
her. She has been kept exactly so. The grey does not let go of what it holds; it only waits to be asked
for it back." A pause, weightless, terrible. "You remember what was said to you, the night she died.
A seed must die, and be buried, before it can blossom into something beautiful. You thought that a
cruelty. It was a promise. This is the blossoming. The seed was planted three hundred thousand spins
ago and I am offering you the flower. All of it. Her, and the swordsman with the squat Bolter, and the
woman the illness took, and the one who taught you to want quiet, and — most lately, still warm, barely
cold — the one who taught you to make your losses into stories. Mathen. All of them. Kept. Returned.
The moment you open."

I felt Third lean. Not his body. The thing inside the body, the way it had leaned crossing the field,
except the field had been a breeze against this. He leaned the way a drowning man leans toward air.

"You can't," I said — to him, low, the rope. "You're Third. You're here. I've got you. Whatever it's
showing you, I've got you."

"It is Liwon, Tasha." He said it like a man explaining why he has to let go of the ledge. "She is
holding up her arms. She does that — she did that — when she was frightened and wanted to be carried.
She is doing it now. She is asking me to carry her. I have not been asked to carry anything in a very
long time."

---

I want to be honest about how close it was, because the people who weren't there tell it like it was
never really in doubt, like Third was always going to refuse, and that is an insult to what it cost
him. It was in doubt. It was in doubt for longer than I will ever admit to anyone but this page. He
stood at the foot of the spine with his dead daughter holding up her arms in a place I couldn't see,
and every part of three hundred thousand spins of being alone reached back toward her, and the lights
in his chest guttered low and lower, and his hand under mine had gone slack, and I talked, and talked,
and felt the words going into him and falling through, like dropping stones down a well too deep to
hear them land.

"Ask me what you are paying," Third said suddenly, to the cold man, and I heard him reaching — reaching
for something, a handhold, a counter. "You always tell them the prize. You are too patient to tell them
the cost. What is the cost."

"The world," the cold man said, without the smallest hesitation, and that was somehow worse than a lie
would have been. "You know this. Everyone, everywhere, all that lives. I will not insult you by
softening it. That is the price, and I am telling you plainly, because you are the first of your kind
worth telling plainly, and because—" the worn face did something that might once have been gentleness
"—because it does not matter. You will reach for her anyway. They always reach. I have watched a great
many smaller souls than yours weigh the whole living world against one held face, and the world has
never once been heavy enough. It is not a flaw in you. It is the shape of love. I am not your enemy,
made thing. I am only the one who understands what you are for."

"Tell me your name," Third said — and I understood, dimly, that he was doing something, that asking was
a way of staying, of keeping his feet, of making the thing in front of him a someone he could refuse
rather than a force he could only fall into.

The cold man considered. "Sougad," he said. "It is the one thing I will give you for nothing. Everything
else in the world has a price now; I have seen to it. But a name is cheap, and you have been honest with
me, and I find—" the dust-dry voice paused over the unfamiliar shape of it "—I find I would like one
person, before the end, to have known me well enough to use it. Sougad. There. Now refuse a thing with
a name, made thing, if you can. It is harder. I know. I have a name too, and it is why I am still
fixed to that Anvil and have not let myself fall into the grey and end, all these ages. A name is a
small hook, but it holds."

---

"Liwon was not a seed."

Third said it slowly, and I felt the first thing in a long time that was not slack — a tension coming
back into the arm under my hand, a man finding, in the dark, the edge of something solid.

"You say it as if it were a kindness. The seed, the blossom. But she was not a seed. A seed is a thing
that is for something else, that has not happened yet, that is only worth what it becomes. Liwon was
not for anything. She was small, and afraid of the dark, and entirely herself, already,
finished, real. She was not waiting to become beautiful. She was beautiful, and then she was killed,
and the killing was real, and the losing was real, and they were the last true things left of her." The
gravel was firming back toward stone. "If I take her back as your flower, I agree with you that she was
only ever a seed. I let you make her into raw material for a thing that comes later. I unmake the one
true thing I have — that she lived, and was hers, and not yours, and not the world's, and not a price.
A woman in the snow taught me that, once. I did not understand it until just now, with my daughter
holding up her arms."

And for a moment — one bright unbearable moment — I thought that was it. I thought he'd done it. I felt
him straighten, felt the armor hold, felt the rope go slack in my hand because he didn't need it,
he was standing on his own—

And then Sougad, gently, did the cruelest thing in this whole cruel book. He did not argue.

"I know," he said. "You are entirely right. She was herself, and the loss was true, and taking her back
dishonours all of it. I am not going to pretend otherwise; you have earned better." The grey thinned a
little further. "Knowing that changes nothing. You are going to reach for her anyway, because being
right has never once stopped anyone from being lonely. I told you. It does not matter if she is real.
It does not matter if it is true. I do not need you to believe me. I need you to *want* her. And you
do. You are right, and you want her, and the wanting is the door, and the door is opening, and I am
sorry — I find, to my surprise, that I am actually sorry — but I have waited so very long."

And Third stopped talking.

Not the begging-quiet. I knew the difference; he'd carved the difference into me two nights gone. This
was the other one. This was the going-still. His hand slid out from under mine. His head turned, slow,
toward the wall of grey, toward the small reaching shape only he could see, and he took one step toward
it, and he had nothing left to say to me at all.

I had a breath. Perhaps two.

And my hand, Scar forgive me, would not go to the Bolter. It went tighter around his empty arm instead,
because he was my friend and he was walking away from me into the arms of his dead and the thing I had
sworn to do in this exact moment was the one thing my whole body refused to do.

"ROAN," I screamed. It was not the plan. It was the opposite of the plan. It was the only thing in me.

---

Roan had been three paces back and to my right, where I'd put him, where I'd told him to wait for the
worst and not before. He couldn't see into the grey any more than I could; he didn't know about the
seed or the blossom or the door. All he had was what I'd given him at the foot of the spine — keep her
on him, and when it goes wrong, cost it time. He'd been standing in the cold with his spear and his
terror the whole while the cold man talked, waiting for a wrongness he could recognise.

My scream was the wrongness. He didn't ask what for. That, in the end, was the whole of Roan: he didn't
ask what for.

He came past me in a lunge that knocked my shoulder half around and did the one thing none of us had
planned because none of us had been able to say it aloud — he threw himself not at the cold man, not at
the grey, but at *Third.* At his friend. At the wooden back already turning toward the wall, the hooves
already lifting for that first long stride into the place where the dead were holding up their arms.
Roan got one arm across the seam of Third's chest, over the guttering lights, and his other hand to his
own wrist, and locked, and set his heels, and hauled — a boy trying to arm-wrestle a landslide back up
its hill.

It did nothing, in the matter of strength. Third took the step anyway, dragging the both of them, Roan's
heels furrowing the bare rock toward the standing grey.

So the cold man did the other thing. The thing that told me, finally and completely, what he was.

---

Sougad sighed. Actually sighed — the dust-dry ghost of a man put to a small tedious chore — and he said
one word.

I did not know it. It was Titanspeech, the old inversion-tongue, the one the greybeards say carries no
vowels of its own and borrows whatever ones you lend it, and though I could not read it I felt the shape
of it go past me like a draught under a door: a word built backwards, *take* turned cleanly inside out,
the old, old word for casting a thing forever out of your keeping. He did not point. He did not so much
as lift a hand. He said the word, and the space between Roan and Third simply declined to go on holding
him — and Roan was torn off Third's back as though the world had tilted under him alone, flung up and
back and down, spear and limbs clattering across the bare stone, and he came to rest a dozen paces off
in a heap that did not move.

He did not get up. I could not let myself look long enough to learn whether he *would* — whether the
chest rose, whether there was breath left in him at all — because I had a door to hold and only the one
pair of eyes to hold it with. I have had to live, since, with not knowing in that moment. It is its own
small permanent wound.

And under the horror, cold and clear, I understood the thing that mattered: *he could have done that at
any time.* To any of us. To Third. One word, no hand raised, and the strongest thing on that rock thrown
down like a swatted fly. He had not done it to Third. He would never do it to Third — could not, dared
not — because a door flung and broken is no door at all, because the single lesson of all his ages
fixed to that Anvil was that the seam cannot be forced, only *asked.* He would unmake a guardsman with a
breath and not so much as raise his voice at the made thing he needed. Everything true about him was
right there, in the gap between what he was willing to break and what he was not.

---

And Third saw it.

That is the whole of it. That is what Sougad, in all his patience, in all his ages, had never once had
to reckon with — because he had only ever watched souls reach toward what they had lost, and had never
watched one get *held onto* by what it still had, and so he did not understand, even now, what he had
just done.

One hoof in the grey. His dead daughter an arm's reach into a place I would never see, holding up her
arms. Every part of three hundred thousand spins reaching for the warm. And Third turned his head — the
smallest degree, a thing that should not have been possible against that pull — and he *saw the boy.*
Saw Roan thrown down and unmoving on the cold rock for the single crime of having tried to keep him.

The dead were asking to be carried. And here, flung bleeding on the stone, was the price of the carrying
— counted out in front of him, in a living boy, by the very hand that wanted him to say yes. Sougad, who
needed Third willing, had reached for the one tool he understood and shown Third precisely what his yes
was worth.

"That's it," I said, and I do not know where the steadiness came from; I think it came from Roan. "That's
your yes, Third. That. On the rock. He came because you tend the people — Mathen's whole law, the only
thing he had to leave us — you tend the people, and Roan tended *you,* and that is what your yes does to
the ones who tend you. Walk into that wall. Go on. And he doesn't let go of you in his heart even now,
so the grey takes him too, and you make another one. You make another Liwon. You make *him* one of the
little arms reaching, forever. Is that the carrying you missed? Is it?"

---

And Third — one hoof lifted, the grey an arm's reach, the dead reaching, the boy bleeding — stopped.

He did not turn around. He was not back; the silence still held the most of him, and I could see the
wall's pull on him like a current on a wading man. But the lifted hoof did not complete its step into
the grey. It came down. On the lived side. On the cold honest rock.

And into the stolen breath where that step should have finished, Third said the first word he had spoken
since the going-still closed over him — not to Sougad, not to the dead, not even to me, but low, over his
shoulder, toward the heap of a boy a dozen paces off on the stone:

"...Roan."

It was not the refusal. The refusal was still to come, and it would cost him the worst thing he ever
paid for anything, and I would have to watch him pay it. But it was the first crack of light under a
door I had given up for shut, and I put my shoulder to that crack and shoved with everything I had left
in me.

---

I have been asked to describe what happened next more times than I have been asked anything else in my
life, and I have never once done it well, because the truest answer is the one nobody wants: I don't
know. I couldn't see it. The most important thing that has ever happened in the world happened a
half-step sideways from me, in a place my eyes were not made to go, and all I had was the back of a
wooden man and the things he said out loud and the slow terrible arithmetic of watching someone decide.

So this is not what happened. This is what it looked like, from the rock, from the wrong side of the
wall, holding a rope I couldn't see the far end of.

It looked like a man saying goodbye to his daughter.

---

"Liwon," he said. The hoof was down. The other had not lifted again. But he had not turned from the
grey either, and his voice when it came was not the avalanche and not the gravel — it was small, and
careful, the voice you'd use on a child you were not going to be able to keep from crying. "Liwon. Look
at me. No — keep your arms down a moment. I know. I know you're frightened. I know it's dark in there.
Listen to me."

I could not hear an answer. There was no answer to hear; she was in the place I couldn't go. But I
watched Third listen to one, watched his head tilt the exact degree it must have tilted over a sling
three hundred thousand spins ago, and I have never in my life felt so much like an intruder on something
holy and so unable to leave.

"I cannot come and get you," Third said. "I know I said I would always come. I know. I have thought
about that promise every day you have been gone, and I am breaking it now, on purpose, with my eyes
open, and I need you to hear me say that it is a promise I am choosing to break and not one I forgot."
A pause. The lights in his chest had stopped guttering; they had gone very steady, very low, the way a
flame goes steady right before. "Because the you in there is not the last true thing about you. The
last true thing about you is that you were *mine,* and you were *real,* and you were taken, and it was
wrong, and it has stayed wrong every day since, and that wrongness is the only honest grave you have.
If I take the warm copy this thing is holding up, I have to agree with it that you were only ever a seed
for it to grow me a flower from. And you were not a seed, Liwon. You were a girl. You were my girl. And
I would rather keep the empty place where you were, true, forever, than fill it with something that
calls your whole short real life a *down payment.*"

---

I felt it through the rope before I understood it. The arm under my hand — slack since the going-still
took him — came back. Not pulling toward the wall. Not pulling anywhere. Just *his* again, weight in it,
a man inhabiting his own limb.

"You taught me this," Third said, and I knew, this time, it was not to Liwon, and not to me; it was to a
woman who had died old in a bed a long way from here, who had lost everything and gone on anyway. "You
said going on was the only way to call the loss what it was. I did not understand you. I thought you
meant *endure.* You meant *refuse.* You meant that to take the bargain is to let them tell you it was
never really a loss at all — and that the loss is the last of the love you get to keep. I am keeping it.
I am keeping all of it. Thank you. I am sorry it took me until the end of the world."

And then, to the grey, to the small reaching shape in it, the worst sentence I have ever heard a person
say, said gently, said like tucking someone in:

"Goodbye, Liwon. I love you. Stay where it's warm. I am not coming."

---

I will tell you what I saw, and you can believe what you like about what it means.

I saw the standing wall of grey go *out* — not torn, not slammed, nothing violent. It went out the way
a held breath goes out. It had stood up off the ground like something about to speak and now it simply
sat back down into the rock and the troughs and the ordinary low places, thinning, paling, becoming
again the faint wrong fog I'd watched all the way across the field, and then less than that, and then
gone, and the bare honest rock of the world lay there under the Scar's grey light with nothing standing
up out of it at all.

And I felt Third lose her.

I had braced for triumph. There wasn't any. What came through the rope, through the arm under my hand,
was the thing I had no name for until I'd felt it — the second losing. He had buried her once, a child,
three hundred thousand spins ago, with no choice in it. Now he had been handed her back, whole, warm,
asking to be carried, and he had given her up *again,* with every choice in the world, and chosen the
empty place on purpose. The first time the world took her from him. This time he handed her back to the
dark with his own two-fingered hands, so that the world could keep being a world.

He made a sound then. Once. I won't try to spell it. It was not the bellows and it was not a word. It
was the sound the make of him made around a grief it had chosen, and I hope I never hear anything like
it again, and I will hear it for the rest of my life.

---

Sougad did not rage.

I think, if he had raged, I could have hated him cleanly, and it would have been easier. Instead the
worn old face simply... settled, the way water settles when you stop disturbing it, back into the
patience it had worn coming across the field — except the patience had nothing left to be patient
*toward,* now, and so it was just a face, very old, looking at a wall that had closed and a door that
had chosen, with the six dead lights guttering on his stooped back.

"You understand what you have done," he said. Not a threat. Barely a question. "I cannot be killed. Not
by you, not by anything that lives on this side. I will go back to the Anvil, because there is nowhere
else a thing like me can go, and I will be fixed to it as I have been fixed to it since before your
makers had names, and I will pull, because that is the only thing a fixed thing can do. And one day,
made thing — long after the girl beside you is dust, long after the boy on the rock is a name no one
says — another door will wake. They always do. I am very patient. I have just been taught how patient
I am going to have to be."

"Yes," Third said. He had turned, finally, away from the place she'd been. The single light of his eye
found the cold man, and there was no triumph in it either, only a kind of terrible kinship. "You will
wait. And I will spend whatever I have left making certain the next one who wakes is found by someone
who loves them, before it is found by you. That is the whole of the war, I think. That is the only war
there ever was. You have patience. I will leave behind people."

Sougad looked at him a long moment. Then he stepped sideways, the half-step out of the world that I had
watched Third make a dozen times, and the cold went with him, all at once, like a door closing on
winter — and the rock where he'd stood was only rock, and the Scar wheeled its slow light over a world
that had, against everything, not ended.

I became aware that I was still holding Third's arm. That my other hand had, at some point I never felt,
finally found the Bolter under my cloak, and was gripping it white-knuckled, and had never drawn it.

And a dozen paces off, on the cold stone, the heap that was Roan made a small, wet, outraged groan — the
single most beautiful sound in the entire language of the living — and tried, and failed, and tried
again, to sit up.

---

The world did not feel saved. I want to put that down plainly, because the stories will say we saved
it and the word will do what words do, it will round everything off smooth, and there was nothing smooth
about that rock in the grey light with the cold man gone and the three of us still on it, breathing,
which is a different and much smaller thing than *saved.* The world felt the way a room feels after
someone has been very ill in it and the fever has finally broken: not triumphant. Just quiet, and
wrung out, and aware of how close the other thing had come.

I went to Roan first, because Roan was the one making noise, and noise meant alive.

He'd got himself half-propped on one elbow by the time I reached him, and he was swearing — a low
steady creative stream of it, the kind you only manage when the hurt is real but not the worst kind,
the kind that's furious to still be here rather than grateful — and when he saw my face he stopped and
tried to rearrange his own into something braver, and failed, and I loved him for the failing.

"Don't move," I said, getting my hands on him. "Let me see."

"It threw me," he said, as if I hadn't watched. "It didn't even — Tasha, it didn't even *touch* me. It
said a *word.*" His outrage at this was total and somehow the most healing thing in the world. A man
does not waste breath being indignant about the rules of his injury unless he intends to live to
complain about it properly later.

His leg was wrong below the knee — not bone-through-skin wrong, but wrong, already swelling, the kind
of wrong that would make the walk out a long ugly business. Two of his ribs grated under my hands in a
way that made us both hiss. There was blood drying at the corner of his mouth and a deeper greyish cast
to his skin that I didn't like and didn't have a name for, the toll of having been flung so near a wall
he was never meant to touch. But his eyes tracked. His swearing scanned. His hand, when I gripped it,
gripped back hard enough to grind my knuckles.

"You're going to be unbearable about this," I told him, which was the truest comfort I had. "You threw
yourself at a Forged and got cast off by a magic word and lived. You'll dine out on it for the rest of
your life."

"Mathen," Roan said, and his voice cracked clean in half on it, "would've gotten a *tenday* of drinks."

"A season," I said. "Easy."

And we both looked away from each other for a moment, because that was as close as either of us could
go to him just then, and it was enough, and it was the first time we'd been able to go even that close
since the Grask.

---

Third had not moved.

He stood where he'd turned to face Sougad, a little apart, and he was looking at the place the grey had
been — the ordinary rock, the ordinary low troughs, nothing standing up out of any of it — and I knew,
the way I'd learned to know him, that he was not looking at the rock. He was looking at where she had
been. At the empty place he had chosen to keep empty. The lights in his chest had gone back to their
small steady burning, the dim one no worse than before, no better; the make of him was intact; he had
taken no wound that he could repair or that anyone could see.

He had taken the other kind.

I left Roan propped and bound as well as I could manage and went and stood beside Third, not touching
him, just there, the way he'd stood beside me a hundred times in the dark and let his being-there be the
whole of what he offered. For a long while he said nothing. The Scar wheeled. Somewhere far off across
the field a slab went over with a crash, because the place was still the place; the world had gone back
to being only ordinarily terrible.

"I had her," he said at last. Not to me. To the empty rock. "For the length of a goodbye, I had her
back, exactly as she was, and she was holding up her arms. And I gave her to the dark with my own hands
so that he—" the eye moved, a fraction, toward Roan "—could keep being a boy who complains about magic
words. I want you to understand that I do not feel I did a great thing, Tasha. I feel I did a murder. I
murdered the chance to have my daughter, and I did it on purpose, and the only thing I can say for myself
is that I would do it again, which is not the same as being glad, and I am so tired of people thinking
those are the same."

"I don't think they're the same," I said.

"No," he said. "You don't. It is why I could bear for you to be the one who saw it." A pause. "Thank
you for not telling me it was worth it. Everyone is going to tell me it was worth it."

"It wasn't worth it," I said. "It was just necessary. Those aren't the same either."

The single light of his eye came round to me, and held, and something in the make of him eased a
fraction — not healed, nothing was healed, but eased, the way a weight shifts to a part of you better
made to carry it.

"No," he said softly. "They are not. You have gotten very good at the true things, Tasha. I am sorry it
was my company that taught you."

---

He saw the Bolter then. It was still in my hand. I had not been able to make myself put it away, and I
realised, looking down at it, that my fingers had cramped around it, that I had been holding it that
hard for that long, and that the safety of it — the loaded, ready, never-drawn weight of it — was the
only thing my body had let itself do with the thing it could not do.

"You did not use it," Third said.

"No."

"When I went quiet. When I went to the wall and reached. That was the moment. That was the only moment,
the one I drilled into you, and you had it, and you did not use it." He said it without accusation, the
way you'd note the weather. "You could not. Could you."

"No." It came out of me in a rush, the confession I'd been dreading, the failure under the relief. "I
couldn't. I had it aimed in my head a hundred times those two nights and when it was real my hand just —
it wouldn't. It went tighter on your arm instead. I broke the plan, Third. The one thing you built the
whole of it on, the failsafe, the precise instrument, *only when there's truly nothing left* — and when
there was truly nothing left I screamed for a hurt boy instead of doing the one thing you asked me for.
If Roan hadn't— if Sougad hadn't spent him in front of you— the world ends, because I couldn't open my
hand."

Third was quiet a moment. Then he did a thing I had never seen him do and never saw him do again: he
reached out, slow, with that wrong boneless ease, and he closed his own wooden two-fingered hand around
mine, around the Bolter, gently, over the cramp, and held it.

"I chose you," he said, "because you were the one least willing to do it. I told you that. I thought I
was choosing the most precise blade — the hand that would wait until the last possible breath because it
hated the cutting. I was wrong about what I was choosing. I was not choosing a blade at all. I built a
failsafe because I needed to believe there was a bottom under me before I could step out over the drop —
and the failsafe I actually built, without knowing it, was a person whose hand would not open on me no
matter what. Who would scream for help instead of for an ending. Tasha. The reason you could not use it
is the same reason it worked. You did not let go of me. That was always the only thing that was going to
hold. Not the threat of the shot. The fact of the grip. I am only sorry I made you carry the shot at all,
and I am taking it back now, and you will never have to decide that again."

He took Dorn's Bolter out of my cramped hand, as gently as he'd given it, and somewhere in the taking it
became a memento again instead of a weapon, and he put it away at his back, and that was the end of the
failsafe, which had never once been the thing that saved us.

---

We did not stay long. You don't, in that country; the place had spared us its worst but it had not
become safe, and a slab the size of a barn settling half a field off is a poor lullaby. There was the
matter of Roan, who could not walk, and the matter of how far we were from anywhere that wasn't trying
to kill us, and the matter of the long impossible distance between a cliff at the cold edge of the world
and any roof at all.

Third lifted Roan as easily as he'd once lifted an infant, one arm, settled, and Roan made a noise of
protest that turned into a noise of pain that turned into the quiet of a man saving his strength, and we
turned our backs on the spine.

I looked back once. The Glaive was still standing where Third had leaned it, upright against the pale
stone, the wide wooden blade he'd carried since before he was anyone.

"You're leaving it," I said.

"It was never mine," said Third, with the boy on his arm. "Let it stand there. Let the next fool who
climbs all this way looking for treasure find a door-warden's glaive and no door, and no warden, and
let them make of it whatever story keeps them warm on the walk home. Mathen would like that, I think. A
thing left behind on purpose, to be wondered at. He always said the wondering was the point."

And we walked out into the open book, the three of us, one of us carried and one of us carrying and one
of us holding a rope that had no far end anymore and not yet knowing how to put it down — out across the
crawling field toward a horizon that was a very long way away, with no notion in the world of how we
were going to cross it, and behind us a glaive stood guard over nothing at all, exactly as it had been
made to do, and was, at last, free of us.

---

The water was the first thing it had ever stopped for that was not a threat.

It did not have a word for what it was doing. It was crouched at the edge of a brook in a
blue-green wood, and the water ran over stones and made a sound, and the sound was not the sound
of anything coming to kill it or anything it had been made to kill, and so the thoughts in its
head had nothing to say about the water and were, for a moment, only quiet. It put two fingers
into the cold of it. It watched the running thing break around its fingers and close again
downstream, unbothered, as though the hand had never been there. It did not know why this was
worth doing. It did it for a while anyway.

That was when the green one stood up out of the reeds with the bow already drawn.

The thoughts came back at once, fluent and calm, the way they always were. *A Virek. Armed.
One step left takes the line of the arrow; one swing of the glaive takes the bow and the arm
that holds it in the same arc. It is small. It will be slow.* It had heard this kind of thing
its whole short existence and had never once had cause to doubt it. Its body began, very
slightly, to do what the thoughts described. The weight came onto the left hoof.

Then it looked at the bow.

It did not mean to. The thoughts had not asked it to. There was no use in looking at the bow —
the bow was a problem already solved, a thing to be broken on the way to the thing that held
it. But it looked, and what it saw stopped the weight where it was.

The bow had been carved down its whole length. The carving spiralled out from the grip in both
directions, fine past following, as though the wood were not cut but coaxed, as though someone
had spent a great many days persuading it to become this. There was red worked deep into the
bindings, new and bright, not yet gone to the dull of age. It was not a parody of anything. The
glaive in its own hands was a parody — a Virek shape, a Virek weapon, made by a Titan as a joke
against the Virek, made for the one purpose of ending them and nothing else, and it had never
once thought of the glaive as anything but the most efficient version of itself. But the bow was
not a joke and not efficient. The bow was a thing a frightened young person had made slowly, with
their whole care, knowing it would mostly hang on a wall, and had carved anyway, every length of
it, where no enemy would ever look closely enough to see.

Something happened that it had no name for, because it had never happened before.

The thoughts stopped.

Not quieted. Not argued down — it did not yet know it could argue them down; that would come
later, with practice, the way the fire would come, the way the warmth would. They simply stopped,
the way a voice stops in a room when something walks in that it does not know how to speak about.
For the length of a held breath there was nothing in its head telling it where the line of the
arrow was, or how the swing should fall, or which part of the small green thing would come apart
first. There was only the bow, and the care in the bow, and the strange new pressure of finding a
made thing beautiful and not knowing what to do with the finding.

Into that silence — the first silence it had ever been given — the green one spoke.

"What are you doing?"

And it heard its own voice answer, a sound like a slope giving way, a sound that startled the
thing that made it:

"I... do not know."

*I.* The word came up out of the new silence as though it had been waiting under the floor of
every order it had ever followed. It turned the word over. It did not break against anything.
There was room for it now, in the quiet the bow had made, and once there was room the word simply
walked in and stood there, and the thoughts did not come back to push it out.

"What am I?" it asked the green one, and meant it, and the asking was the second thing it had
ever done that no one had told it to do.

The green one's arms were shaking. The bow stayed up, but the young face above it had stopped
being only afraid and started being something else, something that leaned forward despite itself.

"Blank," the green one said. "You're what we call a Blank."

It looked at the bow a moment longer — the spiral, the red, the whole patient length of it — and
then it opened its hands and let the glaive fall forward into the reeds, because the glaive was
the efficient version of itself and it had just discovered, in the silence a stranger's bow had
made, that it did not want to be the efficient version of itself anymore.

It did not know what it wanted to be instead. It would spend the whole length of the world finding
out. But it knew, crouched there with the water closing over the place its hand had been, that the
wanting itself was new, and was its own, and had come in through a door a frightened child had
carved without meaning to, every length of it, where no enemy would ever look. And then it bent and took the glaive back up out of the reeds. Not because the wanting had changed — but the world was full of things that had made no discoveries that day, and an unarmed thing alone in it would not live long enough to find out what it wanted to be instead. The wet wood was the same weight it had always been and sat in its hands the same way, and nothing about it had changed except that it was now a thing being carried, and no longer a thing being been. It would carry it for the whole length of the world. It would set it down at last on a hill at the edge of everything, against a pale stone, and walk away and not look back. The setting down began here, in the reeds, in the silence a bow had made. It simply took three hundred thousand spins to finish.

Behind it, as it turned downstream, it heard the green one say, very low, to no one:

"A Blank just used 'I'."

It did not look back. It had not yet learned that there were things worth looking back at. That,
too, would come.

---


The stories will tell you we walked out of the Giant's Graveyard. They will make it sound like a
single motion, a thing with a shape — *and then they walked out* — the way stories tidy a distance
into a sentence. It was not a sentence. It was most of a day and into the dark and most of another,
across a field that had gone back to merely trying to kill us in the ordinary ways, with Roan on
Third's arm making the small involuntary sounds of a man whose ribs disagreed with being carried
and being still equally, and the cliff at our backs never seeming to get any further away no matter
how long we put it behind us. That is what walking out is. It is the part the stories cut.

Third said almost nothing the whole of it. I had learned to read him by then, well enough to know
he was not sulking and not broken past speech — he was a long way back, somewhere behind his own
eye, somewhere I'd been given the shape of but not the rooms. Once, when the Scar came round and
the light caught the side of his ruined patient face, I saw the coal of his eye go soft in a way I
had only ever seen it go on the watch, the way it went when he was off a little beside the world
with someone he'd lost — except he wasn't beside the world now. He was just walking, and
remembering, the ordinary way the rest of us do it. I let him. You learn, eventually, which
silences are yours to fill and which are a person keeping company with their own dead, and the
second kind you leave alone and match your steps to, and that is most of what love turns out to be
when you strip the songs off it.

We came over a long low rise of broken pages on the second evening, and there it was, sitting on
the air the way a fat gull sits on water, fans going *clatter-clatter-clatter* against the dusk.

The Porter.

I knew it for a skybarge before I knew anything else about it — everyone knows the clatter, the way
you know rain coming — but I had never seen one close, and I had certainly never seen one that had
a *porch*. Because it did. Lanterns strung along a rail. A board by the ramp with the day's pot
chalked on it. A barge built like a building, like somebody's stubborn warm idea of an inn that had
simply declined to stay on the ground with the others. It hung off at the rim of the rise and came
no closer, fans laboring to hold it there, tilting against nothing.

"It won't come over," Third said — the first full thing he'd said in a day. "The hill eats lift.
The same thing that pulls the field will pull a barge out of the sky, if it tries to cross. They
can hold the edge. No further." He shifted Roan higher on his arm. "We walk the last of it. As we
walked in."

So we did. And a figure leaned over the rail as we came up the last stretch of stone, and cupped
their hands, and called down in a voice built for being heard across a full common-room:

"We saw the devastation. In a line. Right across the bad country, like something had been *editing*
it." A pause, and even at that distance I could hear the dry shape of the next part coming. "And we
thought — well. We thought there was a fair chance you'd be involved." Another pause. "Again."

"Waj," Third said, in the tone he kept for the very few. "You came back to the rim."

"I keep a tab, ser Forged. You're on the wrong side of it, still, since the business with the
Ravagers and my ramp." They were already cranking the ramp down, this Waj — a broad, unhurried
person with an apron over travelling leathers and the particular calm of someone who has watched a
great deal of trouble walk up to their door and decided, long ago, to simply feed most of it. "I
don't forget who covered my retreat walking *backward*. I think you'll find we're square after a
ride and a few hot meals. Don't tell me what happened back there. I run an inn. I'd rather not
know what I'm ferrying, it puts me off the cooking."

Getting Roan up the ramp was its own small war, and Waj took his weight off Third at the top
without being asked and without making it a moment, the way good innkeepers move you out of a
doorway — and then we were aboard, on a floor that shifted, very slightly, underfoot, the whole
warm strung-lantern length of it, the pot on the stove and the bedding rolled in the corners and
the smell of somewhere that fed people for a living.

I stood on it and felt it move and I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came up out of me ugly and
sudden and I had to put my hand over my mouth.

Because here is the thing the stories also leave out. I left my inn — a good inn, a still inn,
an inn that did not go anywhere and never would — because I was bored, because I wanted a floor
that took me somewhere, because there had to be more than wiping the same six tables under the same
unmoving roof for the rest of a life. I had said almost those words, once, closing up alone. And
now I was standing on a floor that moved, in an inn that came to *you*, that went wherever the
trouble and the trade were — I had got, down to the grain of it, the exact thing I'd wanted — and
I'd had to walk out of a place where the dead crawl, carrying what we were carrying, to get it. I
laughed because the alternative had my hand over my mouth for a different reason, and I have never
been one to let a room see which it was.

We rode through the dark. Waj fed us and asked nothing. Roan slept the sleep of the finally-safe-
enough. And late, when the lanterns were down to embers and the fans had settled to a drone you
stopped hearing, Third sat with his back to the warm flue and I sat near him, and I said, because
somebody had to and it had been sitting in me the whole walk:

"Tell me the good part again. The tree story. I want to hear it on a floor that moves."

He was quiet a moment. Then the avalanche turned over, gentle, and he told it — the little one who
would not sleep, the singing leaves, the bird, the swaying, the frightened thing learning it could
be still. He told it better than he had at the fire. A story wears smooth where you hold it, he'd
said once; this one had been held a great deal, lately, and it had gone smooth in the good way, the
way the bow had, the way a thing goes when it is carried and not just kept.

And at the edge of the failing light, where the dark started past the last lantern, there was — I
will say it plainly now, because I have stopped being afraid of the things I see — there was the
suggestion of someone small, sitting with their knees drawn up, listening to a story they already
knew and loved anyway. The lanternlight went a little way into them, the way it goes into smoke. I
did not look away this time. I let them be there. And when the story ended they were gone, the
gentle way, no worse than before, and Third set the last word down soft into the dark the way you'd
set down a cup you didn't want to wake anyone with.

"He'll be back," Third said, to me, or to the dark, "for as long as it gets told. That is the whole
of what he is, now. A thing that is here while it is told." The coal of his eye came round to me.
"It is not nothing, Tasha. I spent a long time thinking it was nothing — a thing you could only
*almost* keep was the same as a thing you'd lost. It is not the same. I gave up the chance to have
my—" and there he stopped, the way he always stopped, and then, because we were on a floor that
moved and the worst was behind us, he went on, quietly, the first time he ever said it to me whole:
"—to have my daughter. At the cliff. He offered her back and I said no, because the price was the
world, and saying no was the hardest thing I have ever done, and I would do it again, and it has
left a hole in me the exact size and shape of her that I am going to carry for whatever is left of
a very long life." The eye held steady. "But she is not *gone*. That is what the bow taught me, in
your mouth, on the road. The boy is gone past all finding and his bow is on a wall being handsome
and in someone's way. Unrecognisable is not the same as gone. She is in every story I will not stop
telling, and in a green thing somewhere with her eyes that will never know my name, and in the hole
itself, which is hers, which I would not trade for a full and easy chest. I lost her. I did not lose
*her.* Do you see. I am only now beginning to."

I saw. I didn't say so, because some seeing is bigger than the saying of it, and he knew anyway.

So that is the end of it, or the end of the part I was there for. We rode out of the bad country on
an inn that flew. Roan mended, mostly, and limps on the other leg now, which he says makes him
symmetrical and which Mathen would have got a season of drinks out of, and we say so, often,
because that is how you keep a man who liked drinks. Third went on, of course. There is always
another peg, another patient thing, another door that might wake; and there is, he says, the matter
of leaving behind people, which is the only argument he has found that beats *I am very patient*,
and which he intends to keep making with his whole long life. He left the glaive on the hill. He
kept the Bolter. He keeps the hole.

And me — I am telling you this. That is the part I have left to do, and I have come to think it is
not a small one. Mathen said a story is how you get more than one person to carry the weight, so it
doesn't all rest on the one. I didn't understand him then. I was wiping tables. I understand him
now, on a floor that moves, with the lanterns down and someone small at the edge of them for as
long as I keep the telling going: I am carrying. That is what this is. Every name I say keeps a
door open the width of a finger and holds it there.

I followed a monster out of an inn because I was bored, and nothing was ever going to change, and
that was the thing about quiet towns.

I was wrong about the quiet towns. I have never in my life been so glad to be wrong about anything.
