# FRD — BOOK 2
## Scene 3 Draft — The Naming
*July 2026 · First draft*

---

We were four days out from the clearing before the question of what to call him became a problem I had to solve rather than a curiosity I could carry around.

It started with an innkeeper. A small place, the kind with three rooms and a common fire, and the woman behind the counter looked at what was standing behind my shoulder and went the particular grey that people go when their body hasn't decided yet whether to run. She didn't ask his name. She asked mine, twice, as though repeating the question might produce a different companion.

*"And your—"* She didn't finish it. Her eyes went to him and stopped there, the way eyes did.

*"He doesn't have one,"* I said, before he could answer for himself in whatever flat way he'd have answered. *"We're working on it."*

We were not working on it. I had been calling him nothing at all, which is a harder habit to maintain in conversation than you'd think — you find yourself constructing whole sentences to avoid a pronoun, or you use *it*, which felt wrong within the first day and worse by the fourth, though I couldn't yet have told you why. Once, out of frustration, I'd called him *Blank*, because that was what he was — an absence where a name should sit — and he hadn't objected, hadn't reacted at all, which somehow made it worse. Blank is what you call a shape on a form. It isn't what you call someone you've been walking beside for four days, sharing a fire with, watching hold still through a rain shower like it was a personal insult he'd decided not to dignify with movement.

We walked on past the inn — I hadn't liked the innkeeper's face, and neither, I think, had he, though he'd have called it something else if you'd asked — and made camp early, in a stand of trees that felt old in a way I didn't have the language for yet. The kind of old where you notice the quiet has a shape to it.

*"You need a name,"* I said, once the fire was going.

*"I do not require one."*

*"That's not what I asked."*

The eye came round to me. I'd started to learn, by then, that this was not the same as being looked at by a person — there was no face behind it doing the small human work of deciding what expression to wear. It was just attention, arriving.

*"Blank isn't a name,"* I said. *"Blank is a type. It's what you call a form before someone's filled it in. You're not a form. You're—"* I gestured, uselessly, at the whole of him, the glaive laid across his knees, the fire finding the wrongness of the eye and doing nothing to soften it. *"You're a person. Even if you're not sure of it yet. People have names."*

*"I have been given a name before."*

That stopped me. It wasn't what I'd expected — not a refusal, not the flat non-answer I'd have put money on. I waited, the way I was learning to wait with him, which is to say without filling the space myself.

*"I do not use it,"* he said, and that was all. The eye didn't dim, exactly. It just held very still, the way a held breath holds still, and I understood — not the whole of it, not then, but enough — that I had touched something with an edge on it, and that the correct thing to do was not touch it again.

*"Alright,"* I said.

He didn't offer more. I didn't ask for it. I have thought about that moment often, since — turned it over the way you turn over a stone you suspect is sitting on top of something. I still don't know most of what it was sitting on. I know only that whatever name he'd had, it wasn't mine to ask for, any more than it was his to spend twice.

I thought about it while I ate — dried meat, a heel of bread from the inn I'd bought without going back in, the last of what I'd taken from the pile in the clearing — and I thought, as I often did in that first stretch of road, about my uncle.

He was a woodsman. Not the kind who cuts and hauls for a living, though he did that too when the season called for it — the kind who *understood* a forest, the way some people understand horses or weather. He'd taught me to read broken branches for the size and hurry of what had broken them, to tell a deer trail from a goat trail from a boot print trying to look like either, to build a fire that wouldn't be seen from more than a few trees away. Everything I'd used in that clearing — the axe, the angle I'd taken against the tree, the decision to fight rather than run because running teaches a chaser where you're weakest — I'd learned from him, in a forest very like the one we were sitting in now, a lifetime and a war ago.

He'd died in it. Not fighting — that was the strange, bitter shape of it, the thing that had never sat right no matter how many times I turned it over. He wasn't a soldier. He didn't take a side, not really, not in a war he thought had nothing to do with a forest that belonged to nobody. The Herdukii came through anyway — driven off their range by the fighting further south, my father always said, though nobody really knew, and it hardly mattered by the time it was done. They weren't hunting him. They weren't hunting anyone. They were just moving, the way displaced things move, taking whatever was in front of them, and what was in front of them, on one particular day, was a woodsman checking his lines near the border of a country called Luek that doesn't exist anymore except as a word old people still use.

He didn't die of a wound. That was the part I could never make anyone outside the family understand, when I tried. He died of what the encounter left behind — some sickness carried on them or with them, the kind that doesn't announce itself with teeth marks, that just settles into a person over the following days and doesn't let go. My mother used to say he'd have laughed at it, if he'd had the strength left to laugh at anything — that a man who'd walked through forests full of things that wanted to kill him outright, for his whole life, had been taken down by something that didn't even know he was there.

I told Third all of this. Not in one clean piece — it came out sideways, the fire between us, him listening with the particular Third-quality of attention that didn't interrupt and didn't rush me and didn't, so far as I could tell, need me to hurry up and get to a point.

*"His name was Third,"* I said, when I got there. *"Nobody ever told me why. I think it might just have been what his mother called him — third child, third son, something practical that turned into a name the way practical things sometimes do. But it fit him. He came third in everything and never seemed to mind — third to the fire in winter, third to the good tools, and it never made him smaller, somehow. Just — steady. Unclaimed. His own."*

I looked at the shape across the fire from me. The seams at his elbows. The glaive he hadn't set down since I'd known him, resting the way a habit rests, close enough to reach without looking.

*"You know the forest,"* I said. *"Or you're learning it faster than I did. You keep going, when a thing would stop most people. You don't belong to a side — you didn't ask whose bounty it was before you cleared that clearing, and I don't think you'd have cared either way. I think it fits."*

He was quiet long enough that I thought I'd have to say something else, fill the silence the way I sometimes did when it got too wide to bear. But before I could, he said:

*"Third."*

Just that. Not a question. Not tasting it the way a person might, turning a new word over to see how it sat. He said it the way you'd confirm a fact you'd just been told and had no reason to doubt.

*"Yes,"* I said.

*"I will answer to Third."*

And that, as it turned out, was the whole of it. I'd expected — I don't know what I'd expected. More ceremony, maybe, or none at all, a flat acknowledgment that meant nothing and changed nothing. What I got instead was somewhere between: no ceremony, and everything changed. I called him Third the next morning without thinking about it, the way you'd use anyone's name, and he answered to it without hesitation, as though he'd always had it and I'd simply been the one to notice.

I didn't know, that night by the fire, that I was doing anything larger than solving a small practical problem — what do you call the thing you're travelling with, so an innkeeper's question has an answer. I know better now. But I've come to think that's how the large things mostly happen. Not with ceremony. With a girl who missed her uncle, and a fire, and a name that was sitting unused, looking for someone to fit.
