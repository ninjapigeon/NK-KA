# THIRD — BOOK 2
## Writer's Outline v.1
*Developed in collaboration with Claude · June 2026*
*Companion document: Third — World Bible v.15*

---

### OVERVIEW

**Narrator:** Adura. First person throughout. Warm, direct, less guarded than Tasha. Her voice is established in *take.md* and *Era_2.md* — those documents are the tonal reference for this book.

**Structure:** Chronological. No braid. Three acts plus a close-third coda with no narrator.

**The book's question:** Who is the man she named?

**The book's shape:** She names him in Act 1. The rest of the book is her account of finding out who she named — all the things he doesn't tell her, all the things she half-sees and can't quite reach. She never gets the full answer. The reader of Book 1 already has it. The gap between what she knows and what the reader knows is where the book lives.

**The book's end:** Her narration ends when she does. The slat-book's last page has holes for the next slat she never added. Third carries the book. He keeps walking. The Lonely Man register.

---

### STRUCTURAL NOTE — THE SLAT-BOOK

Act 1 is written from memory — Adura reconstructing the early road retrospectively, once she has the clockwork press and blank slats Third gave her.

Act 2 onward is the journal proper — present-tense entries, running forward. The early pages of the slat-book reconstruct Act 1; the journal catches up to now and then moves ahead of it.

This means Adura's voice in Act 1 is slightly more reflective than Act 2 — she knows how it ends, in the sense that she knows they're still travelling together, still alive, still on the road. She doesn't know how her own story ends.

## ACT ONE — THE ROAD
*(Written from memory. The voice of someone looking back at the beginning of a thing she didn't know was a thing yet.)*

---

### SCENE 1 — The Village

**What happens:** The attack. Adura's self-rescue. Third walks out of the trees.

**How it opens:** She doesn't narrate what was lost. She narrates what she *did*. The axe. The sword she lifted from someone who no longer needed it. The group around her when he came. She describes the village practically — the way you describe a place you're not going back to and have already finished grieving. The reader feels the loss through what she doesn't linger on.

**Third's arrival:** Not heroic. Sudden, efficient, and completely indifferent to how frightening he is. He finishes the job. Then he looks at her. She looks at him. Neither of them knows yet what the other is for.

**Tone:** Practical. Tight. No dwelling. She is the kind of person who takes stock and moves.

**Close on:** The silence after. Two creatures in a clearing, one of which is made of wood and has one eye, and a decision neither of them has made yet.

---

### SCENE 2 — The Offer and the Refusal

**What happens:** Third offers Blacks for her share of the kill. She refuses payment and attaches herself.

**Register:** Verbatim spirit from *take.md* and *Era_2.md*. This is the scene that already exists — it now belongs to Adura. He says "interesting rationale" and keeps walking. She keeps pace. He doesn't send her away because sending her away requires more social machinery than he has. *"Humins,"* he says. She grunts.

**Small beat:** She takes the cloak from the pile. The belt. She leaves everything else. She is the kind of person who takes only what she can carry and use. This detail will rhyme quietly against the slat-book — she fills that too, no more than she can use.

**The coins:** He offers two Blacks for her kills. She refuses. He puts them back. He continues walking. She bounds after him.

**Close on:** They are walking. She has decided something. He has not decided anything because he is not yet the kind of being who decides things. He just doesn't stop her.

---

### SCENE 3 — The Naming

**What happens:** She names him Third.

**When it happens:** Early on the road — within the first act, once she's established that "Blank" is what someone calls a type, not a person. The naming lands chronologically early and earns its weight through accumulation rather than position. The book is what she's calling it after.

**The uncle:** Her uncle was a woodsman named Third. Taught her everything she knows about surviving in a forest — which is most of what she knows about surviving at all. He died in the Pale and Green War. Herdukii moving through his forest near Luek, not looking for him specifically, killing him by disease and bad timing. Not a soldier. Not a combatant. Just a woodsman who was there. She carries his memory as the person who made her capable. She thinks the name fits: something that knows the forest, that keeps going, that belongs to no side.

**Third's response:** The grunt-of-acknowledgment. Not moved, apparently. But he answers to it from that moment without being asked twice.

**What the reader does:** The reader of Book 1 stops here. The name that appears on the last page of that novel — the name Tasha never questions, the name nobody explains — this is where it came from. A girl with a stolen sword and a dead uncle, standing on a road she doesn't know. Not cosmic. Just hers.

**Adura's note (retrospective, since this is written from memory):** She probably notes that she wasn't sure it would stick. It stuck immediately. She finds this characteristic of him.

---

### SCENE 4 — Learning the Terms

**What happens:** The episodic early road. Compressed, warm, practical.

**What she teaches him:** What it means to need to eat, sleep, stay dry. He is worse at all of these than she expected. He pays what people ask for without argument, which wastes money and she can't fix it. He doesn't understand why rainfall is unpleasant. He doesn't understand why a bug-infested inn is different from any other inn.

**What he teaches her:** Nothing intentionally. Everything accidentally. She learns to read his stillnesses — different qualities, different meanings. The eye dims when something costs him. It brightens when something catches him. She doesn't know yet what any specific dimming or brightening means. She writes it down anyway, later, because she is that kind of person.

**Worldbuilding texture:** Anka outside a settlement. The road's wildness, the toddler-god aesthetic. This is her first extended time outside a village. Beauty-then-lethal rhythm throughout. The world reads wrong, but wrong in a way that's always been true, so she just keeps walking.

**Small recurring beat:** He pays too much for things. She tries to teach him to haggle. She never quite gets there. *"It was what they said they wanted."* She explains that's not how it works. He accepts this explanation without applying it.

**Close on:** She realizes she is going to have to do the work of making his accounts into something a person can carry. She hasn't decided to do this yet. She's just going to.

---

### SCENE 5 — First Job Together

**What happens:** Third takes a contract. Adura comes along because she's there and he doesn't send her away.

**The dynamic established:** She's not a fighter at his level. Doesn't pretend to be. But she's observant, quick, and doesn't panic — these are things Third notices without articulating. She is useful. He registers this the way he registers most things: by continuing to allow it.

**What she sees:** Third working. It's not that it's violent — she's used to violence. It's that it has no feeling in it, the efficiency. She hasn't decided yet whether that means he has no feelings or very well-hidden ones. She suspects the latter. She has no evidence for this suspicion.

**After:** He gives a flat account of what happened. She realizes, watching him do this, that his accounts are the raw material of something, and that she is the person who knows how to cook them. This is not a decision. It is a recognition.

**Close on:** The silence after the account. She says something. He answers. She thinks: *I'm going to be writing this down someday.*

---

### SCENE 6 — The Side-Step

**What happens:** First time Adura witnesses Third step sideways into the grey.

**How it looks from outside:** One moment he's there. Then he isn't. Then he is again, seconds later, and something in his manner has changed — a particular quality of return, like a person who has come back from somewhere that left a residue on them. She doesn't know what she's seeing.

**She asks.** He says something like: *"I went somewhere. I am back."* She doesn't push. Not yet. Not this early.

**That evening:** She thinks about it and writes down what it looked like from the outside. Her description is wrong in the specific ways a person without the right vocabulary is always wrong about something they witnessed accurately. She knows it's wrong. She writes it anyway, as a placeholder for the right words, which she doesn't have yet.

**The structural note:** Adura never gets the inside of the grey. She always has the outside view. Her account of Third is always the account of someone who watched and wrote down what they could see — which is most of it, but never all of it. This is the limitation and the gift of her narration.

**Close on:** She notes the particular quality of stillness he has when he returns from wherever he goes. Different from his other stillnesses. More — she thinks the word is *expensive.* She doesn't know why she thinks that.

---

### SCENE 7 — The Drift Begins / Act 1 Close

**What happens:** She starts gravitating toward settled life. She doesn't decide to. It happens the way things happen.

**How it happens:** A town she likes. Work she's good at — something that uses her observational skills, her practicality, her ability to read situations and people quickly. The discovery that she's been moving long enough to want to stop for a while. She tells Third. He accepts this with the same affect he accepts everything, which she has learned is not the same as not caring.

**The goodbye:** No ceremony. They part at the edge of the road. She thinks she'll see him in a few hundred spins. She's right.

**Third's face:** She describes, one final time in this retrospective section, what his stillness looked like when she said she was stopping. The eye dimmed slightly, then steadied. She writes: *"He looked at me the way he looks at things he is adding to his accounting. I did not know then what that meant. I think I know now."*

**Close on — Act 1 end:** Her first night in the town. The road quiet outside. She lists what she has: the cloak, the belt, the lifted sword, whatever wages the road paid. She doesn't list what she doesn't have. She doesn't need to. The reader already knows.

## ACT TWO — THE JOURNAL
*(The clockwork press arrives. The slat-book begins. The early entries reconstruct Act 1 from memory; then the journal catches up and runs forward. Register shifts — more immediate, less retrospective. Sometimes Third's exact words, as she recorded them in the moment.)*

---

### SCENE 8 — The Gift

**What happens:** Third visits. He produces the clockwork press and a supply of blank slats.

**The gift:** It is the nicest object in the room immediately. A clockwork press is expensive — rare, available only in larger towns, a significant purchase even for someone with accumulated Blacks. A supply of blank slats alongside it means he bought the whole system. He doesn't explain why. He may not entirely know. He heard her say, early on the road, that she'd like to record their travels someday. He bought the thing that would let her do that.

**Her reaction:** She asks how much it cost. He tells her. She closes her eyes briefly. She takes it. *"You paid how much?"* / *"It was what they said they wanted."* / A beat. He still hasn't learned to haggle.

**The first entry:** That night, she begins. The first entry is practical — what the book is, when she got it, who gave it to her. The second entry begins: *"I should go back to the beginning."*

**What this means going forward:** The slat-book is now a character in the book. It fills as she fills it. Its last page has binding holes for the next slat she never added. The reader knows this before she does.

**Close on:** She writes by lamplight. The press is heavy, well-made, the mechanism satisfying in the way precise things are satisfying. She thinks: he bought me something useful. She thinks: he bought me something that is also beautiful. She thinks: that's more like him than he knows.

---

### SCENE 9 — The Account Structure Established

**What happens:** A visit where Third brings an account of something that happened between visits. The translation dynamic is dramatized for the first time.

**Third's telling:** Flat, factual, sequential. What happened, in what order, what he did about it. No texture, no weight-sharing, no sense that he's carrying anything by saying it. It is a report. It is accurate. It is also almost completely useless as a story.

**Adura's translation:** She gives it back to him in her own words. She watches the eye change — not pleased exactly, but something adjacent to relieved. The glow steadies slightly, the way it does when something that was costing him costs him less.

**What she doesn't say:** She doesn't tell him what she's doing. She doesn't tell him this is something you can ask someone to do. She just keeps doing it.

**A note she makes that the reader recognizes:** *"He doesn't know yet that this is something you can ask someone to do. He thinks I'm just writing it down."*

**Close on:** She writes the translated account into the slat-book. Then she writes: *"This is what I actually do with these entries. I translate. I don't know yet what I'm translating toward."*

---

### SCENE 10 — The Artifact Appears

**What happens:** The traveler comes to her town. The artifact is with them. Nobody recognizes it as anything but a remarkable old tool.

**The traveler:** Anron Hool — a skybarge navigator, passing through, someone with means. Not a merchant exactly. Someone who stops long enough to become a face rather than a stranger. They have the navigation map aboard their vessel — moored somewhere accessible to the town, close enough to visit. The Hool name implies a family lineage in skybarge work that runs forward to Waj Hool in Book 1 (~32,000 spins later); whether this echo is noted on the page is the author's call, but it is intentional.

**The encounter:** The traveler shows it to people because they're proud of it. It does one thing so well, has done it so long, that they've stopped thinking of it as remarkable. They mention it in passing: *"Nothing else comes close. Half the size of the ship, mind you — the old builders made things differently. But it's always current. Show you a washed-out road before you get there, a new settlement before you've heard of it, weather coming in three hours before anyone else smells it."* They say this the way you'd say anything about a reliable tool you've had your whole life. Matter-of-fact. A little proud. Not awed.

**Third's contact with it:** Either he crosses on this vessel briefly (or visits it), and notices the map's material — large, unusual, good craftsmanship — without flagging it. He doesn't go still. He notes it the way he notes anything interesting: he notes it, and moves on.

**Adura's entry:** Brief, logistical. Third crossed on / visited a particular vessel. Unusual map, she mentions, because he mentioned it to her in passing. No significance flagged.

**Close on:** The traveler moves on. The town goes back to normal. Nothing happened.

---

### SCENE 11 — Episodic Entries

**What happens:** A run of journal entries covering several visits across many spins. The book's middle register — the texture of the years.

**Third's accounts (a selection of what he brings her):**
- A Shredder hunt (she writes it down without knowing it's revenge; the reader of Book 1 catches it).
- The geyser-clearing work — she notes he does this without pay, which she finds impractical and characteristic.
- The slow change of the world: Hexwood arrows in Ravager hands. *"He mentioned that the arrows that used to snap when he pulled them out no longer snap. He said this the way he says everything. I wrote it down."*
- The Powder Charter's local operation tightening its grip on a site he'd been keeping free. He notes it with the equanimity of something that has outlasted everything so far.

**Her translations:** Getting sharper. She's learning which of his flat reports are actually accounts of things that cost him, and which are genuinely flat. The distinction matters. She notes it without explaining it to him.

**The relationship in this section:** Warm, episodic, without drama. This is the good part. The reader of Book 1 knows it won't last. Adura doesn't know this. She is just living it.

**The Sougad rumor (placed here, within the Dorn-era entries):** Third brings it as a routine item in a larger account. A traveler's report, third-hand at minimum. A floating figure. Six vessels on the back. Magefire. Passed through a settlement near the eastern passage. Nobody approached. Nobody saw where from or to.

Third's exact words: *"A traveler from the eastern passage reported seeing a floating figure near [settlement]. Six vessels on the back. Magefire. Nobody approached it. Nobody saw where it went."* He moves on to the next item.

Adura's translation / journal note: She writes it down verbatim, because she writes everything down. Then she adds one line: *"He mentioned this the way he mentions weather. I wrote it down the way I write everything. I am not sure why it seems worth noting, except that he told it to me, which is not nothing."*

She doesn't know why she flagged it. She just did.

**[NOTE FOR DRAFTING: The Book 1 reader stops on "six vessels." Third carries two. Sougad carries six. That number is the tell — the reader does the math Adura cannot do because she doesn't know the math exists yet. Nobody in the book notices. The entry sits between two other entries about road conditions and a contract paid late.]*

**Close on:** A quiet entry. Something small Third brought her — a piece of flora from a region she'd described wanting to see, or a trinket from a market, or something that cost almost nothing but showed he'd been paying attention. She adds it to a list she keeps. The list is longer than she expected.

---

### SCENE 12 — The Standards Guild, Early Form

**What happens:** Woven into the episodic entries — Third's observations about the Guild's early shape, delivered flat in his accounts and translated by Adura.

**The information:** The local Standards Guild is tightening control over Buhar ammunition. A site Third had been keeping free for poor harvesters has been claimed and tolled. Buhars are everywhere now — unremarkable. What's changing is who controls the powder.

**Third's account:** Flat, observational. He notes the Guild's behavior the way he notes geological change — as a slow process he's watching, not alarmed by, not resisting. He has outlasted every institution he's watched form so far.

**Adura's translation:** She is more irritated by this than he is, which she notes. She has practical experience with the toll system. She finds his long-view equanimity maddening in a way she recognizes as affectionate.

**Small worldbuilding texture:** The Buhar-caliber standardization. The cargo dimension rules. The plinth requirement for new towns. She mentions one of these in passing as a town she knows gets its founding plinth — she finds the ceremony slightly absurd; Third has been watching towns rise and fall since before the Guild existed and doesn't find the ceremony absurd so much as young.

**Close on:** She notes: *"He has watched more things become institutions than I will ever watch become ruins. I don't know what to do with that. I wrote it down."*

---

### SCENE 13 — The Shared Hunt / Dorn

**What happens:** Third takes a contract that turns into more than one hunter's job. Dorn appears.

**The hunt:** Third is a step too far — a creature beyond his immediate reach, the specific failure mode that recurs. A shot comes from somewhere elevated. The creature drops. Third locates the shooter. They regard each other across a dead Titanspawn.

**The ambiguity:** Possibly both had the same contract. Possibly Dorn hesitated because he wasn't entirely sure which creature was the greater threat. This ambiguity is the seed of his character — practical, watchful, not in a hurry to commit. He waits for information before acting. This time, acting without full information would have meant shooting Third.

**Dorn's introduction (via Third's account):** Third's telling is flat as always. *"There was another hunter. He had a Bolter. The shot was well-placed."* Adura's translation reads the subtext correctly: *"What he means is he was glad someone else was there, which he will not say, and that the Bolter startled him a little, which he will also not say."*

**The joke:** Third reports a joke Dorn told in the aftermath. Third reports it verbatim without indicating he understood it was a joke. Adura finds this funnier than anything Dorn actually said.

**Close on:** Third mentions that the other hunter — Dorn — asked if he was on contract. Third confirmed he was. Dorn said something flat and practical. They parted. Third says this as though it's unremarkable. Adura writes: *"I think he liked him. He won't say so."*

---

### SCENE 14 — Dorn Visits

**What happens:** Dorn comes with Third to meet Adura. The trio forms.

**Dorn's register:** Easier in a town than Third is (not saying much), but practically so — he's been around long enough to know how to be useful in populated spaces. He notices Adura is the person who makes Third legible to other people. He respects this with the specific respect of someone who recognizes a skill they don't have.

**The joke dynamic:** He tells one. It lands. Third watches this process with what Adura interprets as scientific interest — he is observing how the joke functions socially, what it changes in the room, why it works. He does not tell a joke himself.

**The Bolter:** Adura asks about it. He explains more warmly than Third would — practical, matter-of-fact, the tool of someone who's had it long enough to know its limits. By this era, Buhars are unremarkable as objects. What's notable is the care he takes with it.

**The three together:** A different texture than two-plus-one. She notes this without being able to name it yet. Something about a third person — particularly this third person, with his tool-jokes and his watchfulness — changes the shape of things. It's not warmer, exactly. It's more like a table with a third leg: it stops wobbling.

**Small beat:** Third does something that surprises her in front of Dorn — pays attention to something small she mentioned wanting, or corrects a detail in her retelling, or produces the exact right thing at the exact right moment. Dorn catches her reaction. He says nothing. She knows he noticed.

**Close on:** After Dorn leaves. Third stays a little longer than usual. He doesn't say anything she can write down. She writes down the shape of it instead.

---

### SCENE 15 — The Artifact Disappears

**What happens:** The navigation map is stolen. Third makes the connection. He doesn't share it.

**How Adura gets the news:** A job posting, via her broker network — a skybarge crew seeking scouts and cartographers. She brings it to their next meeting outside town in the broker register she always uses: practical, matter-of-fact, slightly curious about the odd detail. *"Apparently this map they had got stolen. Enormous thing — half the size of the ship. Most just use Humin-sized maps at the largest. Always current, they said — showed new towns, washed out roads, weather coming in. Sounds like a tall tale to me, but they're offering good coin for replacements so apparently it was real. Now they need—"* she enumerates the replacements: a cartographer for new settlements, a weather-watcher, scouts for road conditions.

**The jurisdiction gap (new beat, per World Bible's Legal & Dispute Infrastructure):** Adura, being practical, asks the obvious follow-up — who's looking for whoever took it? The answer, relayed secondhand, is a shrug. The vessel belongs to no town; it sails between compacts and city-states that each handle their own disputes and nothing beyond their own borders. Nobody — no Charter office, no local arbiter — actually has standing to chase a theft that happened somewhere over open country, between places that don't share jurisdiction and never agreed to. It isn't that no one cares. There's simply no one whose job it is. This is the practical reason the crew is hiring replacements instead of hunting a thief: replacing what's gone is achievable. Finding it belongs to no one. Worth a single dry line from Adura here — she's the kind of person who'd clock this immediately and find it more absurd than sad.

**Third does the math:** The material he noted on that crossing. The size. *"Always current."* Each replacement covers one thing the map did simultaneously, automatically, without being asked. The material that wasn't what it looked like.

**He goes still:** Not at the function. At the recognition. A Titan-willed object is gone from where it was, and something took it deliberately, and this is not the first time — not that he has a pattern, because a pattern requires more than one data point, but this is the second data point, and he doesn't have a word for what that is yet either.

**Adura notices:** Between one sentence and the next, he's no longer fully present. A particular quality of stillness — different from the others she's recorded. Longer. A different quality. She keeps talking. She finishes the contract terms. She doesn't push.

**That evening:** She writes it down. She doesn't know what she wrote down. She writes: *"A different stillness today. I've learned to write those down especially. I don't know what this one meant. I don't think he's going to tell me."*

**What Third does not do:** Does not take the cartographer contract. Does not investigate. He doesn't have enough yet to know what he'd be investigating toward. One data point is not a pattern. He files it. He walks.

## ACT THREE — THE LOSSES
*(Short. The journal entries get shorter too — not because Adura is dying yet, but because the things happening are harder to translate.)*

---

### SCENE 16 — Dorn's Death

**What happens:** Adura gets this as an account.

**How she knows before he speaks:** Third comes to her alone. She knows before he finishes crossing the distance to where she's waiting. She has been reading his silences for years. This one is the specific shape of something permanent.

**His account:** What happened. The camp. The Shredder. Coming up from below, from underneath, before Third had finished standing up. *"Before I had finished standing up"* — flat, accurate, and the most precise description of failure she has ever heard from him. She writes it down verbatim.

**She closes the entry.** Starts another one. The second entry is very short. It contains the joke. She'd heard it from Dorn in Scene 14 — the Mender, the Crafter, the Grask. She writes it out in full because she doesn't want it to go unrecorded. She doesn't note why. The reader of Book 1 knows why.

**Third keeps the Bolter:** He doesn't say he's keeping it. He just has it when he leaves, the same way she kept the sword and the cloak from her village. She doesn't comment on this either.

**Close on:** A short entry. She writes: *"The table has two legs again."*

---

### SCENE 17 — After Dorn / Before the Illness

**What happens:** A visit that's quieter than the others have been. The shape of grief, in two people who don't grieve the same way.

**Third's going-on:** He is doing the thing where he goes on. She knows what it costs. She has watched him do it before. She writes: *"The going on is the tribute. That's what someone told him once, and he has never said it, but I think he still believes it."* She knows Lujian's name from his accounts — from entries she wrote down over the years, filing things he said that she didn't fully understand. She has assembled Third's history from the outside, the way she assembles everything.

**What she gives him:** She brings the account of Dorn out again. His exact words, when she first wrote them down — the joke, the timing, the way he deployed it like a tool. She reads it aloud. Third listens. His eye dims and steadies. She says: *"I wrote it down. It's in the book. He didn't go unrecorded."*

**The small gift:** Third brings her something from the road. He's better at this than he was. Still not good. She adds it to the list. She notes the list is longer than she expected it to be.

**Her illness, beginning:** She doesn't name it yet. Just a line at the end of the entry: *"I've been tired lately. Probably the season."* She is the kind of person who writes down everything except what she doesn't want to look at directly.

**Close on:** He leaves. She watches him go from the doorway. She thinks: *he walks the same way he always has.* She finds this, unexpectedly, comforting.

---

### SCENE 18 — The Last Entry

**What happens:** She's ill. She doesn't dramatize it.

**How she writes it:** Practically, with clear eyes — the way she's written everything. She is not afraid. She is organized. She makes sure her affairs are in order with the same competence she brought to the stolen sword and the cloak from the pile. She leaves instructions about the slat-book.

**The last entry:** A translation of something Third told her on his most recent visit. He came with a flat account of something on the road — a job, a creature, a stretch of country he passed through. She renders it in her voice. She gives it weight. She makes it something a person can carry.

She writes well. She always has.

**How it ends:** Mid-thought, or on a small ordinary sentence — something that has no knowledge it's the last thing. She was going to say more. There was more to say.

The last slat. The binding holes along its edge. The next one she didn't make.

## CODA
*(Close-third. No narrator. Short.)*

A nervous villager arrives at the usual meeting spot outside of town — the place Third always comes to, the place meets always happen, because he can't go in and she couldn't come out to him anymore. The villager is carrying something. A parcel. They are trying not to look directly at Third.

They say: *"She wanted you to have this."* They hold out the parcel. They are ready to leave as soon as he takes it.

He takes it.

He opens it. The slat-book. The first edition — printed, the press working, each slat clean and sharp. Her handwriting in the notes she added by hand. A letter tucked into the front, written on a slat in her hand: brief, practical, the way she said everything. Not a goodbye exactly. More like: the last entry.

He reads it. Or perhaps he holds it for a moment without opening the letter. The scene doesn't require us to know.

He closes the slat-book.

He walks.

The Scar moves overhead. The road ahead is the same road it has always been — the same scale, the same wrongness, the same beauty that turns lethal at close range. He has walked it before. He will walk it again. He carries: Dorn's Bolter. The falcatas. The slat-book. The road.

The camera pulls back. He gets smaller.

The road bends.

He's gone the way he always goes — not dramatically, not with ceremony, just no longer visible from here.

The road continues in the direction he was walking.

*(Lonely Man plays.)*

## TONAL REFERENCE

Adura's voice is established in *take.md* and *Era_2.md* — those documents are the primary tonal guide. Key characteristics:

- Warm but not soft. She sees clearly and says what she sees.
- Practical first, emotional second — but the emotion is there, in what she notices and what she chooses to translate.
- Wry without being detached. She finds Third funny in the specific way you find someone funny when you love them and can't say so yet.
- Doesn't dwell on her own losses. Notes them once, files them, keeps moving. This is a coping mechanism she shares with Third, which is probably part of why they work.
- Her translations of his accounts are where the book's warmth lives. She doesn't editorialize often. When she does, it's precise.

**Distinction from Tasha:** Tasha is guarded, dry, observational — she holds people at arm's length as a default. Adura is warmer by nature, more willing to say the thing directly. She is not less sharp. She is more open. The two narrators should feel like different women who loved the same person.

---

**Opening approach — VERSION A (locked):** The book opens cold on Adura's voice, in action, without meta-framing. The retrospective quality of Act 1 is present but invisible — she's a person telling a story, not a person writing a document. The found-document frame (if used at all) arrives only in the coda, as a quiet revelation rather than an established premise. The reader doesn't know until the last pages that they've been reading a physical object. This makes the binding holes on the last slat land harder.

The coda is close-third, no narrator, attributed to no one — Option C. The implication that someone is relaying this is present but never stated.
