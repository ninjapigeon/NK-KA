# FRD — BOOK 2
## Scene 1 Draft (Scenes 1 & 2 combined)
*June 2026 · First draft*

---

The way I remember it, I was winning.

Not the way you'd want to be winning — two of them down, four still standing, my back against a tree I'd been slowly trying to put between me and the largest of them. But I'd lifted the axe from the first one before he'd finished falling, and the sword from the second, and the arithmetic was moving in a direction I could work with.

Then something stepped out of the forest and the arithmetic stopped mattering.

What I noticed first was that it was ordinary-sized — a man's height, unremarkably, and I remember thinking *oh, a man* for exactly the length of time it took me to look at the face. One eye, off-centre, glowing faintly from somewhere inside the wood, and the head perfectly still while the eye moved — rotating slowly in its socket to find me, the way a lamp turns in a bracket when someone adjusts it. Not the way anything alive moves. My mind tried to find a category for it and couldn't.

The four remaining Orks had stopped. I'd been so focused on the one closest to me that I hadn't noticed — but they'd all stopped, the way animals stop when they've registered something further up the chain.

Then it moved.

I have seen good fighters. I know what skill looks like, the particular economy of someone who has done this long enough that nothing is wasted. What came out of the trees was not that — or it was that, but underneath something else, the way a good song is also mathematics underneath if you care to look. The closest I've come to describing it, in the years since, is the ribbon-dancers at the spring markets: the long pole, the silk tied at the end, the wide arcs that keep it always moving, always exactly where it means to be, nothing tangling, nothing stopping. Except the ribbon here was a glaive, and what it moved through was four Orks who had hexwood swords.

Hexwood doesn't break. Ask anyone who's worked with it — it doesn't yield, it doesn't chip, it is the hardest thing most people on Anka will ever hold, harder than stone, soaked through with Titan blood and dried into something that has never had cause to apologise to anything it's met.

The glaive went through two of the hexwood swords in a single arc. Not broke — through. Like reeds.

The other two Orks lasted a little longer. Not much.

When it was over the clearing was quiet and the thing made of dark wood was standing in the middle of it with its single eye settling on me, and I was still against the tree with an axe in one hand and a sword in the other and nowhere I urgently needed to be.

We looked at each other.

*"I had it,"* I said.

It said nothing.

*"Two of them,"* I said. *"Before you arrived. I'd already got two."*

The eye moved — not the head, just the eye, finding me again. It examined me the way you'd examine an interesting rock.

I lowered the axe. Kept the sword.

*"Do you have a name?"* I said.

*"I have not been given one."*

The voice came from somewhere in the chest — stone on stone, felt in the floor before it reached the ears. Not loud. Just deep the way a well is deep, which you understand only after you've dropped something in and haven't heard it land yet.

I took stock the way I'd been taught to take stock: practically, without flinching, one detail at a time. Ordinary height. Old heartwood colour, smooth, no grain you could read. What might have been muscle if you weren't looking too carefully at how it moved — too evenly, nothing settling into place the way living tissue does. Legs that bent backward at the knee, ending in something cloven and very quiet on the forest floor for something that had just cut through hexwood. Arms with seams at the elbow that I couldn't quite name and filed away for later. A glaive, held at its side now, still, as though it were simply a walking staff and not the thing that had just ended the question.

It was already moving through the clearing, collecting something from near the bodies. Evidence for whatever bounty had brought it here.

*"Who sent you?"* I said.

*"The local authority placed a bounty on the Ork incursion. I am collecting the relevant proofs."*

*"And me?"*

It looked up. The eye found me again.

*"You were incidentally present. Are you the last that lived?"*

I thought about Klar. Klar who knew the forest better than anyone and was probably three villages over by now, telling a story in a warm room. Good. Let him tell it.

*"Yes,"* I said. *"I think so."*

*"You should go to the next town."*

It went back to what it was doing. As though that were the end of it — as though I would walk into the trees and it would collect its evidence and both of us would carry on in our respective directions and that would be all.

I found the warmest cloak in the bounty pile, a belt in good repair, secured the sword properly at my hip. Left the rest. I have never carried what I can't use.

*"I don't know where the next town is,"* I said. *"I have a difficulty with directions."*

*"East,"* it said, without looking up.

*"And alone?"*

A pause. Not consideration — I'd learn to read the difference, eventually — just processing. *"The forest is clear in that direction."*

*"You just cleared it."*

It straightened. Gathered its things and began walking in what I assumed was the direction of whoever had paid it.

I followed.

It stopped.

*"The town is east,"* it said.

*"Yes,"* I said. *"So I'll go that way too."*

The eye found me. Held there.

It reached into its bag and produced two coins — tarnished black, like every coin on Anka — and held them out to me.

*"One for each kill,"* it said. *"Your share of the bounty."*

I looked at the coins. I looked at the clearing. I thought about what came next, and what came after that, and how long money lasted when you were making the kinds of decisions I was going to have to make.

*"No,"* I said.

It continued to hold out the coins. *"One per kill. The standard rate."*

*"I know what the rate is."*

*"Then—"*

*"You saved my life,"* I said. *"Now you owe me to take care of it."*

The silence that followed was arithmetic — I could almost hear it working, some process behind the still face that wasn't designed for this particular problem. The coins stayed where they were for a moment. Then it put them back.

*"Interesting rationale,"* it said.

It turned and kept walking. I kept pace. Behind us the forest closed over the clearing, and neither of us looked back, and I had questions — dozens of them — but I've never been the kind of person who asks questions before I've decided I'm staying. I was staying. The questions could wait.
