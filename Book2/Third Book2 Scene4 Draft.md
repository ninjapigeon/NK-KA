# FRD — BOOK 2
## Scene 4 Draft — Learning the Terms
*July 2026 · First draft*

---

The stretch of road after the naming taught me things I hadn't expected to need teaching, and taught him things he was, I think, genuinely surprised to discover he didn't already know.

Sleep was the first one. The second night, I woke somewhere past middle-dark to find him exactly where he'd been when I'd closed my eyes — same angle, same stillness, the fire burned down to coal and him sitting beside it like something set there on purpose and forgotten. I asked if he'd moved at all. He said no. I asked if that was uncomfortable. He said the question didn't apply. I decided, that night, not to ask him about it again until I understood it better myself, and wrote instead — much later, once I had the means to write anything — that his stillness at night had a different quality to his stillness in the day. Softer, somehow. Less like waiting and more like the closest thing he had to rest, even if rest wasn't the right word either.

Rain was the second one, and it very nearly ended in a fight, or as close to a fight as the two of us ever came in the early days. It caught us on open ground, no shelter for a mile in either direction, and I spent the worst of it hunched under my cloak while he stood in it, upright, unbothered, watching me with the particular attention he gave to things he didn't understand.

*"You're distressed,"* he said, at one point, as though noticing for the first time.

*"I'm cold,"* I said. *"And wet. Both of those are distressing."*

*"The rain is not distressing to me."*

*"You're made of wood."*

*"Yes."*

*"I am not made of wood."*

He considered this, in the way he considered most things — a pause with nothing visible happening behind it, the eye held level, neither bright nor dim. Then he took off the cloak he wore over his shoulders, more habit than necessity as far as I could tell, and held it over me instead, at an angle that kept the worst of it off without quite managing to keep off all of it. It wasn't a good shelter. It was, I think, the first time he'd ever tried to build one, and it showed. But he held it there without complaint for the better part of an hour, arm outstretched, unmoving, while I stopped shivering enough to think straight again, and I decided that counted for something, even if the geometry of it needed work.

Food was simpler, in its way, because he didn't need any of it explained twice. *"I do not eat,"* he told me, the first time I offered him a share of something, and that was the whole of the conversation — no follow-up, no curiosity about why I did, just a fact stated and filed.

The town was the lesson I only needed once.

It was my idea, and I'll own that. Some ten or eleven days out, tired of foraged food and worse water, I said we should find a proper market — a real town, real supplies, a night indoors if they'd have us. He said that would not go well. I asked why not. He said, *"They will not let me approach,"* which I took, wrongly, for simple shyness on his part, or some old grudge with a specific place I hadn't heard about yet. I didn't understand, because he said it the way he said everything, without enough weight on it to tell me it was a warning rather than a preference.

We got within sight of the walls before the bell started.

I've heard that bell in memory more times than I can count, in the years since, and it never gets any smaller. One clean note, then three fast ones on top of it, and by the second toll there were people on the wall who hadn't been there a breath before — a horn answering the bell, gates groaning shut on their chains, and I remember, with a clarity nothing else from that whole first year has, a child being hauled backward off the road by the arm, fast enough that her feet left the ground.

He stopped walking. I hadn't told him to. He simply stopped, well outside anything that could be called close, and stood there while three archers took position on the gatehouse and a man in half-armor shouted something I couldn't make out over the bell, and nobody — not one person on that wall — looked at him the way you look at a stranger. They looked at him the way you look at a wolf that has wandered, somehow, impossibly, out of the stories and into the field behind your house.

*"We should go,"* he said, after a while, and we went, and neither of us spoke of it again for a long time.

I understood, walking away, that I had asked a genuinely dangerous thing of him without knowing it was dangerous, and that he had known, and had told me as plainly as his nature allowed, and that the fault for what nearly happened was entirely mine for not hearing a warning dressed as a fact. I have tried, in the years since, never to make that particular mistake again. From that day I did the going-into-town myself, alone, and he waited at whatever treeline or crossroads was furthest out that still counted as near, and neither of us ever suggested otherwise.

Which left the question of how he'd managed, all the centuries before me, whenever a settlement wasn't an option and foraging wasn't enough.

The answer, it turned out, was Ossam.

He kept a cart on the low road south of wherever we happened to be — I came to understand, over the years, that there were several such carts along several such roads, and several such men, and that Third had simply always known where they were, the way you know where water is in a country you've walked long enough. Ossam was old, and blind, milk-white in both eyes and entirely unbothered by it, and he ran his little cart of dried goods and oddments by ear and by touch and by a memory for voices that put mine to shame. He didn't startle when Third spoke. He didn't startle at anything, so far as I ever saw. He simply turned toward the sound of a voice, the way a sighted man turns toward a face, and did business.

It was the first time I watched him actually try to haggle, because it was the first time anyone had ever haggled with him in good faith rather than fear.

*"Dried meat,"* Third said. *"Enough for one, some days."*

*"Two Teeth the strip,"* Ossam said, already weighing.

*"That is more than it was worth in the last town."*

*"Then you should have bought it in the last town."*

Third paid two Teeth the strip. I watched the entire exchange with something between delight and despair, because here — finally, actually — was a fair fight, an honest number thrown out high the way every honest number is thrown out high, waiting to be pushed back down, and he simply didn't push. Ossam, for his part, seemed to find this endlessly, quietly funny, in the manner of an old man who has met every kind of customer there is and finally found the one who'll never learn to bargain and doesn't especially need to, given how rarely he spends anything at all.

*"You're being robbed,"* I told him afterward, once we were back on the road.

*"He asked for two."*

*"He asked for two because he hoped you'd offer one and he'd settle for one and a half. That's the whole game. That's what haggling is."*

*"Why would he ask for more than he expected, only to be offered less?"*

I had, by then, had this exact conversation enough times, in enough forms, to know I didn't have a better answer than *because that's how it's done* — which even I could hear wasn't much of an argument, and which he accepted, as he accepted most explanations that didn't change anything the next time coin changed hands. I gave up trying to fix it somewhere on that road south, and let him go on paying Ossam whatever Ossam asked, for years, the two of them running the same small honest theater every time we passed that way, neither of them in on quite the same joke, both of them, I think, glad of the company regardless.

The wilder country between towns had its own lessons, and its own reminders that Ossam's stretch of road was the exception, not the rule.

There was a swamp, a few days past one crossroads, thick with trees that looked something like mangroves — twisted roots standing half out of black water, a frond of leaves far overhead. Low among the roots of nearly every one sat a single yellow tuber, waxy and round and unmistakably eye-shaped, and it took the better part of an hour of walking before I admitted what I'd already noticed within the first few minutes: it wasn't staying still. It turned. Slowly, smoothly, the way a sunflower turns toward the sun, except what it was turning toward was me — and by the time we were properly into the grove I couldn't take three steps without feeling some new one pick me up, one after another, the watching passed hand to hand down the whole line of trees like something being whispered ahead of us.

*"They're not eyes,"* I said, mostly to hear a voice that wasn't the swamp.

*"No."*

*"Then why do they turn?"*

*"Warmth. Or movement. I don't know which. Only that it isn't sight, whatever it is."*

That helped less than he probably intended. I understood, walking through the worst of it, exactly why a person alone out here might finally stop and put a knife into one of them — not for any sensible reason, just to make the turning stop, just to have one moment where something wasn't tracking them across the dark. I understood it so well I had to stop myself reaching for my own knife more than once, and I wasn't even the one it seemed to be following.

Somewhere near the heart of the grove, a bird startled out of the upper branches, and one of the trees let go of something — a soft sound, more breath than crack — and the air around its roots went dark, ink-black, spreading and thickening before it thinned back to swamp-light a few long seconds later.

*"The sap does that,"* he said, before I'd found the words to ask. *"Turns to gas on contact with the air. If more than one tree startles at once, the whole grove can go with it. Things that hunt here use the dark for cover."*

I have never, before or since, wanted someone to lie to me quite so specifically as I wanted him to lie to me in that moment. He didn't, of course. We walked faster after that, and I didn't look at the roots again if I could help it.

The shard groves, further on, were almost a relief by comparison, if only because their danger announced itself honestly. Tall pale trunks, bare most of their height, with the leaves bundled far overhead like a second sky — amber, glasslike, catching the light so beautifully that I laughed, the first afternoon we walked beneath one, at how unfair it was that something so lovely could exist in the same country as everything else we'd seen. The wind picked up an hour later, and the laughing stopped, because the same glass that made the canopy so lovely from below was, coming down fast in a stiff wind, more or less a knife. He got me clear of it without seeming to hurry, and I noticed — because I noticed everything about him by then, the way you notice a person's handwriting once you've read enough of it — that the eye dimmed, very slightly, in the worst of it. Not fear. I don't think it was fear. But something in him registered the falling glass as a cost, the way it registered most dangerous things, a small tax paid out of whatever he had to pay it from.

I saw the opposite only a few days later — a hawk, doing something with a thermal I didn't have the eye to follow, folding and dropping and opening again all in one motion — and the glow brightened, just slightly, just for the length of the movement, the way you'd lean forward at something unexpectedly well done. I didn't know yet what either of those meant. I only knew they were different, that the difference seemed to matter, and that I was, without quite deciding to, keeping a private ledger of which was which.

I didn't know, in that whole first stretch, that I was doing anything beyond getting used to him. I wasn't planning ahead. But I was already doing, without noticing I'd started, the thing I'd end up doing for the rest of the years I had: taking what he gave me — flat, small, unexplained — and turning it over until I understood what it had actually cost him, or someone else, to arrive at.

He never asked me to do that. I don't think it occurred to him that it was being done.
