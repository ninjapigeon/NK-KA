


## The Emperor’s Causeway

From a distance, this is just a very long bridge. Up close, it’s almost absurdly oversized: a roadway wide enough for fifteen Humins to walk shoulder to shoulder, carried on arches that average six Humins high. Every thousand paces or so, an arch above the roadway opens onto a corkscrew ramp leading down to the ground — these are the only way on or off, since the bridge itself has no other access points along its length. The roadway-side gates over these ramps still have working clockwork chain mechanisms; the ground-side ramps have no gates at all.

It’s commonly said the Causeway was built during the Titan war, to move large bodies of titan-spawn troops quickly between fronts. The trouble is that it doesn’t connect to anywhere any historian can place as a front, and titan-spawn rarely moved in groups organized enough for a bridge like this to matter militarily. Most people who’ve thought about it longer than a few minutes settle on the same conclusion: it’s a Titan creation, built the way the Titan built everything — because it could, not because it needed to.

The bridge's name is older than any of these theories, and outlasts them. "Emperor" refers to Acnash, first of the Giants to steal magic from the Titans and, in an age before Virek, Kro, Ork, or Humins existed, Emperor over his own kind. Whether the Titan raised the Causeway at Acnash's request, in his memory, or without any thought of him at all — the way it built everything — is a question nobody living can answer. What's left is only the name, worn onto a road that predates every people currently walking across it.

## The Giant’s Graveyard

Also called the crawling cliff, or the crawling rocks — names that undersell just how unsettling the place is once you’re standing in front of it.

The Graveyard is a single sheer, pale cliff — locals call it the spine — roughly five kilometers wide and a hundred meters high, with broad, flat slabs of the same pale stone peeling away from its face at every angle. The image that sticks with most people, once they’ve seen it, is an enormous hardcover book lying open on its back: the cliff is the spine, and the slabs are its pages, caught mid-turn. Near the face, the pages crowd close together and stand nearly upright; further out, they lean more; further still, they lie flat on the ground, half-buried in ferns and moss. Each slab is enormous — causeway-scale — yet from a distance the whole cliff resolves gradually, the way a true horizon does, rather than announcing itself as a wall. About a kilometer out in any direction, the greenery gives way to bare rocky desert, and the desert is where most travelers first realize just how big the thing in front of them actually is.

The pages move. Not while anyone’s watching — campers who’ve tried to keep a slab in view report it happening the moment they look away, or fall asleep, or simply blink too long. Sometimes there’s a crack like thunder, and a new page has appeared near the spine; sometimes a smaller pop, followed by a crash, as a page far out from the spine finally falls flat. The cliff never seems to get any smaller, the field of fallen pages never seems to get any larger, and the ring of desert never expands — whatever’s happening here balances itself.

People have tried to live here anyway. Small folk occasionally build into or onto a standing page; goblins occasionally nest on one. Both eventually leave, one way or another — usually because the page they were living on chooses, without warning, to become a fallen one.

The cliff is the exposed root of something far larger — the same mountain-root that, ground down across an age, became the Anvil the Forged were made on. What’s left of it now sits half in Anka and half in the grey, which is the real source of everything strange here: the slabs “crawling” when unobserved, the thunderclap arrivals and departures, even the way the cliff resolves like a horizon instead of a wall. A Buhar carried here goes strange and unreliable. And anyone who can step sideways finds the crossing far rougher than it should be — the grey pulls here in a way it doesn’t anywhere else.

## The Glass Sea

A roughly circular expanse of smoky, glasslike black material — easily mistaken for obsidian — surrounding the Spire for about five kilometers (a quarter-day’s travel) in every direction. The city of Dunarth sits at one edge of it, the last real stop for anyone foolhardy enough to be heading toward the Spire itself.

Despite its name, the Glass Sea is bone dry, and aggressively so — anyone spending real time inside it needs to watch their water supply carefully, both while moving and while camped, since the place does nothing to help and a great deal to hurt. At night, without real protection, the cold here is enough to leave a person dangerously hypothermic. The “glass” itself seems to repair small damage to its surface on its own, given time.

Skybarges cannot cross it — something about the place is flatly hostile to whatever keeps a barge in the air — and any magefire-powered device carried across visibly loses power the deeper in it goes, Buhars included. The same goes for anything relying on a magefire battery to keep functioning at all: this much raw, ungrounded Ptah in one place seems to overwhelm batteries entirely rather than merely draining them, and whatever they’re powering simply stops. For a Forged — whose chest batteries are the only thing keeping them going — crossing the Glass Sea isn’t a risk. It’s a death sentence, and reason enough for even the oldest and most curious of them to give the place a permanent, respectful distance.

## The Gnawing Fields

At a glance, these are simply fields — yellow grass, sometimes shading to red, heather growing wild. What sets them apart is what’s scattered through the grass: squared-off white plinths, none taller than a person, some no higher than a knee, in no pattern anyone’s identified, for height or placement either. Touch one and it’s warm, even on a cold day.

These are the glutton’s teeth — pulled, or knocked loose, sometime during the Titan war, and left exactly where they landed ever since. Don’t call them gravestones. People who do tend to assume some connection to the Giant’s Graveyard, and there isn’t one; the two places share a color palette and nothing else.

Fist-sized chunks of debris drift among the plinths in loose, slow clusters of about a dozen — floating, with no visible support, the way nothing else on Anka does. This is floatstone, and it’s a genuine local curiosity: a piece will pull away from any nearby vein of black glass as if repelled by it, which is what keeps it aloft. A whole town at the rim of the Fields lives on harvesting and selling it as novelties and charms — but it’s a curiosity, not an industry. Skybarges fly on fans and arcane batteries, same as everything else; floatstone has never scaled into anything bigger than a trinket.

> [!tag]- Secret

The real danger here isn’t the plinths — it’s what moves between them. Grinding Teeth (see Fauna) are drawn to motion, and the Gnawing Fields are full of them, mostly indistinguishable from the floating debris until they’re already converging. The only reliable defense is the one nobody wants to rely on: stay still, and they lose interest.

## The Scar

The defining feature of Anka’s sky, and the reason the world has anything like a “day” at all. As the story goes, when the titan of storms was sundered, it tore a long rift across the sky on its way down — and that rift has been rotating overhead, slow and steady, ever since.

As the Scar turns, it carries its rift across the sky, plunging whatever lies beneath into darkness and then carrying it back into light again, with a regularity nothing else on Anka quite matches. The edges of the rift aren’t sharp — they’re said to be “fuzzy” — which is why the change from bright to dark (or back again) takes a few minutes rather than happening all at once; travelers get roughly five minutes’ warning before it actually arrives. Locals don’t bother with separate words for “day” and “night” the way other worlds do. There’s just “day,” with the Scar somewhere in it, dividing the bright part from the dark.

The Scar’s rotation is also the reason Anka has no moons and no stars: whatever’s up there, the Scar is the only thing in it.

## The Snake Forest

Not a remarkable forest at first glance — the trees aren’t especially tall, and the wood isn’t especially dense. What’s remarkable is that every single tree in it has exactly the same curve to its trunk: a long, slow bend, as if each one had started growing sideways and slowly corrected itself to vertical over a very long time. Some say they look like snakes, frozen mid-uncoil and turned to wood. Nobody’s found a reason why every tree would bend the same way, in the same direction, with the same curve.

Nothing in the Snake Forest attacks. Nothing has ever been seen to move. But people who spend any real time under its canopy report the same thing, in roughly the same words: a low, building unease that doesn’t go away and doesn’t seem to come from anything in particular — just from looking, day after day, at hundreds of identical trees that shouldn’t be identical. It wears at people. Travelers who can avoid a long stay in the Snake Forest generally do, not because anything will hurt them, but because something about it doesn’t sit right, and a few days of not sitting right is enough for most.

## The Spire

A 300-meter spire of smoky black material, visible — on a clear day — as far as the rim ward mountains. From a distance it reads as little more than a dark line on the horizon, like an impossibly thin, impossibly tall stalagmite; up close, it resolves into something closer to a single enormous shard of dark glass, with a section near the tip, roughly the size of a person, that catches and throws back the sky’s light for kilometers around. People who’ve gotten close enough to really look at it describe it less as glass than as a diamond — a single, continuous, spear-headed crystal driven point-first into the world.

The Glass Sea surrounds its base for about five kilometers in every direction, and the resemblance isn’t a coincidence — both are Ptah, the same material that makes up the cracks running across Anka and the dead geyser-fields. But where the Glass Sea is the soft kind — old, but still being added to, and still soft enough to work or break apart — the Spire is something else entirely: the oldest and densest Ptah anywhere, made rather than dried, gone fully adamant. Nothing has ever so much as scratched it, magic or otherwise, and nothing taken from it has ever proven usable for anything. Whatever the Spire actually is, it was already exactly like this — unmoving, unbreakable — before anything else on Anka had a history worth recording.

Functionally, the Spire does nothing. It isn’t a weapon, a prison, a battery, or a door. What it is, mostly, is a direction: visible from nearly everywhere on Anka, it gives travelers a fixed point to call spireward, and an entire world its only real landmark.

## Titan Footprints -- texture note (locked): 
Not uniform. Some stretches read as dragged and swept (furrows, knuckled stone, smooth walls); others — as established in Book 1, ch10 — read as violently broken: shattered, fractured, “a mouth full of broken teeth.” Whatever moved through left different marks in different places, possibly depending on what it was dragging, how it was moving, or how long ago the ground gave way.
