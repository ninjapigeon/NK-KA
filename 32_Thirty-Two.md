# Thirty Two

We knew it was the day because the field stopped pretending.

For a tenday the open book had played its patient game — moved only when we looked away, settled its great canting pages a little nearer or a little further each time our backs were turned, so that you could almost believe you'd misremembered, almost believe stone behaved. That morning it stopped bothering. We woke to a field rearranged in plain sight, not while we slept but while we watched: a slab the size of a barn sliding its base across bare rock with a sound like a slow tooth, grinding itself a hand's breadth spireward and stopping, as if it had remembered it no longer needed to be shy. The grey was running so hard toward the cliff by then that even I could feel it, and I have no gift for the grey at all — a tug, low in the chest, the exact place you'd press if you were trying not to be sick, except the pull was outward, toward the pale wall, as though the morning itself were draining into it.

Third stood at the rim with his back to our dead fire and looked at the cliff the way you look at a debt you have always known you would have to pay.

"He is on the field," he said. "He came in the dark, while we kept the watch facing the wrong way again." A dry sound, not quite the bellows. "We are very bad at facing the right way. It is, I think, the most humin thing about all of us." The eye came round. "It is today. Do you still mean to come, or have you the sense the night might have given you?"

"Don't," I said. "You know I'm coming. Roan?"

"Coming," Roan said, and checked the lashings on his spear for the fourth time that hour, which was how Roan said *I am terrified and I am coming anyway,* and which I had learned not to comment on.

"Then we go to it," Third said, "while we can still choose the ground. He will make for the spine. The spine is the last of it — the last pin, the place the whole shape is hung from. If he reaches it unopposed he has everything he needs and we have a long walk home with nothing to show for it but our lives, which he will not let us keep regardless." He took up his Falkas, and the Boltcaster, and he did not put on the cloak. He had no more use for looking like a man.

I have crossed bad country. I grew up a walk from a black swamp; I had been over the Gnawing Fields with their floating teeth and their pale plinths; I had stood a tenday at the rim of this place watching it crawl. None of it taught me what it was to walk *into* the open book, out among the pages, with the rule of the place — *do not look away* — turned from a curiosity into the only law that mattered.

Because you cannot watch everything. That is the whole cruelty of it. You have a front and the field has no manners about which direction it uses. We crossed in a tight knot, the three of us, each facing a third of the world, turning our heads in a slow shared sweep we fell into without discussing, like oarsmen finding a stroke — and still, every time the sweep came round, something was *different.* A slab that had leaned left now leaned right. A gap we'd meant to walk through had closed to a seam. A flat page we'd marked as our path had tilted in the breath our eyes were elsewhere and become a wall, and we had to find the path again from nothing, in a place that rearranged its own evidence the instant we trusted it.

Roan said, once, very low, "It's like being watched by something that stops the moment you turn." And none of us answered, because he was exactly right and there was nothing to add to it except more fear.

The pages stood taller as we went in. At the rim they had lain in the moss like fallen lanes; here they rose hillside-high, pale and thin and canted at angles that hurt to stand under, each one a leaf of a great book laid open and reading itself in the dark. And between them, in the low places, the grey had come up out of the ground. I had never seen it from the lived side — Third's country, not mine — but there it was, pooled in the troughs between the great stones like ground-fog that had forgotten to be white, a faint moving greyness with the wrongest light in it, all of it sliding, all of it pouring, the same direction we were walking. Toward the wall.

The wall took its toll on Third by lengths.

I'd promised to watch him and I watched him, and what I watched was a slow leaning. Not of his body — his body crossed that field like it crossed everything, sure-hooved and exact — but of *him,* the him inside the make of him, tilting toward the cliff the way the slabs tilted, the way the grey poured. By the time the pages were hillside-sized he was walking at a slight cant against nothing, leaning upcurrent into a wind only he could feel, and the seam down his chest showed both lights working hard, the dim one guttering with the effort, and his answers had gone short.

"Third."

"I am here."

"Say the thing. The whole thing. Out loud, for me."

He knew what I was asking. We had made it a drill on the slab two nights back — that if the pull got into him I was to make him *talk,* because the part of him that talked to me was the part that was still walking to the cliff to stop a thing, and not the part that wanted to lie down in the warm and be known.

"I am Third," he said, and the avalanche of it steadied as he said it, the way a man's hands steady on a rope he has remembered he is holding. "I am going to the spine to refuse him. I will not be made willing. You will not believe the version of me that begs. There is a girl named Tasha walking on my left who is going to be the whole of my will if my own gives out, and a guardsman on my right who has chosen the people over the sense, and they are mine, and they are *here,* and that is more true than anything that wall is going to offer me." A pause. The cant of him eased, a degree. "Thank you. Ask me again when I go quiet."

"I will. I'll ask you the whole way."

"I know you will. It is why I let you come."

We saw him when the field gave us a long straight aisle between two leaning pages — a corridor of bare rock running spireward, and at the far end of it, small with distance, a figure walking.

He walked the way the grey-faced traveller had described back at the inn, and worse for the seeing of it: dead straight, unhurried, putting one foot exactly in front of the other along a line only he could see, as though the field's crawling did not apply to him, as though the slabs declined to rearrange in front of *that.* And riding his back, even at that distance, even in the day, six small cold lights — bottled lightning gone to dying embers in their jars — six of them where Third had two, and one of Third's already failing. He did not look at us. He had not, I understood, looked at anything in a very long time that was not the thing he was walking toward. He went building to building through a dead town and never turned his head; he would cross a field that ate the unwary and never break his line; and he was going to reach the spine at the same time we did, from the other arm of the open book, the two of us converging on the hinge of the world like the covers of the book closing.

"There," Third said, unnecessarily. The eye had gone very bright and very steady. "The hand of the thing under everything."

And then he went still — stiller than the moment asked for, a stillness with a different grain to it, the same stillness that had come over him in the inn the day the grey-faced traveller spoke, as though the shape of that far walker had reached across all the bare rock between us and pressed on something in him he had no intention of letting me see. Whatever it was, he didn't say it. His head stayed level on the distant figure, and his answer, when I asked with a look, was only the bright steady eye and nothing behind it I was allowed to read.

I filed it. Of course I filed it; it is the curse Mathen used to tease me for, and I had no Mathen now to tease me, so I filed it alone and said nothing, and we walked the last of the aisle toward the place the whole shape was hung from, where the grey stood up out of the ground in a pale drowning wall against the pale dead wall of stone, and where a thing was waiting that had a place in it shaped exactly like Third, and a girl from an inn was going to have to be a rope.

The spine rose in front of us, and resolved, the way the cliff always resolved on approach — gradually, grudgingly, a little more true with every step until you could not unsee how big it was. And at its foot, in the last of the bare rock before the stone and the grey, there was room enough to stand and say the things that had to be said before the other thing arrived to say his.

"Here," Third said. "We say what we must here. Quickly. He is close, and once he is closer there will be no more saying, only doing."

I looked at Roan, who did not know yet all of what he had walked into.

It was, I realised, my turn to hold a door open for someone — and this time, the cruel kind, the kind that only opens inward.
